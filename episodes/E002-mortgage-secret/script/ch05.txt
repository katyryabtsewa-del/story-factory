# One Thing to Sign

When my son stood on my porch on that Monday morning, holding a leather folder and smiling too hard, I did what any mother would do, which was to put the coffee on.

"Come in, Trevor," I said. "Sit. Have you eaten?"

"I'm fine, Mom. I can't stay long. The thing is, we're lowering the rate."

He sat at my kitchen table, in the same chair as always, and set out the papers in a neat row, the way a salesman lays out samples. He is a regional sales manager for a flooring company, and I have watched him talk a contractor into three more pallets of laminate in that same patient, friendly voice. It is a very good voice. It had just never been used on me until that morning.

"Cass found a broker online," Trevor said. "He says that with how much the house has gone up, we can refinance, get a better rate, and pull some equity out. It saves everybody money, Mom. All it needs is your signature on one thing for the title company. It's a formality."

"Which thing?" I asked.

"It's just this page, here. It's more of a technicality, so the bank can work with us directly."

I pulled my reading glasses down from my gray curls. "I'll just read it all, dear, while you drink your coffee."

He did not like that. He started in on the interest rate again, and the monthly savings, while I read every page from the top, one at a time, with my finger on each line. I have read school district budgets that were shorter than that folder. I was not in a hurry.

Here is what I found. It was a cash-out refinance, for a new loan of two hundred sixty thousand dollars. The new loan was in the names of Trevor and Cassidy Dunmore. Marlene Dunmore appeared nowhere on it, except as a signature at the bottom of one particular page. That page was a quitclaim deed. It said that I, Marlene Dunmore, the grantor, did hereby release all my right, title, and interest in forty-one Tamarack Court to Trevor Dunmore and Cassidy Dunmore. Below it was a line labeled "consideration," which is the place where a deed says what the buyer is giving in exchange. The line had been left blank.

I looked at that empty line for a long time.

"Trevor," I said, "this says I give the house to you."

"It's just for the title company," he said quickly. "It's a technical step. Then the loan goes through, and then, you know, we put the deed back."

"Who puts it back?"

"We would. Obviously. Mom, come on."

"And what is the money for, the two hundred sixty thousand?"

Trevor rubbed the back of his neck. "Some home improvements. And Cass's studio. She's been wanting a real home studio for her business, with lighting and a backdrop wall and all that. It would be a big step. And, you know. Consolidating some things."

"What things?"

"Her cards. A few cards. It's not a lot." He laughed, but it did not go anywhere. "Mom, it's all in the family. It's all going to the same place."

I did not say that it was going to a place with a different last name. I closed the folder. I said I would like to take the weekend to look at it properly, and I would like him to leave the packet with me.

"Mom, I really need it by Friday," Trevor said. "Cass already put down a deposit on the studio. It's nonrefundable. And the rate lock ends after the Fourth."

"I see," I said. "Then I'll let you know by Friday morning."

"Great," he said, and his whole body went loose with relief. "You're the best, Mom." He drank his coffee and stood up and kissed the top of my head, which he had not done since the funeral.

When he had gone, I took the packet back to the table and read it a second time, more slowly, and that was when I saw the sticky note.

It was a small pink square, stuck to the back of the broker's cover sheet, and somebody had meant it to be peeled off long before I got there. Perhaps Trevor had missed it. The handwriting was one I would know in any light, a loopy, confident hand with a little circle over every letter i, which I had seen on six years of Christmas cards and thank you notes for my lemon bars.

It said, "Get M to sign before the Fourth. Don't let her read it, she'll make it a thing."

I sat quite still. The clock I have owned since my wedding ticked on the wall, and a chickadee landed on the feeder outside the window and went to work on the seed.

I was not shocked. I would like to say I was, because it would make me a better story, but a bookkeeper is rarely shocked when the numbers finally come out. What I felt was a great calm, like when you have listened for weeks to a leak somewhere in the walls, and at last you know which pipe it is.

I lifted the note off the cover sheet and carried it to my study. I opened the third green ledger to the last page, pressed that little pink square flat beside the total, and wrote the date underneath, in pen.

I made my decision right there, and I want to tell you what it was, so that you do not think better of me than I deserve. I was not going to take revenge. Revenge is a feeling, and feelings are expensive and never balance. What I was going to do was arithmetic. For six years I had kept the books on this family, and somebody had finally asked me to sign off on a lie, and I would not be signing. I would be reconciling, which is not cruel. It is only what you do when the numbers on one side do not match the numbers on the other.

I did not call Odessa, because she would have driven straight to Tamarack Court. I did not call Trevor. I picked up my kitchen telephone, a beige one with a cord that Gerald had stretched all the way to the stove, and I dialed a number I had not used since the week we settled his estate.

It was a woman's voice on the other end, young and quick. She said, "Feld Law, above the bakery. How can I help?"

I said my name and asked if Naomi Feld had any time that week, and that it was a matter involving a deed and a cash-out refinance. The young woman put me on hold for about the length of a verse of "Blessed Assurance." When she came back, her voice had changed, the way a voice does when somebody important has told you something.

"Missus Dunmore," she said, "Miss Feld can see you tomorrow at eight. She said to tell you to come early, and to bring the green books."
