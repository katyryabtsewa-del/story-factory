# Recess

Pearl had just told me I looked as if I were about to drive a bus, and I sat down beside her on the hallway bench with ten minutes of recess left.

The hall outside Courtroom Two is long and narrow, with a worn green runner on the floor and four benches, and I counted twelve people murmuring along it in the low voices of people who know they are in a building made of echoes. Pearl handed me a peppermint without being asked. She has been handing them out in emergencies for as long as I have known her, to babies, to preachers, once to a state trooper.

"You heard something," she said, in what she imagines is a whisper. Two of the farmers looked over.

"I did," I said.

"Is it good?"

"It is true," I said. "That is better. I will tell you when it is over, Pearl, and I would be grateful if you did not look at anybody in particular for the next hour."

"I will look at the floor," she said, "like a Methodist."

Danny came down the hall with a paper cup of coffee in each hand and gave one to me before I could say no. It came out of a machine and tasted like a warm penny, and I drank it gratefully. He stood in front of the bench with his hat tucked under his arm, shifting from one big boot to the other.

"Miss Vera, I am sorry about the questions in there," he said. "I answered them as straight as I could."

"You answered them the way you should have, Danny," I said. "That is all anybody can ask of a deputy, and a good deal more than some of them manage."

"The stakes bothered me, ma'am. I wrote them down, and then the next call came in, and the next one. And if a return came back on that plate, nobody put it in front of me. I should have asked."

"You did what the paper let you do," I said. "I counted them myself."

He smiled, a small bashful smile, and went back to his post at the courtroom door, and I finished the coffee and held the paper cup in both hands. I thought about Lloyd. I try not to bring him into rooms where he cannot answer for himself, but that morning I let him sit beside me for a minute.

It was the last autumn of his life. He was on the porch in the evening with a Comice pear in one hand and his pocketknife in the other, cutting it into quarters and handing me the first quarter, the way he always did, and he was looking out at the rows going blue in the dusk. That evening he said, "You know, Vee, you don't own an orchard. You borrow it from the next fellow." I asked him what he meant, and he said he meant that his grandfather had borrowed it from somebody, and his father from him, and that the only thing a person could do was hand it on with the trees in better shape than he found them. Then he ate his quarter of the pear and said it was a little early. It was.

On that bench I understood that I was not afraid for myself. I was afraid of handing it on in worse shape than I found it.

My young lawyer came out of the courtroom then and sat down on the other side of me, and he took a long time getting his legs arranged. He had a manila folder under his arm, and he looked tired, and I realized that he had been working on my case for a good while before I noticed.

"Missus Halvorsen," he said, "I have done a few things. I did not want to worry you."

"What things?" I asked.

"On Friday I put in a request for the sheriff's dispatch records for the six calls. I asked for the log itself, with the times." He lowered his voice. "The woman in the green sweater down the hall is the records clerk. She brought it herself, in a folder, because I told her I could not promise the court would want it. I also asked the postmaster for a written statement about the change of address. It is in my folder."

I looked down the hall. A woman in a green sweater sat on the far bench with her purse on her lap and a manila folder balanced on top of it, holding it the way you hold a pie on a bumpy road. She was eating crackers out of a plastic bag, with the calm face of someone who has read the log many times and finds nothing in it to worry about.

I thought, he has done all this, and he did not say a word, and he did not ask for credit. That is a rare quality in a young person, and in some old ones.

"Thank you," I said. "I have one thing to ask of you."

"Anything," he said.

"When the judge asks me something, do not stop me. Do not stand up, do not object, and do not look worried. Let me answer all the way to the end."

"Do you think she will ask you something?" he said.

"I watched her while Danny testified," I said. "She put a finger on one line of the visitor's report, at the word stakes, and she left it there until he stepped down. A judge does not keep her finger on a line unless she means to come back to it."

He looked at me for a moment and then nodded slowly. "Yes, ma'am," he said. "I was not going to."

The bailiff opened the door and said that court would resume in two minutes, and the hallway began to stir. Pearl stood up, and I stood too, and I counted the steps back to the door, which were twenty-two from that bench, and I went in with my back straight.

Brent was already at his table. Tucker Rand had taken his seat in the last row. The judge came in at five minutes to eleven by my watch, we rose, we sat, and Brent's lawyer stood and said that the petitioner rested. He sounded satisfied. Judge Marsh nodded. "Before the respondent's case, a note for the record," she said. "The court visitor's report, which I have read twice, observes forty-one orange survey stakes along the north boundary of this farm, and fresh tire ruts in the dry grass. Mister Halvorsen works in land brokerage. And I see that Mister Rand, of Copperleaf Homes, is in my gallery." She did not look at either man. My young lawyer began to rise, and I knew he was about to say that he wished to call me to the stand.

The judge lifted one hand, and he stopped halfway up. She took off her reading glasses and let them fall on their cord, and she looked at me directly for the first time that morning, a short, dry, tired look, the look of a woman who has been lied to by experts.

"Missus Halvorsen," she said. "I have one question."
