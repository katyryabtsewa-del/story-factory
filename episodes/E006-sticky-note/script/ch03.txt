# Marisol's Receipt

I let the girl from the copier in out of the cold, and for a full minute neither of us said anything.

I hung her parka on the hook, put the kettle on, and said, "Let's see what you've brought." Inside the bag was a sage-green bridesmaid dress with a hem that had been let down once too often. Her name was Marisol Vega, she told me, and the dress belonged to her sister, who was in a wedding in March. Nobody can tell a story straight to your face when she is frightened. But she can tell it to the top of your head, if you are kneeling on the floor with a mouthful of pins.

So I stood her on the little stool in the sage-green dress, and I knelt and began to mark the hem.

"I wrote the note," Marisol said, to the ceiling. "I couldn't think how else to reach you. Mister Brandt was watching everybody."

"It was a good note," I said around the pins. "Short. I have been handed worse instructions by brides."

She laughed, and it broke in the middle, and then she told me all of it.

On Thursday, the twentieth of November, Curtis Brandt had come into the office and sat down at her desk. He never raised his voice, she said, and that was part of what turned her stomach. He dictated a durable power of attorney to her, line by line, from a template Mister Teague kept on the computer. It named Josephine A. Ledbetter as the principal and Curtis as her agent, with power over every account and every piece of property she owned. Marisol typed it, because that is her job. When she asked where Missus Ledbetter was, Curtis said that you had signed already, and that the family was handling it with great delicacy. "But I typed it on Thursday," Marisol said to me now. "How could you have signed it on Tuesday?"

"I looked in the appointment book," Marisol said. "There was nothing for you on Tuesday the eighteenth, and the paper said you signed in front of Mister Teague that day at two-fifteen in the afternoon. I was at the front desk all that afternoon, Missus Ledbetter. I ate a yogurt at my desk and never left it. Nobody named Ledbetter came through that door."

"Go on," I said, and I moved the pins to the next fold.

Mister Teague stamped the paper on Friday. Two days later, on a Sunday when the office was closed, she went in for a scarf and found him at his desk with his notary journal open, writing an entry dated the eighteenth. In the column where he was meant to note how he knew the signer, he had written two words, personally known.

"That night I couldn't stop thinking about the signature," she said. "It looked practiced. And then I remembered something."

She stepped down from the stool in her stocking feet, and from the inside pocket of her parka she took a plastic folder with a picture of a bride on the front. It was her wedding folder, and only then did I know her. A bride looks different when she is not on a stool. She took out a yellow carbon receipt, two years old, from the first week of June. It said Ledbetter Alterations at the top, from the last of my old receipt books, and under it, in my own hand, bridal gown, take in bodice, shorten hem, rush, eighty-five dollars, paid. At the bottom was my signature, and under the last r, small as an eyelash, my tick.

"You made that little flick at the end," Marisol said, "and I asked what it was, and you said it was a knot, so the thread wouldn't pull out. I thought it was the sweetest thing. And I always make a copy of everything, it's how I stay out of trouble, so I had a photocopy of the power of attorney from before Mister Teague filed it."

She laid it next to the receipt. The signature on the power of attorney was very nearly right. The loop of the J was right, the slant was right, even the careful little A. But it ended at the r, flat as a shut door. There was no knot.

"That is a good forgery," I said at last. "Someone worked from a copy of a copy, a pension form, I'd guess, or the mail card. A knot that small does not survive a photocopier."

Marisol began to cry then, quietly, with her hand over her mouth. Her husband drove a delivery truck, she said, and they were saving for a house with a porch, and she paid her mother-in-law's rent besides. If Mister Teague fired her, there would be nothing in the account by spring. For nearly two months she had told herself it was none of her business, and every day it had grown heavier. Then on Tuesday she had watched me squinting at a page, with Mister Brandt patting my hand, and she had stuck the note to the clipboard with her fingers shaking so badly that she dropped the stapler.

I got up off the floor, with some help from the stool, and took her cold hands in mine. "Nobody does anything today," I said. "Not you, not me. You go back to the office this morning, and you answer the telephone, and you stay as ordinary as a Tuesday. Can you do that?"

"I think so."

"Then you've already done the hard part." I did not tell her yet where I had been at two-fifteen on the eighteenth of November. I kept it under my tongue like a pin. "One more thing. What does Curtis want my name on?"

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "There's a loan, Missus Ledbetter. A home equity loan through Harlan Ridge Lending, for one hundred eighty-five thousand dollars, against the house on Hollis Street. He used the power of attorney to apply. But the lender wants the borrower at the closing, in person, to sign for herself. That's why he said Medicare. The closing is Friday at ten o'clock in our conference room, and page four was so you'd say, in writing, that you had signed the first paper."

My house was paid for. Earl and I had made the last payment on a Saturday in April, and we had gone out for pie. I stood there with the pincushion on my wrist, smoothing the green skirt with my palm, and asked her to say the amount again, and she did.

She put on her parka and went to the door. With her hand on the knob she turned and said, as though she had been saving it for last, "He told Mister Teague that his wife would sign the witness line, and on the second of December she did. I saw the page come back with her name on it, and I took a picture of it, because I take a picture of everything. Missus Brandt is your daughter."
