# The Man from Ames

I got the red toolbox back on Wednesday morning, though not before I nearly lost it on my own front lawn.

I was in the machine shed by six thirty, in dry clothes and my canvas jacket, with Walt's small brass key in my pocket. Greg was asleep in his old bedroom with his shoes off. But the toolbox was not under the bench where it had sat for fifty years. There was a clean rectangle in the sawdust, and the door of the shed stood open.

I found it outside on the grass by the driveway, under a blue tarp, with a yellow sticky note on the lid that said Ames, nine o'clock. Tessa's helpers had left it for the buyer like a milk delivery. It was red steel gone brown at the corners, with a brass padlock the size of a plum.

I did what any sensible woman would have done, and sat down on it.

At twenty to nine a pickup with a utility trailer turned into my gravel and stopped. The man who got out was about fifty, thick through the shoulders, in a seed cap and a canvas coat that had seen a few winters. He took in a seventy-year-old woman sitting on a toolbox in the damp with a sticky note stuck to her knee, and he took off his cap.

"Morning, ma'am," he said. "I believe I'm early."

"You're right on time," I said. "But I'm afraid the box isn't for sale."

He looked at the note on my knee, and then at the house. "The lady said everything out here was an estate cleanup," he said. "She said the owner had passed and the family wanted it gone."

"He did pass," I said. "I'm the family."

He turned his cap around in his hands. "Ma'am, I'm sorry. I didn't know. I put forty dollars down to hold it, but I'd just as soon not carry off a thing that makes a woman sit on it in the rain."

"I'll give you the forty back," I said, and I reached into my jacket and counted it out in twenties.

He tucked it away, and then he looked past me into the open shed. "That's a nice old band saw," he said, carefully.

Walt owned two band saws, and he had cursed the older one since nineteen ninety-one. I sold it to the man from Ames for sixty dollars, which was more than Tessa's tag, and he loaded it onto the trailer himself and would not let me lift a thing. At the end he shook my hand and said whoever built that pegboard knew what he was doing. I said the man who built it had hummed the whole time, and he said he could tell.

When the trailer was gone I took the tarp off the box and unlocked the padlock. The brass key turned as if it had been waiting.

The lid came up on a smell of oil and old wood. Under a tray of ordinary things, a square and two pencils and a tape measure, lay three objects wrapped in a clean dish towel. A green spiral notebook. A small bank passbook with a blue cover. And a folded index card with Win written on the front.

The card said, in pencil, Don't give it away on a whim. Walk the route first.

Then I opened the passbook. It was from Fenmore Savings and Loan, in the name of Walter Brandvold, and across the top of the first page Walt had written Route Fund. The deposits ran down the column in small amounts, fifteen dollars, forty, one hundred and twenty, going back almost nineteen years. Every one was a lawn mower repaired, or a chair rebuilt, or a cabinet for somebody's daughter. The last line gave a balance. It was eighteen thousand four hundred dollars.

I sat on the grass for a while. In the notebook was the rest of it. Walt kept records like a shop teacher. Date, name, mile marker, what he had done, and what it cost. The very first line, and the longest, read Hilda Voss, mile eleven, roof, two thousand nine. Under it, in the margin, he had drawn a small dot. After that there were eighteen more. A furnace for Dewey Marsh. A well pump for the Lund twins. A wheelchair ramp for Ila Fenn's father. A rope for the church bell, and a shelter for the school bus stop at mile marker nineteen.

Nineteen times he had come home with sawdust on his shirt and said he'd been at the shop, and I had never asked at whose.

I looked for mad, the way you hunt for your glasses on top of your head, and I could not find it. I found something large and sore and proud instead.

I was still sitting there when Tessa's minivan came up the drive.

She got out with her clipboard and looked at the empty trailer tracks, and at the open toolbox, and at the notebook in my lap. "Winnie," she said, "where is the man from Ames?"

"He went home with a band saw."

"I gave my word to a stranger." She did not raise her voice, because she never does, and it was worse for that. "I can't get this house ready if you undo every single thing I do. Winnie, you cannot live in a museum."

I closed the notebook. "I've spent thirty-four years on a mail route, Tessa," I said. "A museum is where you keep things nobody visits. A route is the opposite. Everything on it is still in use. Every box has somebody at the other end waiting for something."

"It's a tin of index cards," said Tessa. Her voice shook, just a little, and I saw her catch it. "Two hundred scraps of paper that you never cooked from. It was mildew and silverfish, and it was taking up a whole shelf."

"I never cooked from it," I agreed. "I read it. You didn't throw out a shelf. You threw out the route."

For one moment I thought she would argue. Then something went across her face that I could not read.

"Greg told me to slow down," she said, quietly. "So I am. The open house is Saturday at ten, Winnie. It's small. It's Bryce and the stager and a few agents. You don't even have to come."

"It's my house."

"I know it is." She turned to go. "I'm only trying to help you."

Her minivan went down Elm Row and out of sight. I carried the red toolbox up to the kitchen myself, one handle at a time. Then I went to the hall closet and took down the thing that Tessa's helpers had somehow missed, behind the vacuum cleaner. The map of Route Three, big as a bedsheet, with two hundred and forty-odd pins.

I unrolled it over the passbook and the ledger, and I did not hurry. I had eighteen thousand four hundred dollars, nineteen dots, and three days.
