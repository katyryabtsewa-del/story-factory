# E001 — Story bible

Present day of the story: September to December of this year; epilogue the following June. The story is told by Diane looking back from that June.

## Characters (8 named — do not add more named characters)

**Diane Whitaker** — narrator, 69. Widow. Born and raised in Harlan Creek, Ohio. With her husband she ran the Blue Kettle, a diner on Main Street, for thirty-one years; they sold it eight years ago. Famous in town for her pies (sour cherry, buttermilk, apple with a cheddar crust). Calm, observant, dry humor, slow to anger and impossible to fool. Speaks plainly, short sentences when she is angry. Habit: when she is thinking, she wipes the counter that is already clean. Wears her reading glasses on a beaded chain Olivia made her. *(Added in ch. 2:)* Her late parents and grandmother stay unnamed: her father bought the sapphire ring in nineteen fifty-one; her grandmother pieced a quilt and left the vinegar pie recipe. *(Added in ch. 11–12:)* Walter baked exactly one pie in his life, an apple pie, the week Diane was in the hospital having Rachel, "so the house would smell right"; the crust "could have shingled a roof," and for forty years he told the counter his pie was better than hers. Diane "does not take directions from a telephone" and reads the map aloud.

**Walter Whitaker** — her late husband, died fourteen months before the story begins (heart attack, in July of last year) at seventy-two. Carpenter's hands, ran the grill at the Blue Kettle, quiet and careful, wrote everything down. Built the kitchen table himself in nineteen eighty-four. Fished every Saturday with Harold. *(Added in ch. 9:)* At the Blue Kettle he rang a little bell on the pass when an order was ready (Diane taps her glass "the way Walter used to"). *(Added in ch. 12:)* Always wanted to see the Grand Canyon; they could never close the diner long enough, and after the sale there was his heart.

**Kevin Whitaker** — eldest son, 44. Sales manager at a car dealership in Columbus. Big house in a Columbus suburb, a boat he cannot afford, credit card debt, a bonus his employer clawed back. About eighty-five thousand dollars in debt that nobody in the family knows about. Charming in public, impatient in private, calls his mother "Mom" in a soothing voice when he wants something. Leader of the plan. *(Added in ch. 5–8:)* Knew the date of Diane's checkup from the calendar on her refrigerator (Brittany spent three Saturdays in that kitchen). Named the group chat "Operation Sunset" (Olivia: "He names everything like it's a sales contest."). Assumes the trust says "three children, three equal shares"; has never read it. Called Diane's Thanksgiving idea "really mature of you." *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Thanksgiving: navy sport coat and tie, the leather folder with the power of attorney set on top of the china hutch; carved the turkey on the platter "until it came apart in rags"; calls Olivia "kiddo" and Brittany "Britt." Once talked his way out of a speeding ticket and a failing grade in chemistry. Got his first bike, a red one, at six and fell off eleven times (Walter wrote it down). Leaving, he said "I'm going to get a real lawyer" (he never did) and, for the first time in seventeen years, told Brittany no: "Leave her, Britt. She drove herself." Then: "Drive careful tomorrow, kiddo." Afterward: sold the boat in February; the leased black car went back to the dealership and he now drives a small gray car; the big house was listed in March; Tuesday nights with a financial counselor on the phone. Sunday in April: came alone with a store-bought apple pie in a plastic dome; confirmed the eighty-five thousand dollars (boat, credit cards, clawed-back bonus); "There really was a car wash, Mom, but I was never going to put a dime into it. I needed that sixty thousand to keep the cards quiet." "We told your doctor you were losing your mind so we could sell your house." Peeled the blue dot off the underside of Walter's table. Comes every other Sunday since, always with a store-bought pie. Drove Olivia to her cooking program interview.

**Brittany Whitaker** — Kevin's wife, 42. Former pharmaceutical sales rep, now "between things." Polished, organized, status-hungry, speaks in a bright, sing-song voice and calls Diane "Mama Di," which Diane hates. Made the spreadsheet. The real engine of the scheme. *(Added in ch. 2:)* Married to Kevin for seventeen years; had never once driven down to see Diane on her own before the "declutter" Saturdays. *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Thanksgiving: cream-colored dress and heels; champagne with a gold bow, put in Diane's refrigerator "for after"; "Careful on those, Mama Di. Just think, at Maple Grove, no more stairs."; Maple Grove talk at dinner (outlet mall shuttle on Tuesdays, bingo on Fridays). Outburst at the reading: "This is insane. She's confused. She leaves the stove on, Harold! You can't let a confused old woman hand our money to a sixteen-year-old." Left holding the leather folder; "Olivia, get your coat." Forgot the champagne. In the spring she went to stay with her sister for a while.

**Rachel Whitaker** — middle child, 41. Dental office manager in Indianapolis, divorced last year, one son (ten years old, unnamed, lives with her). Anxious, avoids conflict, wants approval from her older brother. Went along with the plan for the money and the jewelry; uneasy about it. Partly redeemed at the end. *(Added in ch. 5:)* Her son is learning the trumpet. When she nearly confesses she says, "Never mind. It's nothing. I'm just tired." The work wedding has already happened by October 29; she says she "keeps forgetting to mail" the ring and will bring it at Thanksgiving. *(Added in ch. 8:)* Her son spends this Thanksgiving with his father, so Rachel comes alone. Diane's last words to her before Thanksgiving: "don't forget your grandmother's ring." She calls Harold "Mr. Greene." *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Arrived at eleven on Thanksgiving with bakery rolls; tried twice in the pantry (the gravy boat: "I need to tell you something before Mr. Greene gets here"; the cranberry sauce at twenty to one: "Mom, about Grandma's ring"), stopped first by her own nerve, then by Kevin's horn. At the reading she set the ring, wrapped in tissue paper, on Walter's table: "I wore it to the wedding, Mom. And then I put it in my drawer and told myself it was going to be mine anyway." Fourth grade: went out of the county spelling bee on "meringue." Washed dishes with Diane after (Rachel dries); her confession: she sat in her car in the work parking lot for an hour waiting for an answer to her chat message, and "I was so glad he'd answered me that I forgot what I'd asked." Diane: "forgiveness is a recipe like any other... if you rush it, it comes out tough" and "Call me Sunday." She stayed the night in her old room; has called every Sunday since. Came at Christmas with her son, who played "Silent Night" on the trumpet.

**Tommy Whitaker** — youngest son, 36. Mechanic, owns a small two-bay repair shop in Harlan Creek. Lives ten minutes away, comes to Sunday supper every week, fixes the porch and the gutters, drives Diane to appointments. Not married. Gentle, few words, the siblings look down on him ("the mechanic"). Knows nothing about the scheme until Thanksgiving. *(Added in ch. 5:)* Slow to anger like Walter, "but when it comes, it comes all at once"; that is why Diane keeps him out of it. At the October 30 supper he brought rock salt for the porch steps, changed every smoke detector battery, and checked the back door lock twice. *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Came at nine on Thanksgiving in a new flannel shirt; brought the two table leaves up from the basement; asked "Mom, why is Harold Greene coming to Thanksgiving?" and let it go. Said grace: "Thank you for this food, and for Mom, who made all of it. Thank you for Dad. We miss him. Amen." Stopped Kevin from pulling out Walter's chair with a look. Read the "free dinners" page himself and said "Kevin" in a low voice; Diane's hand on his wrist kept him in his chair. At five he took apart Diane's toaster; one screw left over (Diane found it in Walter's shirt pocket on wash day; the toaster rattled for nine more years). Learned about the house from Diane alone that night: "I don't want a house, Mom. I want you around for another thirty years." Diane: "Then you'll get both." In April: Kevin came by the shop; "I let him hold the flashlight."

**Olivia Whitaker** — Kevin and Brittany's daughter, 16. Junior in high school. Wants to be a chef. Spends many weekends with Diane baking; Diane is teaching her the Blue Kettle pie recipes. Brave, honest, a little shy. Has the old family iPad that is still signed in to her father's account, which is how she sees the group chat. *(Added in ch. 1–2:)* Kevin and Brittany's only child (this matters for the trust: Kevin's share passes to her alone). Has her driver's license and drives herself down from Columbus. Keeps a recipe notebook and writes down Diane's Blue Kettle stories. In ch. 2 she notices her mother's color-coded dots ("Mom uses these. She color-codes everything.") and goes quiet; this seeds her later decision to show Diane the chat. *(Added in ch. 6:)* On October 30 Kevin and Brittany were in Cincinnati overnight for a dealership awards dinner, so nobody knew she drove down. She uses the old iPad for cooking videos; a message with Diane's name popped up and she opened it. She slept in Kevin's old room, alarm at five, and left at six the next morning with a thermos of cocoa. Diane told her: do not delete anything, do not answer anything, do not tell anybody, put the iPad back, and stop looking. Diane's promise: nobody will find out she showed the chat "unless you decide to tell them yourself." *(Added in ch. 8:)* Diane has told her only that Harold will read some papers at Thanksgiving and that what she showed might come up; Olivia chose to be in the room ("you won't be standing in it alone"). She does not know Kevin's share will pass to her; she learns it at the reading in ch. 10. *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Drove down after school on Wednesday, November 23, made the crusts with Diane, and stayed through Thanksgiving night (Brittany said Diane could use help "at my age"). Stood at the reading: "From me." Learned there that Kevin's share is hers. Stayed behind when her parents left; Harold let her beat him at gin rummy. The Saturday after Thanksgiving she and Diane peeled forty dots off the furniture. Kevin let her come at Christmas. Brings news of her father on Saturday mornings. In May she was accepted to a six-week summer cooking program for high school students, starting in July (unnamed). Drove most of the way to the Grand Canyon in June.

**Harold Greene** — 74, estate lawyer in Harlan Creek, semi-retired, Walter's best friend and fishing partner for forty years. Bow tie, unhurried, very dry. Drew up the Whitakers' trust six years ago. Keeper of Walter's sealed letter. *(Added in ch. 1:)* Owns a cabin on the lake north of town; gave Walter a key in nineteen ninety and never asked for it back, so Walter always called it "the cabin." That is why Brittany's spreadsheet lists "the lake cabin": the Whitakers do not own one. Diane "made a note to tell Harold he had been divided up too" (a possible dry callback in ch. 7). *(Added in ch. 7:)* Callback used: "Tell them the roof leaks over the bunk beds, and the mice have a lease." Tall and thin; wore an orange bow tie on Halloween. His coffee "has been terrible for forty years." Uses a yellow legal pad and a fountain pen. Explains the trust as "a big strong box with a lid"; the trustee "holds the key." On being trustee for Olivia: "Mostly Tommy. I don't intend to live forever, but I intend to give it a good try." Calls Diane "Diane"; Rachel and Olivia call him "Mr. Greene." *(Added in ch. 9–12:)* Thanksgiving: arrived at half past one in the orange bow tie again ("A man my age gets two holidays out of a good tie") with a scuffed brown briefcase older than Kevin; "ate the way he fishes, slowly and patiently, with his eyes on everybody." To Brittany: "Her doctor writes that she is sharper than most forty-year-olds, Mrs. Whitaker, and I have just had dinner with two of them." To Kevin: "Please do. Have him call me. I'll be fishing, but I'll call him back." Christmas: green bow tie; opened Brittany's champagne ("a shame to waste a perfectly good payday").

## The trust (the payoff mechanism)
- **The Whitaker Family Trust**, created six years ago, after Walter's first heart scare. Revocable living trust. House, investments and later the life insurance are inside it. Diane has been sole trustee since Walter died. She can change it at any time.
- Original terms: everything to the three children in equal shares after Diane dies.
- **Amendment signed on November third**, in Harold's office, with a notary and two witnesses from Harold's staff:
  1. **The sentence that matters:** "Any beneficiary who, during my lifetime, attempts to have me declared incompetent, or attempts to take control of my person or my property without my consent, shall be treated for all purposes of this trust as though he or she had died before me."
  2. Under the trust, the share of a child treated as having died before Diane passes to that child's own children. So Kevin's share goes to **Olivia**, held in trust until she is twenty-five, with Harold and later Tommy as trustees.
  3. The house is left to Tommy, with Diane keeping the right to live in it for life.
  4. Fifty thousand dollars set aside now for the **Blue Kettle Scholarship** for Harlan Creek High students who want to cook for a living.
  5. Rachel is not covered by the clause: she never signed, called or filed anything. Her share stays.
- *(Added in ch. 7–8:)* The sentence is on page two of the amendment. Diane signed with the heavy old pen from the Blue Kettle register and initialed beside the sentence; the notary's seal "made a sound like somebody cracking a walnut." Harold's plain-English version (ch. 7): "If any of your children tries to get a judge to call you incompetent, or tries to take charge of you or your things without your say-so, then as far as this trust is concerned, that child died before you did. Not really dead, of course. Dead on paper." The share "goes down one step, to that child's own children." On whether it reaches back to October: "During my lifetime is a long time, Diane. It covers this October as well as next month." Olivia's share is held until twenty-five "so nobody can talk a girl out of it at eighteen." Diane's reason for the house: Kevin wrote that Tommy gets nothing extra. Diane on Rachel: "Going along is wrong, but it isn't what your sentence covers."
- Diane also gets a full cognitive evaluation from her doctor (results: completely normal, "sharper than most of my forty-year-olds") and a written letter from the doctor. This makes any guardianship attempt hopeless. *(Added in ch. 8:)* The test took most of two hours on Friday, November 4 (clock face set at ten past eleven, five words recalled, counting backward from one hundred by sevens). The doctor phoned Diane herself on Wednesday, November 9: "it's completely normal. Better than normal. You are sharper than most of my forty-year-olds." One copy of the signed letter went to Harold; the other is in Diane's purse next to the printout of Brittany's call. *(Added in ch. 10:)* Harold calls the doctor's letter "dated the ninth of November."

## Walter's letter
Sealed envelope Walter gave Harold six years ago: "For Diane, if the kids ever start circling. Read it to all of them." Content (Harold reads it at Thanksgiving): Walter remembers each child as a boy or girl with love; reminds them their mother fed half the town for thirty-one years and kept the books to the penny; says nothing in this world ever got past her, so if they are hearing this, they already lost; asks them to be the people they were at that kitchen table. Ends: "Diane, the pie is better than mine. It always was."
*(Added in ch. 7:)* A long envelope, yellowed at the edges; the words "For Diane, if the kids ever start circling. Read it to all of them." are written on the front in Walter's square, careful handwriting. Walter gave it to Harold the week the trust was signed and told him he "would know when." Harold showed it to Diane on October 31 without handing it over; she did not open it ("in all our years of marriage I never once opened his mail"), and it went back in the safe.
*(Added in ch. 11:)* Harold carries it in his jacket at Thanksgiving and slits it with the butter knife from the buttermilk pie. Inside: three pages torn from one of Walter's spiral notebooks, the paper fringe still on the edge. Text as read (keep consistent if quoted again): opens "Kids. If Harold is reading you this, I'm gone, and your mother has had about enough of something. My guess is it's you. I was never any good at speeches, so I wrote it down. Memory gets tired. Paper doesn't." Kevin: the red bike at six, eleven falls, "I know it was eleven because I wrote it down." Rachel: fourth-grade county spelling bee, out on "meringue," spelled it right three times in the car, "You just have to say it out loud the first time." Tommy: the toaster at five, one screw left over, "She knew by Monday," "putting this family back together ever since, mostly when nobody was looking." Then: "Your mother fed half this town for thirty-one years, and she kept the books to the penny. Nothing in this world ever got past her, so if you are hearing this, you have already lost." Then: "I built that table so there would be room for all of you. You were loud at it, and you were hungry, and you looked after each other, and you were good. Be the people you were at that table. That's all I want. Love, Dad." The last page holds only: "Diane, the pie is better than mine. It always was."
