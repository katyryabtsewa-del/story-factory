# Saturday, Ten O'Clock

The stager arrived at eight on Saturday morning, and as far as Tessa knew, her open house was going exactly as planned.

The stager was a brisk woman with a helper and a van full of throw pillows. By nine she had put a bowl of fake lemons on my table, taken down my curtains, and carried Ruth's cast-iron rooster doorstop out to the garage, because a rooster draws the eye in the wrong direction. I carried it back. She carried it out again. This went on three times, and the rooster won.

Bryce Lindqvist arrived at nine fifteen, a young man with white teeth and a vest with his name stitched on it, and he stuck a sign in my front yard and a lockbox on my door and told me the house had great bones. Tessa stood on the porch with her clipboard, calm as a pond. Greg stood beside me with his hands in his pockets. He had come at dawn with no casserole and no plan, and had told her he was here to be useful and not to help.

At five minutes to ten, a car turned into Elm Row.

It was Dewey Marsh's brown sedan, with Hilda in the front seat holding a foil-covered pan on her knees. Behind it came a pickup, and behind that a van I knew from the Lund twins' farm, and behind that a little gray car that belonged to Ila Fenn, with two children pressed to the back window. Then they came the way a church lets out, gravel and doors and hellos, cars along both sides of the road as far as the corner. I counted without meaning to, the way I always do, and I got to thirty-one.

They carried covered dishes. Cabbage rolls from a card with a dot in the corner. A pan of corn fritters, still warm. Pickled beets, a ham, three kinds of bars. Two rhubarb crisps, because Hilda had made one and a woman from mile marker six, whose handwriting I had watched shake, had made another. Every dish had come off one of my cards, and a dozen of them were dishes I had read about all my life and never once tasted.

The Lund twins walked into Walt's shed, took down two old doors that lay across his sawhorses, and set them up on the lawn as tables without asking anybody's permission. A folding chair went up for Hilda. Somebody found a roll of paper plates. Bryce stood in the middle of it with a stack of brochures and said, "Is this a block party?"

"It's an open house," said Dewey Marsh, handing him a plate. "Eat something, son."

Tessa came down the porch steps and stopped on the lawn with her clipboard against her chest, and I saw her take it in, the tables and the dishes and the faces. Her mouth was a thin line. I walked over and stood next to her, and I said it softly, one time, so that nobody else would have to hear.

"I'm not moving, Tessa. I'm busy on Tuesdays."

She studied my face. Then she looked at Greg, who nodded to her, once, and she lowered the clipboard.

Bryce took the sign out of the yard himself. He collected his lockbox, shook my hand, and told Tessa he would call her Monday, and I will say this for him, he left with a plate of cabbage rolls and the manners of a boy raised right. The stager left her fake lemons behind. Nobody had the heart to give them back.

Tessa did not leave. She stood on the edge of the party with a paper plate that she had not filled, until Hilda Voss, eighty-eight, from her folding chair, pointed her walker at Tessa and said, "Young woman. Eat that. You're thin." So Tessa took a spoonful of rhubarb crisp, and I watched her take a bite, and her shoulders came down by an inch.

The party went on until the middle of the afternoon. Greg and I stood against the shed wall, and he cleared his throat and told me he was sorry. He said he had been so busy handling things that he had forgotten to ask a single one. I said I knew, and that I was sorry too, for saying thank you in a kitchen when I should have said no. He took my hand, and we watched Hilda boss the Lund twins around the tables.

On Sunday evening Tessa came back, alone, in jeans. I was sitting on the lawn in a chair with a cup of coffee in the long light, and she sat down on the grass beside me, and for a while we listened to the peepers.

"My mother's name was Carol," she said. "She kept newspapers. Then she kept mail, and boxes, and everything. I was thirty. A stack fell in the hall, and I found her." She looked at her hands. "I read labels for a whole year afterward. I cannot see a full shelf without feeling that something is going to fall. I know that's not your shelf, Winnie. I know. It's just that I couldn't."

"Folks who throw things out are usually afraid of something," I said. "Walt wrote that."

She laughed, a short, wet, startled laugh. "He wrote that about me?"

"He said not to be mad at you, and I haven't managed it, which is annoying." I patted the grass. "I won't tell you it's all right, Tessa. You threw out my box. But I understand now, and I can start from there."

"Could I," she said, and her voice cracked. "Could I ride along on a Tuesday? And be taught the route?"

That is how it began, the Tuesday Route. By September I had driven it eleven times with Tessa Brandvold in the passenger seat, a clipboard on her knees and a label maker in her purse, which she is learning to leave in the glove box. We visit the nineteen porches, and a few more that Walt never found. I spend the Route Fund slowly, and never on a whim.

In September I also wrote my very first recipe card, at a counter in Tessa's kitchen, with Tessa watching over my shoulder. It was a bad card. It said, Toast. Put the bread in the toaster. Stand there. Do not leave. Tessa laminated it. She tells me it was the funniest thing she has ever put through a machine.

That card is in the tin now, right behind card one hundred seventeen, Hilda's crisp. Walt's letter lives there too, with the lid taped shut, the way he would have done it.

If you were Winnie, would you have forgiven a woman who only threw things out because she was afraid? And what is the thing in your house that you could never bring yourself to throw away? Tell me in the comments where you are listening from, and thank you for staying with me.
