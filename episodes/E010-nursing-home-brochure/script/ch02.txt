# Card Ending Four-Four-One-Seven

The morning after my seventieth birthday I sat in Lu Banks's kitchen on Cottonwood Street with a gray cat on one knee and a mug of coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. Lu is my oldest friend. I trained her at the console in nineteen eighty-four, when she was a girl with a laugh like a screen door, and she has been laughing at me ever since, mostly with love.

I told her about the gift bag. I told her about the flag on page nine, and then I put the reservation confirmation on the table between the sugar bowl and a cat.

Lu read it with her glasses down her nose. "Four-four-one-seven," she said. "That's your card."

"That's my card."

"Peg, in thirty-six years I never once heard you swear, and I'd like to hear it now."

"I'd rather read it back," I said. "My daughter has a card of mine that she's been using for two years. She used it for a two thousand five hundred dollar deposit on a room I never agreed to. That's the problem as stated."

"That's a felony in some counties," Lu said cheerfully. "In others it's just Tuesday. Want me to call the sheriff? I still know a few of those boys. Most of them are dead, but I know the rest."

Lu has always made her jokes at the edge of a bad night. We both learned that from the console. You laugh at the right moment or you carry the call home with you.

I should tell you how it had gotten that far. It had not happened all at once, and that is the part I am least proud of. After Hank died, Dana took over my pharmacy, which was kind, because I was tired. Then she took over my mail, because I had let some bills pile up that winter, and then she began to sit in on my appointments, and one day I found out she had been calling my doctor's office to tell the nurse about her concerns. When I asked what concerns, Dana said, "Mom, just things." In August I had put the kettle on for a cup of tea and gone out to the garden to look at the tomatoes that the frost was about to take, and I stayed longer than I meant to. 

Dana, who had stopped by with a casserole, found the kettle empty and black on the burner and the whole kitchen smelling of hot metal. Nothing caught fire. I turned it off myself, with a pot holder, and I was embarrassed enough that I let her tell the story however she liked.

Then in September a woman I had never met drove up in a white sedan and said she was a friend of Dana's. She said she was a realtor, and she was only stopping by to look at the light. The light on what, I asked her. "On the house," she said. "It's lovely, the light." She stood in my kitchen for ten minutes admiring my window, and she left a business card, which I still have.

"That woman was pricing your house," Lu said.

"Maybe. Or maybe she's a friend who likes light. I don't know yet, and neither do you."

"Peg Okerlund, you are sitting there with your daughter's hand in your purse."

"I am sitting here with a card number," I said. "I don't know why she did it. A call can sound like one thing and be another. I've heard a man scream that he was killing his wife, and what he was doing was trying to get a splinter out of her foot. I want to know what's true before I say what it is."

Lu looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "You want to fight her?"

"No. I want to see what she saw."

So I did three things that Sunday afternoon. First, I called my bank, the nice one in town, and I asked them to cancel the card ending four-four-one-seven and issue me a new one, and a young woman said she would be glad to, and that no, there was no need to tell anyone. They would send it to my own mailbox, which for two years had not been mine. I drove to the post office on Monday and filed a change of address with my own name on it. It felt the way you feel when you pick your own coat off the hook.

Second, I called Silverleaf Terrace.

"Silverleaf, where every day is a homecoming," said a bright voice. Then, when I gave my name, the voice lit up like a porch. "Miss Peggy! Gideon Pratt, admissions director. Your daughter has told us so much about you. We are so excited. You're going to love it here. Welcome home."

I had not been home there for a minute and a half, but I said thank you. I asked about the week's respite stay, and he said it was two hundred fifty dollars a night, one thousand seven hundred fifty for seven nights, and that he would have a room ready for Sunday, October eleventh. He suggested I come at ten in the morning so we could do "a little intake chat." I said that would be fine. I wrote down the time of that call as well, ten past three, on the Sunday after my birthday.

Third thing. At five o'clock my telephone rang, and it was Dana, as warm as a heating pad.

"Mom! Gideon called me. I can't tell you how proud I am. You're being so mature about this."

"Thank you, dear," I said.

"You'll see. It's not what you think. It's a home. And you'll have people your own age around, instead of that big quiet house."

"I do like to see things for myself," I said, which was true, and she took it for surrender, which was her right.

I spent the next week doing what a person does before a trip. I paid the electric bill. I watered the geraniums on the sill. On Saturday night, the tenth, I packed one bag with three cardigans, my good slippers, a paperback, my pill organizer, and a pair of reading glasses, and I put the green notebook in my pocket with two sharpened pencils and a spare in case the first two went on strike.

Sunday morning the aspens along Willow Bench Road were bare except for a last stubborn gold tree by the cattle guard. I drove Hank's truck the twenty minutes out to the edge of town, and I pulled into the parking lot of a beige two-story building and shut off the engine.

Out front there was a fountain with a stone bird on top, and it was not running. A banner on the porch said Welcome Home in blue letters, and the banner was rippling in a good cold breeze.

I opened the notebook against the steering wheel and wrote, in the margin, nine forty, and beside it, Sunday, arriving.
