# Thanksgiving Morning

On Thanksgiving morning, the day my children believed they were coming to hear my will read, I was up at five o'clock in a dark kitchen with a twenty-two pound turkey and a string of order tickets hanging over the stove. I unlocked the Blue Kettle at that hour for thirty-one years, and I have never lost the habit.

Olivia had driven down after school on Wednesday, and we had made the crusts that night. Brittany had been glad to let her come. She said it would be good for me to have some help, at my age. At half past five, Olivia came downstairs in her socks, and we went to work without much talking, the way you do on a line. The sour cherry went in first, then the buttermilk, then the apple pie with its cheddar crust. By ten to seven, all three were cooling on the counter by the window, and the kitchen smelled of butter and cinnamon and hot fruit.

At seven o'clock exactly, I slid the bird into the oven. Olivia unclipped the ticket that said, "Bird, twenty-two pounds, in at seven," and tucked it into her apron pocket. We were running the day like the old ticket rail. When a job was done, its ticket came down, and the pie tickets stayed up until the pie was on a plate.

Tommy came at nine without being asked, in a new flannel shirt that still had the creases from the package. He carried the two leaves for Walter's table up from the basement, and he and Olivia pulled the table apart and slid both leaves into the middle. I ran my hand along the seams and thought about the underside, where a little blue dot was still sitting beside Walter's name.

Tommy set out the chairs, counted us off on his fingers, and came up one short.

"Eight chairs," Tommy said. "Who's the extra?"

"Harold Greene," I said.

"Mom, why is Harold Greene coming to Thanksgiving?" Tommy asked.

"Because Harold has never missed my pie," I said.

That was true. In forty years I had never once seen Harold turn down a slice. It was also not an answer, and Tommy knew it. He studied me the way he studies an engine that is making a sound it shouldn't. I kept on peeling potatoes. Tommy has his father's patience, and after a while he nodded and let it go.

"Okay," Tommy said. "You'll tell me when you want to."

I nearly told him then. But dinner was five hours away, and I did not want him carrying it one minute longer than he had to.

Rachel came at eleven, alone, with a bag of bakery rolls and her purse clamped against her side the way you hold a purse on a city bus. Her boy was at his father's for the holiday.

I sent her to the pantry for the big gravy boat on the top shelf. A minute later, she called me in.

"Mom," Rachel said, standing under the pantry light with the gravy boat in both hands, "I need to tell you something before Mister Greene gets here."

"All right," I said.

She looked at the cans of pumpkin for a long time. "Never mind," Rachel said. "Do you want the big boat or the little one?"

At twenty to one, she followed me into the pantry again, for the cranberry sauce, and this time she got as far as opening her purse. "Mom, about Grandma's ring," Rachel said.

Out in the driveway, a car horn honked twice, short and cheerful. Rachel snapped her purse shut. My daughter is forty-one years old, and that horn did to her exactly what it did when she was six and her big brother was waiting in the car.

I could have made it easy for her. I could have said, I know, honey, I have read every word. But Rachel had let other people speak for her for most of her life, and just once, I wanted to hear her speak for herself.

Kevin and Brittany had come forty minutes late to Walter's memorial lunch. To hear his will, they came twenty minutes early.

Kevin wore a navy sport coat and a tie, and he had a leather folder tucked under his arm. Brittany wore a cream-colored dress and heels that were not made for a gravel driveway, and she carried a bottle of champagne with a gold bow around its neck. She watched me come down the porch steps to meet them.

"Careful on those, Mama Di," Brittany said. "Just think, at Larkspur Commons, no more stairs."

"Tommy fixed that one in October," I said. "It's the best step in Harlan Creek."

Brittany moved my cranberry sauce to make room for her champagne. "For after," Brittany said, and she winked at Kevin. Kevin set his leather folder on top of the china hutch, the one with his blue dot on the back, and patted it once, the way you pat a dog to tell it to stay. Then he kissed the top of Olivia's head.

"Hey, kiddo," Kevin said.

"Hi, Dad," Olivia said, and she went back to mashing potatoes harder than any potato has ever needed.

Harold came up the walk at half past one carrying a scuffed brown briefcase that was older than Kevin. He had worn the orange bow tie from Halloween. "A man my age gets two holidays out of a good tie," Harold said. In the front hall I asked whether he had brought his reading glasses, and he patted his breast pocket.

We sat down at two. Walter's chair stood empty at the head of the table. I asked Tommy to say grace, and Kevin, who had already cleared his throat to say it himself, closed his mouth.

Tommy bowed his head. "Thank you for this food, and for Mom, who made all of it," Tommy said. "Thank you for Dad. We miss him. Amen."

Then Kevin stood up to carve. For just a second his hand went to the back of his father's chair, as if he meant to pull it out. Tommy looked at him, and Kevin let go and carved standing up. Kevin had watched his father carve for forty Thanksgivings, and he sawed at the bird on the platter until it came apart in rags. Kevin carved the way he remembered his father carving, which is not the same thing at all. We ate it anyway. Gravy forgives a great deal.

All through dinner, Brittany talked about Larkspur Commons in her bright, sing-song voice. There was a shuttle to the outlet mall on Tuesdays, Mama Di, and bingo on Fridays, and you would never have to cook again. Harold ate the way he fishes, slowly and patiently, with his eyes on everybody.

At four o'clock Olivia brought in the pies. She took the pie tickets down one at a time as she cut, until only the last one was left on the string, the one that said, "Table of seven, plus Grandpa's chair." Kevin glanced at his folder on the hutch. Brittany glanced at the refrigerator.

I picked up my fork and tapped my water glass, the way Walter used to ring the little bell on the pass when an order was ready. Every face at that table turned to me.

"Harold," I said, "I think it's time."
