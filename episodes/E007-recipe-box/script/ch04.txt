# The Receipt Under the Magnet

My son had just told me he signed a paper about my house, and I still had to get from that porch step to a rainy hill of garbage, so let me do it in order.

"It's a listing agreement," Greg said. He was talking to his hands. "With Bryce, from Prairie Home Realty. It isn't final, Mom. Tessa said that if we had it on paper, we could at least talk about numbers. Three hundred and ten thousand. In June, maybe. It's only so we can talk."

I let a full minute go by. The peepers went on in the ditch. Then I said it calmly, and I said it once, because I did not intend to say it twice.

"Greg, that house is in my name. It has been since your father passed, and before that it was in both our names. It's not yours to list, and it's not Tessa's, and a paper you signed on a Friday doesn't change who owns the porch we're sitting on. But I'll tell you what bothers me, and it isn't the law. Nobody asked me."

He nodded for a long time. His eyes were wet, and I did not know what to do with that, so I went inside and brought him a glass of iced tea. He said he would talk to her. He drove home in the dark, and I believed him.

On Monday morning a truck backed a big green rolloff dumpster into my driveway at seven o'clock. It was thirty yards, about the size of a school bus. Tessa arrived at eight with two young men in gray shirts, and Greg came behind them with a stack of flattened boxes.

"A few things from the garage," Tessa said, brightly. "And the back bedroom. It's easier on everybody if you're not underfoot. Why don't you go see Hilda?"

I am seventy, not stupid. But I had spent a week saying no to this woman, and I was tired, and I have never learned to be suspicious of a person who offers me a day off. I drove out to mile marker eleven in the Ford and did not look back.

Hilda Voss listened to the whole story with her hearing aid turned up for once. She is eighty-eight, with tennis balls on her walker and a mouth like a paring knife. When I got to the part about the dumpster she put down her spoon.

"Winnie Brandvold," she said, "nobody rents a thirty-yard dumpster for a garage."

"It's a big garage."

"It's a big house, honey." She picked the spoon up again. "Go home and look in your cupboards."

I did not go home and look in my cupboards. I ate her soup, I let her tell me I was thin, and I came home at suppertime to a full dumpster with a tarp over it, a kitchen so clean it echoed, and a note on the counter that said Thank you for being so flexible, Tessa. I did not open a cupboard, because I do not open cupboards for a living, and I went to bed.

Tuesday morning you already know, the clean square in the dust and the receipt under the magnet. I stood in my kitchen with my hand on the counter, and I understood what Hilda had meant, and why a woman who cannot see a full cupboard without wincing would want two hundred and twelve index cards gone before I woke up.

I phoned Del Okafor, whose number I have had since nineteen ninety-six. He answered on the second ring.

"Larkin County Transfer," he said, as if it were already open.

"Del, it's Winnie. Somebody's dumpster is on its way to you from Elm Row. There's a green tin box in it. It's the size of a bread box, and I need it back."

There was a pause.

"Come out the back," Del said. "I'll unlock."

I put on Walt's rubber boots, which have stood by the back door since his last spring and are two sizes too big for me, and I drove the Ford ten miles on wet gravel at a speed I will not repeat to the sheriff. Del was waiting at the gate in a rain slicker, and that is how I came to be standing at the edge of the tipping floor with a rake in my hand.

The floor was a concrete pad as big as a ballroom, walled on three sides, and my load lay in one long slope against the back wall. Bags, boxes, soaked cardboard, a mattress, a high chair, somebody's wedding dress in a dry-cleaning bag. It smelled like a basement that had been given up on.

"Start at the top," said Del. "Light stuff on top, heavy on the bottom. A tin goes to the bottom."

"It's been tipped, Del. Everything's mixed."

"Gravity doesn't read the manual." He spread the tarp out on the concrete for me and put his thermos on it. "Loader idles fifteen minutes before it moves. Regulation. I'm a stickler."

I have decided since that it was the kindest sentence anyone said to me that spring.

I climbed. The rubber boots slid. I dragged the rake through wet cardboard, and every bag I opened was something of mine. There was a bag of old Christmas cards from the route, the ink running in blue streaks. There was the hen cookie jar, cracked straight through the beak. There was the corkboard that Walt had pinned by hand, with a thumbtack still in it, and a strip of paper that said, in his square printing, Win, dentist Thursday.

I sat down on a mattress with that strip of paper in my hand. It was twenty-five minutes past six and my knees had started to sing. I thought, quite plainly, that I was a seventy-year-old woman digging for an old tin in the trash at dawn, and that perhaps I ought to let go.

Then I thought of Hilda's violet ink, and the farm wife at mile marker six with her shaky hand, and I put the strip in my jacket and got up.

At six forty I had worked my way down to the bottom third of the pile, where the weight had pressed things flat. I turned over a sodden flap of cardboard and saw a dent. A green, dented corner, the size of my palm, upside down in a puddle with its lid still shut.

I did not shout. I sat down in the garbage and pulled it into my lap with both arms. It was heavy with water. I wiped the lid with my sleeve until the little latch showed, and my hands shook so badly that it took me three tries.

When the lid came up, the water ran out of it like water out of a boot. Two hundred and twelve cards lay inside in a gray soup, ink bleeding off every one. And pressed against the lining at the back, something pale and small and plastic that I had never in my life put there.
