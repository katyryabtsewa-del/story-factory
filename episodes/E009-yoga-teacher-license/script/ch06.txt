# Tropical Storm Odalys

I had a form in my purse, a date on a sticky note, and a week of ordinary work to get through, and then the Gulf of Mexico sent us a tropical storm named Odalys to see if I meant it.

She never came ashore properly. She brushed the coast on Sunday, the twenty-eighth of September, the way a big cat brushes your leg, leaving a lot of wind and about nine inches of rain in twelve hours, and everyone on Mobile Bay who owned a swimming pool had the same thought at the same moment. The thought was, Will it overflow? And once a person has that thought, she does not call a company. She calls a person.

My cell phone started ringing at four in the afternoon. I was in my kitchen with a flashlight on the counter, because I have lived on the Gulf Coast sixty-five years and I leave nothing to chance. The first call was a Missus Dupree, whose pool drained, as she put it, toward her hydrangeas. The next was a man whose wife had gone to her sister's and who had never touched his pump in his life and was nearly in tears. For thirty-seven years I had written my cell number on a magnet that went on every customer's refrigerator, and not one of them had ever called the office number in an emergency.

By six o'clock I had counted forty-three calls. The office line, I found out later, had rung two hundred and six times, and Tripp Haverty had answered a hundred and ninety of them himself.

I called Otis. He said, "I'll be at your curb in nine minutes, Miss Mimi," and he was, with the wipers going, and I climbed in with my flashlight, a thermos, and a binder of cards.

Here is the thing nobody had ever put in a spreadsheet. Every pool we ever built has an index card in my binder that tells where the main drain valve is, which way it turns, whether the overflow line runs to the street or to a neighbor's ditch, and which pools have a quirk, like the pool where somebody in nineteen ninety-six had put the valve under a birdbath. Otis had most of it in his head. I had the rest on paper. Together we were the only two people in the county who could get to the right bolt in the dark.

We worked that Sunday night until almost five in the morning. We pumped down eleven pools, secured six covers, and dug a trench in the sand for a woman who held an umbrella over us the entire time. Otis did the heavy parts, and I held the flashlight and read the cards. At one point Otis said, "You know, Miss Mimi, Mister Boyd has never once been out in this."

"No," I said. "He's a very good salesman."

"Yes ma'am," said Otis, sincerely, and we pumped.

Around three that Monday morning we came to Magnolia Point Road, to a pink brick house with columns and a swimming pool the shape of a kidney bean. Nettie Rhodes was standing on her porch in a yellow rain poncho and white tennis shoes, holding a flashlight of her own. She is eighty-one, a retired school principal, and she has swum thirty laps in that pool every morning at six for thirty-six years, for her hips.

Nettie came down the steps to meet us like a general. "I called the office three times, and a very polite young man took my name. Marilyn, I taught school for forty-one years, and I know what 'somebody will call you back' means."

"We're here now, Miss Nettie," Otis said.

"I see that." She put her hand on my arm. "Mimi, I don't know what that boy is up to, and I don't need to. But whatever you're fixing to do, I'll follow you. You tell me where to sit, and I'll sit."

"Thank you, Nettie," I said, and I will confess that my eyes were not entirely dry, though it was raining so hard that nobody could have proved it.

Monday morning I went into the office, because the checks had to go out, and every line on the phones was blinking. Tripp was standing at my old desk in a wet shirt with a headset on, saying, "I understand, ma'am, yes, I understand, I'm so sorry," and he looked as though he had been treading water since Sunday. In the middle of the front room stood Skye Calloway in white linen, holding a green juice and gazing at the telephone as if it were a strange bird.

"It keeps making that sound," she said, to nobody.

"That's the phone, dear," I said. "You push the button that's blinking."

"Which one? They're all blinking."

"Then you push any of them, and you say, Lanier Blue Water, and then you listen."

She did, and a man shouted something about his skimmer basket, and she set down the phone gently. I went on into the supply room, and five minutes later Tripp knocked.

"Missus Lanier," he said hoarsely, "I'm sorry to bother you. They keep asking for you. They say you'd know where the valve is."

I gave him the three pools that needed a crew before noon and a list of customers who were frightened rather than flooded, and I did it kindly, because he was a decent young man in over his head. He stared at the list. "How do you know all this?" he said.

"I wrote it down, Mister Haverty. It's what a bookkeeper does."

That evening I saw Boyd. I was in Otis's truck near Pelican Bluff, a subdivision of nice brick homes, and Boyd came rolling down the middle of the street in a rented golf cart under a company umbrella, waving at the homeowners as if he were in a parade, bless his heart. Not one of them waved back. A woman on a porch looked at him, then looked at the truck with Otis and me in it, and raised her coffee cup to us instead.

Around two o'clock on Tuesday morning, after a second night of pumping, I sat on the tailgate of Otis's truck in a thin leftover drizzle and told him. I did not tell him all of it. I said, "Otis, in a few hours I'm going to stop being something I have been for twelve years. I need you to know your pay and your jobs are safe for sixty days. If you'd rather not know the rest, I'll understand."

He took off his Mudhens cap, held it on his knee, and was quiet so long that a heron landed on the fence behind us.

"Miss Mimi," he said, "whatever you're fixing to do, I'm in."

Then I remembered my phone, and found a voicemail left at ten-forty. I put it on speaker. It was Boyd, in his warmest voice, the one he uses on customers who are about to sign.

"Hey, honey. Big day tomorrow. Tuesday, nine sharp, in the conference room. Bring a pen."
