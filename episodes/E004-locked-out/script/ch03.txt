# Whispers

By Monday morning I had a bed at Winnie's, eleven garbage bags in her basement, a steno machine on her dining room table, and a brass key I still could not explain.

I had asked Gus on Sunday what he meant. He said that two summers ago, Ray had stood at the key machine at the shop, grinding a blank, and said in his sideways way, "If anything happens, the box key is on my coat." Gus had asked what box. Ray had only turned up the grinder. Gus had thought about that sentence ever since. I told him I had found the key, that it said four hundred seventeen, and that I did not yet know what door it belonged to. He wrote the number on his wrist with a ballpoint pen, as if the pen might know.

I decided I would find out on Wednesday. Monday, on the other hand, belonged to the church.

Winnie had a casserole dish to return to the fellowship hall, and she insisted I come, because, as she put it, a woman who hides after a funeral gives the neighbors a head start. So at ten o'clock I walked into the church basement with my purse, my good posture, and a clean glass dish. The ladies of the funeral committee were folding tablecloths around the long table, seven of them, and I have known some for fifty years.

They stopped talking when I came in, the way a lake stops moving when the ice arrives.

I did what I used to do when a witness lied under oath, which is to stand still and say nothing, and listen. A woman by the coffee urn said to the woman beside her, in a whisper meant to be overheard, "Well, you know what they say. She was in that hospital room every day, and it was only after those last signatures."

"Worked on a dying man," said a third, very softly, folding a tablecloth into a square. "That's what Tessa told people. She said her father would never have left everything to a wife of nineteen years."

I put the casserole dish down on the table. I said, "Good morning, ladies. Thank you for the lovely luncheon on Saturday. The ham was excellent."

No one answered. I picked up the guest book from the end of the table, because I had come for that, and I added, in a pleasant voice, "I was in that hospital room every day, and I have the visitors' log to prove it. I also have excellent handwriting, in case anyone would like a statement in writing."

A very old woman in the corner looked down at her tablecloth and smiled at it. It was not much of a victory, but I wrote it down.

In the car, Winnie said, "I wanted you to slap one of them."

"I wanted to correct their grammar," I said. "We are both disappointed."

That afternoon I took a legal pad out of my purse and began what I thought of as the record. I wrote the date and time at the top of each page. Saturday, three twenty, locks changed. Saturday, eleven at night, three texts. Sunday noon, eleven bags on the porch, steno case uncovered. Monday ten, church basement, seven women, quote, worked on a dying man. In my experience people tell the truth for about a week and then begin to improve the story, so I wrote it all down while it was still honest.

Tuesday brought paper. At nine in the morning, a teenage boy in a Happy Tails sweatshirt knocked at Winnie's door, handed me an envelope without looking up, and fled to a car with its engine running. Inside was a letter on heavy cream stock with a very official heading, Kessler Estate Services, and two pages of sentences that began with Whereas. It informed me that the property at eighteen Birchwood Lane was the sole inheritance of Tessa Kessler, that I had no right of entry, and that any attempt to retrieve further belongings would be considered trespass.

I read it twice. Then I did what I had done a thousand times in court when a document smelled wrong, and read the bottom first. There was no attorney's name, no office address, only a line of small gray print along the bottom edge. Form generated at an online legal template site, free trial.

"Oh, honey," said Winnie, reading over my shoulder with her glasses on her forehead. "She got her lawyer off the internet."

"I would not call it a lawyer," I said. "I would call it a printer."

Then the phone rang, and it was Gus, with the redness back in his voice. That morning Tessa had called the company that runs the shop's card machine and told them she was the new owner, and they had frozen the account. He had four customers waiting with broken keys and could not run a single card. "Missus Kessler," he said, "I don't understand why she's doing this. She learned to cut keys on that machine. Ray taught her when she was nine."

I did not understand either. I did understand, from the text that arrived while we were still on the phone, that she was in a hurry. The text said, I have a buyer interested in the house. Closing is the end of February. Please stop contacting people in the congregation.

"Contacting," I said to Winnie. "I said good morning."

I wrote the text on my pad, with the time. Under it I wrote a question mark, and under that, a second one. A buyer and a closing date, arranged in four days. Even in Port Alder, nobody sells a house that fast. Somebody had been planning this for a long while.

It was past three when Winnie, who had been standing at her front window for most of the afternoon, with her thermos, said, in a voice that was not her whisper at all, "Loretta. There is a man at your house."

I came to the window. Across the street, on the sidewalk in front of my property, stood a tall man in a long camel-colored coat. He was holding a phone at arm's length and turning slowly, photographing the porch, the shutters, and the roofline, the way a man photographs something he already thinks he owns.

Winnie was out the door before I could stop her, in her flowered boots, with a snow brush in her hand, announcing loudly to the empty street that she needed to scrape her windshield. By the time she had crossed the lane the man had slid something through my brass mail slot, nodded politely to her, and gone back to a gray car at the curb.

Ray's mail slot has a stiff spring. It had caught the corner of what he left, and it stuck out half an inch. Winnie pulled it free with two fingers and carried it back across the snow as if it were a dead mouse.

It was a business card, heavy and cream colored. It said Dale Voss, Senior Funding Partner. Under it, in small silver letters, Lakeshore Bridge Funding.

"What is a funding partner?" Winnie said.

"A man who lends money," I said. I stared at the card. "We paid off that house eleven years ago. I put the mortgage paper in the shop safe myself. So why on earth would a lender be photographing it?"
