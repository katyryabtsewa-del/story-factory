# A Visit Without Notice

My son was still on the witness stand, going on about the stove, but I was thinking of a Tuesday one week earlier, when a woman in a brown cardigan walked up my porch steps without calling first.

I was in the kitchen with my sleeves rolled, labeling jars of pear butter, and I heard the steps before I heard the knock. There are nine steps from the gravel to my porch, and I have counted them every day for forty years. These were light and even, the steps of someone who did not want to startle a person. I opened the door. She was a woman of about forty with short hair going gray at the temples and a leather folio under her arm, and she had a face so still and attentive that it felt like being looked at by a lake.

"Missus Halvorsen, I am Imani Okafor," she said. "The court has asked me to visit you about the petition your son filed. I am sorry to come without notice. It is how the court prefers it."

"Then you had better come in," I said, "because the kettle is the only thing in this house that has any notice of anything."

She laughed, a short real laugh, and came in. A court visitor, I learned, is a social worker the judge sends to look at a person and her house and her life, and then to write down what she sees. I had imagined a stern woman with a clipboard and a face like a locked drawer. What I got was a woman who admired my kettle, accepted a cup of tea with a spoonful of Pearl's honey in it, and sat at my kitchen table with her folio closed for the first twenty minutes, doing nothing but listening.

Then she asked me ordinary things. How I spent my days. Who did the bookkeeping, and I said I did, in green ledgers, the way Lloyd had. How I kept track of my pills, and I said I took one for blood pressure and one for cholesterol and kept them in a plastic box with the days of the week on the lids, and that I had never once missed a Thursday. She asked what I would do if a fire started in the kitchen. I said I would turn off the burner, put a lid on the pan, and call the fire department, in that order, and that I had done exactly that once, years ago, without anyone writing a petition about it.

She wrote that down. It was the first thing she wrote, and I will admit that I felt it in my stomach.

At the ninety-minute mark, though I only knew it because I was watching my husband's watch, she closed the folio and asked whether I would be willing to walk the back acres with her. I said that I walked them every evening and would be glad of the company. I put on my barn jacket. She had brought rubber boots in her car, which told me she had known where she was going before she came.

It was a gray afternoon and the north rows were bare, the old Bosc trees standing with their knuckled branches against the ridge, the buds still tight as fists. Imani walked beside me and said very little. She asked how long I had walked out here at dusk, and I said forty years, that Lloyd and I had done it together every evening after supper, and that since he died I had gone on doing it because the trees seemed to expect me. She asked what I looked for, and I said frost, fallen branches, and deer at the bark. She nodded as if that were a perfectly sensible answer, which of course it was.

Near the top of the rows she stopped, and I stopped. There was an orange stake in the grass with a bit of pink ribbon tied to its top, and then another one a few yards off, and a third beyond that, running along the fence line like a row of small flags. She looked at the first stake for some time. Then I saw her lips move.

I know a counter when I see one. Imani Okafor was counting those stakes, silently, with her eyes going down the line, and she walked on to the corner of the fence and counted again, and then she bent and looked at the dry grass where the ground was torn with the marks of heavy tires. She took her pen out. She wrote something in the folio. She did not ask me one question about any of it, and I did not volunteer a word, because a woman who is being measured does well to let the measuring woman finish.

At the porch she shook my hand and thanked me for the tea. I asked whether she could tell me what she thought, and she said, in that voice like warm water, "I am afraid I keep my opinion for the judge, Missus Halvorsen. But I did enjoy your kettle."

That was all I got. It was enough to keep me awake all week.

The courtroom came back around me. Brent had moved on from the stove, and his lawyer was walking him through the second item on the petition, with great care.

"And the wandering," the lawyer said. "Would you describe what you mean by that?"

"My mother goes out into the orchard at dusk," Brent said, and he shook his head, sadly. "Alone, in the dark, on uneven ground. She has done it for years, but she is sixty-nine now. If she fell, nobody would know for hours."

I had walked those rows for forty years. Lloyd and I had worn a path in the grass that you could see from the road.

"And the property taxes?" the lawyer asked.

"She missed an installment," Brent said. "The county sent notices, and they went unanswered. That is not like her, and that, for me, was a sign that something was wrong."

I knew exactly why I had missed it, and why the notices had gone unanswered, and I knew it for a reason that had made me sit in my truck for a long time a few days before. I held it in my lap and said nothing. My young lawyer leaned toward me with his pen raised and whispered, "Do you want me to object?"

"No," I said, just as quietly. "Let him finish."

He gave me the look a new substitute driver gives when told to take the long way around, and then he lowered his pen. Brent adjusted his blazer cuffs and turned to the judge, plainly arriving at the part he had practiced the most.

"Your Honor," he said, "my mother has become convinced that someone is on her land. She calls the sheriff. She has done it again and again. She thinks men are hiding in her trees."
