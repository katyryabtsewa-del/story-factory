# The New Lock

On the afternoon we buried my husband, my stepdaughter had a stranger change the locks on my front door.

I came up Birchwood Lane that Saturday with a casserole dish in my hands and Ray's good overcoat over my arm, and the snow was blowing sideways off Lake Erie the way it does in January, as if it had somewhere to be. There was a white van in my driveway with the name of a locksmith chain from the next county painted on the door. There was a bright silver deadbolt on my green front door, shining like a new tooth in an old face. And there was Tessa, standing on my porch in a black wool dress, holding a manila folder against her chest like a hymnal.

"This is my father's house, Loretta," she said. "It's mine now."

I looked at her. I looked at the lock. I was sixty-eight years old, I had been a widow for eleven days, and I had just eaten a ham sandwich at a church luncheon in a dress that pinched.

"Thank you for the update, Tessa," I said. "Would you mind if I set this casserole down? It's heavier than it looks."

She had hired a man to change my locks while I stood at the cemetery. What she did not know was that Ray had been a locksmith for forty-six years, and that he had left me something she could not pick.

Let me tell you how I came to be standing on that porch, because it begins about three hours earlier, with a coat.

Ray's funeral was at eleven, in the church where we had been married, and the pastor, a kind woman with a cold, said that my husband was a man who spent his life opening doors for other people. I thought it was a little neat, but I did not mind. Afterward the funeral director, a man in a gray suit who spoke as softly as a library, handed me a clear plastic bag with Ray's wristwatch, his reading glasses, his wallet, and his wedding band. He had checked every pocket of the blue suit but not the overcoat.

The overcoat was charcoal wool, heavy as a rug, the one Ray wore to every funeral for forty years. He had not been buried in it, so I carried it myself, over my arm, from the car to the cemetery, because I could not think of anywhere else to put it.

It was bitterly cold at the grave. Tessa stood on the far side of the hole in the ground with her jaw set, and when I reached for her hand she found a reason to look at her phone. I shifted the coat to my other arm, and my fingers pressed on something hard, down in the hem of the lining, where a seam had come loose.

I am a person who notices things for a living, or I was, for thirty-three years. I did not stop in the middle of a funeral to investigate. I stood and I listened and I sent my husband off. But my fingers went back to that hard little shape three times before the pastor finished, the way your tongue goes to a chipped tooth.

In the car afterward, with the heater running and the coat in my lap, I worked the seam open with my thumbnail. Out slid a small flat brass key, a little worn at the edges, with the number four hundred seventeen stamped into the head. It was tied with butcher's twine to a paper key tag, and on the tag, in Ray's square capital letters, he had written five words.

L. when you need it.

Ray wrote on the backs of key tags all his life, grocery lists and phone numbers and once a very short apology. I held that tag for a long time. Then I put the key in the pocket of my dress, because I thought I would have time.

At the church hall, Tessa ate nothing and left early, saying she had a headache. Winnie Gault, who has lived across the street from me for twenty-six years and has never once left a funeral without taking something home, pressed the casserole into my hands and told me to go on ahead and she would bring the rest of the dishes. So I drove home alone with Ray's coat on the passenger seat, and that is how I arrived at the porch, and the van, and my stepdaughter with her folder.

"You can't just do this," I said.

"I can," Tessa said. "I'm his daughter. His only child. Everything he had is mine, and I'm sorry, I know it's today, but I couldn't let it sit. There are things that have to happen quickly."

That was an odd sentence, and I wrote it down in my head the way I used to write things on my steno pad. There are things that have to happen quickly. She did not look at me when she said it. She looked at the folder.

I put my key in the new lock anyway, because I am stubborn and because I wanted her to watch me do it. The key slid in a quarter of an inch and stopped, like a man who has lost his place in the hymn.

The young locksmith was leaning on his van with his hands in his armpits, and his ears were red. He had pulled the old cylinder Ray cut by hand for our door, and I do not think he had ever seen a lock like it outside of a museum.

"Ma'am," he said quietly, "I was told it was the owner's wish. I'm sorry. That was a beautiful old cylinder. I wish somebody had asked me before I pulled it."

"You were told wrong, young man," I said. "But it is not your fault, and I thank you."

He climbed into his van and left without his clipboard. Tessa told me I could collect my things sometime next week, that she would have them bagged and ready, and that I should please not make this ugly. Then she went inside my house, and I heard the bolt slide home with a sound as clean and expensive as a bank door.

I stood on my porch in the snow. I did not cry, which surprised me, and I did not shout, which would have surprised everyone else. A blue station wagon came down the lane much too fast, and it stopped at my curb with two wheels in the plow bank. Out climbed Winnie in her flowered snow boots, with a thermos under her arm.

"Loretta Kessler," she said, in the loud whisper she has used for twenty-six years, "you get in this car right now, and you tell me what that girl did, and then I am going to say a word I have not said since nineteen seventy-one."

I got in. She poured me tea from the thermos and it scalded my tongue, and I was grateful for it. Then I took the little brass key out of my pocket and held it up to the dome light, and Winnie leaned over to look.

"What on earth is that?" she said.

I turned it to the light. Four hundred seventeen.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But Ray wanted me to have it."
