# Lakeside Cedar Retreat

A white box had just slid down the top of my son's old tablet, and I stood in my kitchen a full minute before I touched it. A nurse learns to look before she puts her hands on anything. Then I put on my reading glasses and picked it up.

Craig never set a code on that tablet. My son has never once believed a thing could be taken from him, so the screen opened for me the way a door opens for a neighbor. The notification was from a booking service I had never heard of. The page it led to was titled Lakeside Cedar Retreat, and it was written in the bright, breathless voice of a person who sells candles on the side.

Historic cedar lake house on Wassumkeag Cove. Sleeps eight. Private dock, wood stove, classic red canoe. Six hundred dollars a night, two-night minimum. Book your memories now.

Historic. I read that word twice. Frank built the house in nineteen eighty-nine, and he was so proud of it that he made everybody stand on the porch for a picture, including the mailman.

The photographs were worse. There was the red canoe, lying in the grass with a bowl of lemons set on the middle seat, which is the most ridiculous thing I have ever seen done to a boat Frank Ellery built with his own hands. There was the mudroom, with the forty years of boots along the wall, captioned Rustic charm, ready for your adventure.

Then came my bedroom.

Somebody had taken off my quilt, the one my sister-in-law made the year we married, and put on a plain white comforter folded back in a triangle the way they do it in hotels. The reading glasses were gone from the nightstand. The caption read, Primary suite, with lake views and a king bed.

The bed is a queen. I know that because Frank carried it up those stairs himself.

I looked at the corner of the picture, where the tablet had stamped a date in tiny gray numbers. It was the fourteenth of July, the week I spent at my sister's in Vermont with the house locked up and a spare key in my son's pocket. My quilt had been back on that bed since the day I came home, and I had never noticed a thing. Craig had simply walked through my bedroom with a camera while I was away, the way a man walks through a house he is thinking of buying.

I scrolled. There was a calendar with colored bars across the days. One bar ran from the thirty-first of December through the fourth of January, and beside it was a name I knew: Whitcomb Events. Eight guests. Four nights at six hundred dollars, deposit paid in full, two thousand four hundred dollars. Somebody had been very organized about my New Year's.

After the calendar came an email thread, and the name at the top was Pinecrest Commons. I know that place. Every nurse in Maine knows the places where people go to be looked after. I had visited three of my old teachers there, with a box of paperback mysteries and a bag of hard candy, and I had watched each of them learn to ask for help one small, humiliating piece at a time. The first message was from Craig.

We're looking for a room for my mother, starting in January. She's very independent and very private, and she'll need someone to look in on her a couple of times a day. Her lake house is going to be a short-term rental. Until the room opens she can stay with me in Portland, and I'm sure she'll be thrilled.

The reply came back in under an hour. We have a room available January fifteenth. Would your mother like to come see it?

Craig had written back, "She'll come around. She always does."

So that was what settled by New Year's meant. I would be driven to Portland at Christmas with a suitcase, put on my son's sofa bed for two weeks like a houseguest with a limp, and then delivered to a room in town, while eight strangers drank wine on Frank's porch. And I would say yes. That was the part he was sure of. I took my glasses off and cleaned them on my fleece. Then I put them back on, because a chart is no place to quit halfway.

The last thing on the tablet was a group chat, the same one called Ellery Family Feast, except that this one had only three people in it. Tyler, Brooke and Craig. The messages were from one forty that afternoon, about the time I was carrying a plate down the shore path.

Tyler: Grandma's house is going to be insane for New Year's.

Brooke: Wait, does Grandma know about any of this?

Craig: Not yet. I'll tell her Friday. It's no big deal.

Tyler: Ha.

Craig: She won't even be there, she'll be settled by then.

Brooke: That is not what I asked.

Craig: Brooke.

That was the end of it. I thought about the boy with the sprained wrist, and his mother saying, Dot, he does that.

Then the phone on the counter rang, and the screen said Craig. I let it ring. I did not decide to; my hand simply stayed where it was. I stood in my own kitchen watching my son's name light up, and my heart went about as fast as it does on a long flight of stairs. It stopped, and a minute later the phone chimed with a voice message. I played it on the speaker.

"Hey, Mom. Happy Thanksgiving. We're stuffed, the chef did this thing with a duck. I hope you had a nice plate. Okay. Love you. See you tomorrow."

He sounded happy. That is what I could not get past. He sounded like a man with nothing on his conscience at all.

Here is how I read when I am frightened, which is not often. I read the way I read a chart at the nurses' station, top to bottom, dates first, then numbers, then the words people use when they think nobody is checking. I got the legal pad from the drawer by the telephone, the one Frank used for dock measurements, and I wrote everything down in two columns. I wrote the word primary and underlined it twice.

I did not cry. I would like to tell you that was strength. Mostly I was too busy counting bedrooms.

Then I went back to the tablet one last time, to the bottom of the thread, and found one more line I had missed. It was dated that morning, and it was not to me or to Pinecrest. Craig had written to a man named Gary Hale.

Friday, be at the lake by ten thirty and bring your tape. Mom will be in the kitchen with the coffee on. She won't even notice you measuring.
