# Saturday, Ten O'Clock

The stager arrived at eight on Saturday morning, and as far as Marnie knew, her open house was going exactly as planned.

By nine the stager had put a bowl of fake lemons on my table, taken down my curtains, and carried Ruth's cast-iron rooster doorstop out to the garage, because a rooster draws the eye in the wrong direction. I carried it back. This went on three times, and the rooster won.

Bryce Lindqvist arrived at nine fifteen, a young man with white teeth and a vest with his name stitched on it, and he stuck a sign in my front yard and a lockbox on my door and told me the house had great bones. Marnie stood on the porch with her clipboard. Greg stood beside me. He had come at dawn, as promised, with no casserole and no plan.

At five minutes to ten, a car turned into Elm Row.

It was Dewey Marsh's brown sedan, with Hilda in the front seat holding a foil-covered pan on her knees. Behind it came a pickup, and a van I knew from the Lund twins' farm, and a little gray car that belonged to Ila Fenn, with two children pressed to the back window. Then they came the way a church lets out, gravel and doors and hellos, cars along both sides of the road as far as the corner. I counted without meaning to, and I got to thirty-one.

They carried covered dishes. Cabbage rolls. A pan of corn fritters, still warm. Pickled beets, a ham, three kinds of bars. Two rhubarb crisps, because Hilda had made one and the farm wife from mile marker six had made another. And every cook had brought a fresh card, copied out by hand that week, because they had heard what became of the old ones. Hilda's was in violet ink again. The Lund twins' mother had written hers twice, once for each son.

The Lund twins took two old doors off Walt's sawhorses and set them up on the lawn as tables without asking anybody's permission. Bryce stood in the middle of it with a stack of brochures and said, "Is this a block party?"

"It's an open house," said Dewey, handing him a plate. "Eat something, son."

Hilda had been lowered into a folding chair, and she tapped her walker on the grass until the lawn went quiet.

"I am eighty-eight years old," she said, "and I will say this once. In two thousand nine my roof fell in, and somebody I never saw spent three days on it, whistling. I have been grateful to a stranger for sixteen years. On Thursday I found out the stranger was Walt Brandvold, who let me insult his wife's cooking every Christmas." She looked straight at Marnie. "This house is not clutter, young lady. It is where that man came home to from every porch in this county."

"He did my furnace," said Dewey, into the silence.

"He built my father a ramp," said Ila, "and never let me say thank you."

I said nothing about money, then or later. I walked over and stood next to Marnie, who had come down the porch steps and stopped on the lawn with her clipboard against her chest, and I said it softly, one time, so that nobody else would have to hear.

"I'm not moving, Marnie. I'm busy on Thursdays."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she looked at Greg. My son walked across the grass to Bryce Lindqvist, and he said, in a voice the whole lawn could hear, "Bryce, I'm withdrawing that listing agreement. My mother never signed it."

Bryce looked at his plate of cabbage rolls and then at the sign. "Understood," he said, with the manners of a boy raised right. "Should I take that down?"

"Leave it, Bryce," I said. "And the lockbox. Until supper." I will give you the reason in one sentence. I wanted my neighbors to eat under that sign, so that none of us would forget how close it had come.

Marnie stood at the edge of the party with a paper plate she had not filled, until Hilda pointed her walker at her and said, "Young woman. Eat that. You're thin." Marnie took a spoonful of rhubarb crisp. Then she looked at the stack of fresh cards on the table, and she said, in a small voice, "Those should be laminated. All of them. I could do that, if you'd let me."

"Hilda gets the first one," I said.

That afternoon Greg told me against the shed wall that he was sorry. He had been so busy handling things, he said, that he forgot to ask a single one. I said I was sorry too, for saying thank you in a kitchen when I should have said no.

On Sunday evening Marnie came back alone, in jeans, with a cardboard box. She unscrewed the lockbox from my door herself with a screwdriver from Walt's pegboard. Then she sat down on the grass beside my chair and opened the box. Twelve mugs, wrapped in paper towels. It had taken her until Friday to find them all in the church thrift, and the one with the cracked handle had cost a quarter.

"I'm sorry, Bernie," she said. "I should have asked. I should have asked about every single thing."

I held the Feed and Seed mug in both hands. "Tell me about your mother," I said.

"Her name was Carol. She kept newspapers. Then she kept mail, and boxes. I was thirty. A stack fell in the hall, and I found her." She looked at her hands. "I read labels for a whole year afterward."

"Folks who throw things out are usually afraid of something," I said. "Walt wrote that."

She laughed, a short, wet, startled laugh. "He wrote that about me?"

"I won't tell you it's all right, Marnie. You threw out my box. But I understand now, and I can start from there."

"Could I ride along on a Thursday?" she said, and her voice cracked. "And be taught the route?"

That is how it began, the Thursday Route. Each week one neighbor cooks a dish from the card of the next porch down the road, and I carry it there, with Marnie in the passenger seat, a clipboard on her knees, and a label maker in her purse, which she is learning to leave in the glove box. By September we had made it eleven times. I spend the Route Fund slowly, and never on a whim.

In September I also wrote my first recipe card, at a counter in Marnie's kitchen. It said, Toast. Put the bread in the toaster. Stand there. Do not leave. Marnie laminated it, and she says it is the funniest thing she has ever put through the machine. It is in the tin now, right behind card one hundred seventeen, and Walt's letter lives there too, with the lid taped shut, the way he would have done it.

If you were me, would you have forgiven a woman who threw things out because she was afraid? And what is the thing in your house you could never bring yourself to throw away? Tell me in the comments where you are listening from, and thank you for staying with me.
