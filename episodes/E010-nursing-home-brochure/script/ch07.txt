# Read Back

On the morning of the conference, I walked past a canvas tote bag on the lobby bench, and the woman beside it did not look up from her paperback, as we had agreed. The conference room had a long oak-colored table, blinds half drawn, and a bowl of foil-wrapped mints in the exact center, like a centerpiece at a very small wedding. It was Thursday, October twenty-second, ten o'clock.

Gideon Pratt had taken the head of the table, in a crisp vest, with a folder and a smile. On his right sat my daughter in her good wool coat, with a pen in her hand, and beside her sat her husband, hunched, looking at the grain of the table as though it had a sad story to tell. On Gideon's left sat a tall woman with her glasses on a chain, who had been introduced to me as the director of nursing, and who looked at the clock twice before I sat down.

"Miss Peggy," Gideon said, "we are all just so pleased. Let's make this easy."

I took the chair across from Renata and put the green notebook on the table. I wrote the time in the margin, which made my daughter sigh.

Gideon opened his folder. "Our team has reviewed Miss Peggy's screening. Nineteen out of thirty, which puts her in the moderate range, and our recommendation is secured memory care. This is a gift, because we've caught it early." He slid two papers toward me. "The residency agreement, and the power of attorney, which Renata has so kindly prepared."

Renata uncapped the pen and held it out to me, handle first, the way you hand scissors to a child. "Mom, I know what's best."

"Thank you, dear," I said. "Before I sign, may I read something back? It's a habit from work. It will take four minutes."

Gideon glanced at the director of nursing, then at the clock. "Of course, of course. We have nothing to hide."

I opened the notebook. "On Sunday, October eleventh, Mister Pratt began my screening at ten twelve. I said five words back to him, I drew a clock, I named twenty-two animals, and I missed the date by one day, because I was looking at the fountain. At ten thirty-one it was over. And I wrote this line. Mister Pratt turned the form over before he scored it."

"Miss Peggy, this is sweet," Gideon said, "but the form is what matters."

"Then let's have the form. It says my recall was zero of five. Lantern, spoon, river, saddle, ash." Renata's pen stopped moving. "It says I could not draw a clock. And it carries a stamp at ten twenty, eleven minutes before my test ended."

"Mom," Renata said, "it could be a clerical error."

"It could. I'll keep going." I turned a page. "On Monday and Tuesday I timed the call lights in Birch Court. Eleven minutes, nineteen minutes, twenty-seven minutes."

"Staffing is a challenge everywhere," Gideon said.

"On Wednesday at two forty, Mister Abernathy fell in the hallway. The aide was alone with twenty residents. I called nine-one-one at two forty-four, and I was told it would be written up as a slip in his room. And on Friday at breakfast, you told the dietary manager that Birch Court had to be filled by the end of December, because the owner's lender had a little list."

"That is taken out of context," said Gideon, with the smile still on.

"I'm sure the context is lovely," I said. "Here is what I would like to understand, Mister Pratt. If your form is right, and I scored nineteen of thirty, then I'm impaired, and I cannot legally sign the power of attorney you just handed me. If I can sign it, your form is wrong. So which is it, and why is it in my file?"

"This is absurd," Gideon said. "Miss Peggy is clearly, understandably, confused."

I turned to the woman with the glasses on a chain. "Ma'am, I have one question. Where were you on the morning of October eleventh?"

She took a long moment to answer. "I was in Denver at the state care conference," she said stiffly. "I drove back on Tuesday. I knew your name from the census, Missus Okerlund. I had not met you."

"Your stamp says you approved my results at ten twenty on Sunday morning."

The director of nursing looked at the form, and then at Gideon. He had stopped smiling, and without it his face was only a face.

"I did not review this," she said, "and I did not stamp it. The stamp is kept at the nurses' station, where anyone can reach it. And I never saw a fall report for Mister Abernathy either."

My daughter turned her head and looked at Gideon Pratt. It was not a hard look. It was the look of a woman who has found the floor lower than she thought.

At ten twenty, by the clock on the wall, someone knocked, and the receptionist put her head in. "Mister Pratt? There are two people from the state in the lobby. They say they're here for an inspection. They have a laptop."

I had told Odette I would run this as though nobody were coming, and I was not sorry to be wrong. Through the open door I could see two people in dark coats and lanyards, and behind them a pair of round glasses over a paperback. Odette gave me the smallest nod. In the doorway stood Imelda in her pink cat scrubs on her day off, with her rosary wrapped around one shaking hand.

"Let's not make this about paperwork," Gideon said, standing.

The woman surveyor stepped past him and set her laptop by the mints. "Actually, sir," she said, "it's going to be about paperwork."

I closed the notebook and looked at my daughter across the table, and I kept my voice low. "Card ending four-four-one-seven," I said.

Renata went white from her collar up. "Mom, I can explain."

Her husband had not spoken all morning. Now he put both big hands flat on the table and looked at her. "Renata," he said. "That's your mother's card. I asked you whose card it was, and you told me it was ours."

I stood up, put the notebook in my cardigan pocket, and left the pen on the table. Nobody stopped me.

In the lobby, beside the fish tank, sat Cyril Abernathy in a wheelchair with his plaid blanket and his blue cast. Imelda had brought him down. "I asked to see the front door, Missus Okerlund," he said. "Forty-one paces to the keypad door, and only twenty-two from here to the street. I will be logging that." He saluted me with a surveyor's pencil.

I walked out under the Welcome Home banner, past the dead heron, and across the lot to Hank's truck. I had my keys in my hand when I heard the automatic doors sigh behind me, and quick footsteps on the pavement.

"Mom," my daughter said.

I did not turn around. Not yet.
