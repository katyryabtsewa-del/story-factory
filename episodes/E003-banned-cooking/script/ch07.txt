# What He Said on the Terrace

My son had followed his bride out to the terrace while the tent was still on its feet, and I did not chase them. A mother who runs after a bride in front of the whole wedding has made the evening about herself, and I had already had my three sentences. So I let the brown butter apple tart come to my table, and I ate every bite of it.

Priya bent down to my ear when the course was cleared. "They're on the lake side," she said. "Mister Ashford is working the room to buy you time. And Missus Pruitt, I looked it up. Section nine binds the client, not us. It is our kitchen, and Marco says the door is open whenever you want it."

I thanked her, picked up my purse, and went out the side of the tent into the cool September dark.

Courtney was sitting on the stone railing in her gown with her satin shoes off and her mascara run down both cheeks, looking about fifteen years old. Gregory stood with his back to the lake and his tie pulled loose. When he saw me, he looked like a man watching a train come.

"Mom," he said. "I'm sorry. The toast, the lunch, the text. All of it."

"I know you are," I said. "That's not the same as being finished."

He nodded slowly, as if he had expected it. Then he sat down on the stone steps in his gray suit, put his elbows on his knees, and did not look up.

"I did my homework in your back booth for twelve years," he said. "And I told the people at the bank that you had retired from something fancy." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I knew the gravy the second I smelled it tonight. I thought, please, nobody say anything. And when I could not stop it, I made a joke about you."

I wanted to put my hand on his head the way I did when he was small. I kept my hand on my purse.

"Courtney," I said.

She wiped her cheek with her wrist. "I used the word cute like a knife," she said, very small, "and I knew exactly what I was doing. I wrote section nine myself. I am not asking you to say it's all right."

"It isn't," I said. I did not soften it, and I did not add anything. Down at the shore the lake slapped against the stones, and nobody spoke for a while.

Then I opened my purse and took out the card with the pressed violet, and both of them looked at it as if it might be hot.

"I bought this card in January," I said. "There was a check in it for forty thousand dollars, toward a house, and a note from me that said Sam would have wanted you to have a kitchen. I took the check out in August."

Gregory's head came up. "Forty thousand? Mom, I did not know. I never asked you for anything."

"No," I said. "You did not. That was part of the trouble."

Courtney said nothing at all. I watched her work out what a down payment was, and then I watched her be ashamed of having worked it out.

She opened the envelope. There was a single white index card inside, and on it, in my handwriting, was the recipe for the pan gravy, down to the last teaspoon of sherry. Beneath it I had written one line. It read, "When you want to learn the real thing, come to Spaight Street on a Saturday and bring an apron."

"That is the kitchen Sam meant," I said. "The money is staying with me for now. I think it has somewhere better to go. Now go back inside. You have a hundred and eighty guests and a father who has been working the room for twenty minutes."

"Mom," Gregory said.

"Go on."

Courtney went first, carrying her shoes. Gregory followed a minute later, a careful yard behind her. I did not tell either of them it would be all right, because I did not know that it would.

By the time I came back into the tent, the music had started and the floor was full, and people kept stopping me. A woman in green asked for the short rib recipe. A man with a white mustache told me his mother had run a diner in Racine and he had not tasted pie like that since nineteen seventy-one. The tall girl with the fork necklace came up with pink cheeks and said, "I am so sorry about the cafeteria thing," and I told her it was an honest mistake. I wrote my address on the backs of programs.

Richard Ashford asked me to dance, and we went around the floor like a refrigerator and a bird.

"You told me you had heard they were very good," he said, in the middle of the second song.

"I had," I said. "From one of the owners."

"Hm." He steered me around a bridesmaid. "I read that invoice, Nora, line by line. Ninety-one thousand dollars. I would pay it twice. And I will tell you something else. In thirty-one hardware stores, we sell every kind of nail ever invented and not one decent jar of mustard. I intend to correct that. I am bringing a case to your next dinner."

"I haven't invited you," I said.

"You will," he said.

At midnight, Priya put her hand under my elbow and walked me through the swinging doors I had been staring at for six hours and up the covered walkway to the big house. The kitchen was a tiled blaze of steel and steam, with forty people in white leaning on counters, and as I came in, every one of them put down what he was holding and began, without being told, to applaud. Marco stood at the end of the line with a tray of sour cream raisin pie bites. He bowed and handed me one, and I stood in my navy dress with my shoes in my hand and ate it, at last, inside a kitchen.

"Fire me?" he said.

"Give yourself a raise," I said.

On Monday morning I drove to Madison College, to a small office with a good view of a parking lot, and I signed my name seven times while a patient woman from the foundation put a gold sticker beside each signature. When I walked back to my car, my telephone buzzed in my purse. It was a text from my son, and I read it standing in the parking lot, just as I had read the first one in March.
