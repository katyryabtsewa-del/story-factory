# Read the Paper

Naomi Feld's office is above a bakery on Water Street, and at eight o'clock on the morning after Trevor's visit, the whole staircase smelled of cinnamon and butter, which is a good smell to climb toward when you are about to learn what a mistake costs.

I carried the three green ledgers in a canvas grocery bag, with the pink sticky note in an envelope on top and the broker's folder underneath. Naomi was at the door before I reached the landing. She is fifty-two, quick and brisk, with short silver hair and purple glasses, and she has been my lawyer since Gerald's estate.

"Marlene," Naomi said. "Sit. Coffee? Good. Now let's read the paper."

She read the broker's folder first, and her eyebrows went up twice. She laid the quitclaim page on her desk and put one purple fingernail on the blank line.

"That's a new way to hand over a house," she said. "I almost admire it." Then she opened the first ledger.

Naomi read for forty minutes. Her pencil moved down each page, and once she laughed out loud, softly, at a line where I had written, in pen, "Garage code changed. Not by me."

When she finished the third book, she took off her purple glasses and set them on the blotter.

"Marlene, first, the numbers. Beyond what Trevor and Cassidy paid you in rent, you have put in one hundred sixty-eight thousand five hundred dollars. That is your share of seventy-two monthly payments, plus the suite, the roof, and the furnace, with receipts, in your handwriting. Second, you are the only name on the mortgage and the only name on the deed."

"Yes," I said.

"Third, in two sentences of plain English. There's no written lease, so they are month-to-month occupants, and a month-to-month arrangement can be ended with proper written notice. And that deed hands them your whole ownership, nothing in that paper pays you for your equity, and nothing obliges you to sign it."

"That's what I thought," I said.

"Then why are you here?"

"Because I wanted somebody to read it who isn't my friend, my son, or me."

Naomi smiled and picked up her glasses again. "Fair. What do you want to happen?"

I told her I did not want to punish anybody. I wanted three things. I wanted to sign nothing. I wanted to end the family rent arrangement properly, in writing, so that nobody could say afterward that they had been surprised. And I wanted to offer Trevor and Cassidy the house, at a fair price, on their own credit, so that they would have what they had pretended to have for six years and I would have back what I had put in.

"Not a punishment," Naomi said, writing it down. "A correction."

"That's it exactly."

"All right. I order an appraisal, which takes weeks. I draft the notice, which says what it needs to say and nothing more, and gives them until the end of ninety days. A notice should land at the start of a month, so we aim for the first week of September. I draft an offer letter with a price. You tell no one. You go to Sunday dinner and you bring lemon bars. And you do not, under any circumstance, let anybody see you do arithmetic."

"That last one will be hard," I said.

"I know," said Naomi. "You hum."

That afternoon, I drove to the Harbor Light Credit Union, where Odessa was waiting in the Buick with the engine running. I had called her after all. I signed a release at the desk, and a young man slid a payoff statement across the counter. Odessa read it over my shoulder, with her glasses at the end of her nose.

"One hundred ninety-eight thousand dollars," she said. "That's the balance, to the dollar, same as the broker's sheet. The house is worth about three hundred ninety thousand. That's a good chunk of equity, Marlene, and every dime of it is yours."

"I know," I said.

"Then why are you still shaking?"

"I'm not shaking," I said, and held out my hand to show her. It was shaking. We both looked at it. Odessa laughed her big laugh, loud enough that a man at the next window turned around, and held my hand flat on the counter until it quit.

Then came the hardest part, which was to behave. On that Friday, I told Trevor I was not ready to sign, that I wanted more time, and that I would not be rushed, which was true on every count. He went white, and then red, and then he said, "Mom, it's fine," in a voice that did not believe it. Trevor told me later that Cassidy lost her deposit on the studio. Bernadette's move, I gathered, slid from August to the first of October, because the studio money had not come through. Nobody asked me about the suite, and I did not offer.

Through July and August, I went to Sunday dinner every week, carried lemon bars up the walk, said "Hello, dear" to Cassidy, and did not do a single sum in front of anyone. I hummed only in the car. Cassidy grew sweeter and sweeter, the way a person does when she means to ask again. She even said, one Sunday, over dessert, "See, Marlene? Boundaries make everything easier. Don't you feel so much lighter?"

"So much lighter," I said, and I passed her the lemon bars.

In late July, the broker telephoned me himself. He had a voice like warm syrup. He said Cassidy had mentioned I was close to a decision, and he would gladly bring the papers to my house. I felt a hymn rising in my throat and turned it into a cough. I told him I would be in touch, hung up, and told Naomi, who said the cough was the best money I had ever spent.

On one of those Sundays, Jonah slipped something into my purse in the front hall. I found it later, folded in quarters. It was a crayon drawing of a train with three cars. He had printed a word on each car in very careful letters, and they said Mom, Dad, and Gran. The engine, which he had drawn with a big smile, did not say anything at all.

At the end of August, with the papers finished and the numbers checked three times, I sat down at Naomi's desk. She slid the notice across the blotter, and I read it from the first word to the last. It was two pages long, with not an unkind word in it. Then I took my own pen, a good blue one, and signed my name at the bottom.

"The courier is scheduled for Friday at four o'clock," Naomi said. "It will go to the house. After that, it's out of our hands."

"And what happens Saturday?" I asked.

Naomi capped her pen and looked at me over the top of her purple glasses.

"The family meeting," she said. "At your table."
