# The Family Meeting

On the first Saturday of September, at two o'clock in the afternoon, three people sat down at my kitchen table, and one of them had not been invited.

The courier had come to Tamarack Court the afternoon before, at four, as Naomi had promised. At nine that night, Trevor called, his voice as thin as wire, to say there was a letter from a law office and that Cassidy thought it was a mistake. I told him I would be happy to explain it at my house at two o'clock tomorrow, and that he should please bring his wife and the envelope. I also asked whether Odessa might take Jonah to the depot museum to see the old locomotives, and Trevor, who would have agreed to almost anything just then, said yes.

I had set the table that morning, with a pot of coffee, a plate of lemon bars on the good china, and four cups, because I had a feeling about Bernadette. I had stacked the three green ledgers in a small tower at my own place.

Trevor and Cassidy arrived together, and so did Bernadette, in a lavender blouse, carrying her purse in front of her like a shield. "I hope you don't mind," Bernadette said. "I heard there was a family meeting, and I am family."

"Of course," I said. "Sit down. Have a lemon bar."

Cassidy set the envelope on the table like evidence. "Marlene," she said, "I'd like to understand why we're getting letters from lawyers."

"I'll read you something first, dear," I said, "and then I'll answer every question you have."

I pulled my glasses down, opened the first green ledger, and read aloud in the voice I used at school board budget meetings.

"The house at forty-one Tamarack Court was bought six years ago for three hundred twelve thousand dollars. I put down ninety thousand, from Gerald's life insurance, and took the mortgage for the rest, in one name, which is mine. You paid eight hundred dollars a month, which was entered as groceries. Everything beyond that, one hundred sixty-eight thousand five hundred dollars, is in these three books, with receipts."

Bernadette's lemon bar stopped halfway to her mouth.

"Now the dates," I said. "January, this year. Garage code changed. Not by me. February, the evening you posted the chalkboard that said home is where we built it. I wrote, in pen, built with ninety thousand dollars of Gerald's insurance and sixteen hundred dollars a month. June, the last Saturday. Support staff."

"That's not possible," Cassidy said. Her smile was gone, and what was left looked younger and frightened. "Trevor told me you were on the paper for his credit. He said you helped."

"Trevor?" I said.

My son sat with his hands flat on the table, looking at the lemon bars.

"Mom paid, Cass," Trevor said. "She's been paying the whole time."

Cassidy stared at him, and then she turned on me, much louder. "You tricked us. You sat in our house every Sunday and let me decorate it and put my name on it, and you let us think we owned it."

"Nobody tricked you, dear," I said. "You were told my name was on the paper. Nobody told you whose money was in it, and you did not ask, in six years, not once. That part belongs to Trevor, who asked me to keep quiet, to me, for agreeing, and to you, for not wanting to know. I will own my share. I am only asking you to own yours."

"Cass, honey," Bernadette said, in a smaller voice than I had ever heard from her. "I gave notice on my apartment. They're renting it to somebody else on the first of October."

Cassidy turned and looked at her mother, and something went out of the room, like air out of a tire.

I opened the second ledger to the envelope I had tucked inside. "This is the broker's closing worksheet. The new loan is two hundred sixty thousand dollars. One hundred ninety-eight thousand of it pays off my mortgage. Of the sixty-two thousand left, forty-one thousand pays your credit cards. The house is worth about three hundred ninety thousand, so the equity I would have signed away is about one hundred ninety-two thousand dollars. Nowhere on that page is a line that pays me a penny."

I slid a second envelope across the table. "This is my copy of the notice you received yesterday. It ends the family rent arrangement, in writing, according to the law, and it gives you until the end of ninety days. And this," I said, and I set the pink sticky note beside it, "was stuck to the broker's cover sheet in the packet Trevor brought me."

Cassidy picked up the note. Trevor leaned over her shoulder and read it slowly.

"Don't let her read it, she'll make it a thing," I said, from memory, and I took off my glasses. "I read it, Cassidy. And I am making it a thing."

"Here is what I propose, in writing, and Naomi Feld will answer any questions you like. From the first of October, the rent is the real cost of the house, twenty-four hundred dollars a month, paid to me. Or I will sell you the house for three hundred sixty thousand dollars, which is thirty thousand below what it is worth. You would take out your own mortgage, on your own credit, with a cosigner who is not me. Trevor's credit has come most of the way back, and your brother has offered at Christmas more than once, Cassidy. We would close within ninety days. If you choose not to, the notice stands and I sell on the open market."

"You're taking our house," Cassidy said.

"I am offering you my house, below its value, so that for the first time you can truly say it is yours," I said. "I am not asking for a profit. I am asking for the arithmetic to come out even."

Then a chair scraped, and Trevor was standing.

I had watched my son graduate, carry a coffin, and hold his boy for the first time, but never stand up to anyone. He did it badly, with his knees unsure of the job, but he did it.

"Cass," Trevor said, "I lied to you for six years. I lied every month when I sent the transfer, because I was ashamed, and I'm sorry. That's mine, not Mom's. And there's one more thing." His voice shook. "You owe my mother an apology. A real one. Not a boundary. Not a guideline. An apology."

Cassidy looked at him as if she had never seen him before. Then she picked up her purse and walked out of my front door without a word, which for Cassidy was the loudest thing she ever said. Bernadette followed her with the lemon bar still in her hand.

Trevor stood alone at the table. He picked up two of the coffee cups, one in each hand, and said in a very small voice, "Mom, can I at least wash these?"

"The cups can wait," I said. "Sit down, son. We are going to talk about why you never told me you were drowning."
