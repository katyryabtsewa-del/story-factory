# Discrepancies

On the third Friday of September, my husband called the whole company into the shop to say something about me, and I had a very good seat.

By then Dale had moved out, Tripp had moved in, and I was doing the payroll from a bucket in the supply room. I had not complained once. I had also not stopped paying attention, which is a different thing, and men like Dale never account for it in a woman who looks like a church secretary.

The meeting was at seven-thirty, in the bay where we keep the trucks, with the big door rolled up and the morning sun coming across the oil stains. Ten people stood around in work boots with their coffee. I stood at the back by the pegboard of pipe fittings, holding my World's Okayest Bookkeeper mug. Dale stood on an upturned bucket, which is how I knew it was going to be a speech, wearing his best golf shirt with the logo on the chest, and Tripp stood beside him with a tablet, looking as if somebody had asked him to hold a baby.

"Folks," Dale said, and he flashed those teeth. "We're entering a new chapter. Big things coming. But before we turn the page, I have to be honest with you about something I've learned. Tripp's team has been going through the books, and we've found some discrepancies."

He let that sit. A couple of the younger techs looked at their boots.

"Cash that can't be accounted for," Dale went on. "I'm not pointing fingers at anybody. I'm just saying, there's a petty cash tin from back in twenty nineteen that came up seventy-one dollars short. Seventy-one dollars. And nobody ever explained it to me." He turned his whole body and looked straight at the back of the room, straight at me, and did a little sad shake of the head. "Now, I'm sure there's a good reason. Somebody who had the keys to that tin, I'd like to hear from them."

I would like to say I felt something dramatic. What I felt was deep professional interest. In thirty-seven years of keeping that man's books, I had never seen him do arithmetic in public, and I wondered how long he had been saving this one.

Nobody said a word. One of the girls from the front desk put her hand over her mouth. I took a sip of coffee, because a woman needs something to do with her face.

Then Ray Beaumont said, "Mister Dale."

He had been standing against the wall with his arms folded. Ray is six feet four inches and moves like a man carrying a glass of water, and when he stepped forward, everyone shuffled to make room. He took a thick old wallet out of his back pocket, one that had curved to the shape of him, and from it he pulled a slip of paper folded and unfolded so many times it was soft as cloth.

"Seventy-one dollars," Ray said. "That's my boots."

"Ray, I'm not saying anybody did anything."

"Steel toes. I'd split the left one on a job in Foley. Miss Mimi wrote me a receipt the same afternoon and put it in the tin so the count would match." He held it out to the room. "She wrote down what it was for, the date, and the amount, and then she took it to you, Mister Dale, and you signed the bottom yourself. Right there. That's your name."

He turned the paper around so everybody could see the big loopy signature at the bottom, and I will say it was a very recognizable signature. It had sold a great many swimming pools.

"I kept it," Ray said, in his soft voice, "because Miss Mimi told me once, a long time ago, to always keep my receipts. She said it's a hard world."

Dale laughed. He has a wonderful laugh, and I have heard him use it to get out of a speeding ticket and, once, a funeral. "Well, there you go, folks," he said. "That's exactly what I love about this team. Everybody paying attention. That's all I wanted to know."

But nobody laughed with him. And Tripp, I noticed, looked at his tablet, and then, for the first time, he looked at me, and he did not look like a man who thought I was difficult.

That was Friday. On Saturday morning I went back to the office to finish the payroll, because I do not hand a person a late paycheck on account of my husband's mood.

The building was empty. The crew was off, Tripp was at the beach, and I set up on my bucket in the supply room with the fan and the calculator and the door open an inch. At ten-fifteen, I heard the front door unlock. I heard golf shoes on the tile and the particular squeak of Dale's chair when he sat down at what had been my desk. I was not ashamed of what I did next, which was to hold very still.

He called Tripp on the speaker. He always puts it on speaker, because he likes to walk around while he talks, as if he were being filmed.

"Dale," said Tripp's voice, a little thin, "I just want to be sure we're clean on the license before the lender's call. They're going to want the paperwork in order."

"It's in order, Tripp. It's paperwork." I could hear him smiling. "The renewal's due Tuesday the thirtieth. Same as every year. Mimi signs it."

"Mimi signs it? I thought the license was in your name."

"Don't worry about the license. It's a form, and she signs where I put the paper. She's a clerk, Tripp. She'll sign anything I put in front of her. Forty-one years, she's never once read the fine print."

There was a pause on the line, and I counted three seconds.

"So we do it in the conference room Tuesday morning," Dale said, easily. "Nine sharp, in front of your Houston people, so the lender sees it done. She signs, you send it off, and Friday we close."

"Okay," said Tripp. "Okay. If you're sure."

"Tripp. Son. I have never been surer of anything."

He hung up. I heard him whistle a little and walk out, and the front door locked behind him with a click.

I sat on my bucket for a long time. Without meaning to, I had lined up all the chlorine tablets on the shelf by size. My hands were quite steady. I had expected to feel hurt, and I did, somewhere underneath, in a part of me I would visit later, but on top of it lay something cool and level, like a pool on a still morning. He had just told me the day, the hour, and the room.

I took a yellow sticky note off the pad, wrote one line on it in my neat bookkeeper's hand, and put it in my purse beside the napkin from the Dockside Diner. It said, Tuesday, nine in the morning.
