# The Spreadsheet

I was carrying a tray of coffee cups past the den when I heard my daughter-in-law put a price on my house. It was the fourteenth of September, the day of my husband Walter's memorial lunch, and the whole house smelled of baked ham and coffee. The den door was half open. Brittany was sitting on the couch with her tablet on her knees, reading aloud in the bright, sing-song voice she saves for waiters.

"Okay, so," Brittany said. "The lake cabin, the jewelry, the house. She won't last five years, and the house alone is three-forty."

My son Kevin laughed, the easy laugh of a man hearing a good weather report. Then my daughter Rachel asked, in a small voice, "What about Grandma's sapphire ring?"

A little over two months later, I invited all three of them to Thanksgiving dinner to hear my will read early. My lawyer read it aloud at my own kitchen table, between the turkey and the pie, and only one sentence in it mattered.

But that is the end of the story, and I was raised to start a recipe at the top.

My name is Diane Whitaker. I was sixty-nine that fall, and I have lived my whole life in Harlan Creek, Ohio, a town small enough that the hardware store knows your furnace filter size. For thirty-one years, Walter and I ran the Blue Kettle, the diner on Main Street. Walter worked the grill, and I worked everything else: the counter, the register, the books, and the pies. People drove in from three counties for my sour cherry pie, though some swore by the buttermilk, and a few stubborn souls still argue for the apple with the cheddar crust. We sold the place eight years ago. It is a coffee shop now, and I wish them well.

Walter had a carpenter's hands, even after all those years at a grill. In nineteen eighty-four he built our kitchen table out of solid oak, and he sanded it so smooth a pie plate would slide from one end to the other without a sound. Every birthday and every hard conversation in our family happened at that table. Walter was a quiet, careful man who wrote everything down in little spiral notebooks, even how many eggs we used the Saturday after the county fair.

He died fourteen months before that lunch, on a hot morning in July. He had taken his coffee out to the porch, and when I went out to ask whether he wanted his eggs scrambled or fried, he was gone. It was his heart. He was seventy-two, and he went quick and quiet, the way he did most things, and I have been grateful for the quiet part and angry about the quick part ever since. His chair still sits at the head of the oak table. Nobody sits in it, and nobody has ever needed to be told.

Tommy came first that morning, the way Tommy always does. He pulled in at eight o'clock with twenty folding chairs from Saint Anne's and had them set up before my first cup of coffee was gone. Tommy is my youngest, thirty-six, and he owns a two-bay repair shop on the edge of town. He doesn't say much, but he noticed I hadn't eaten, and he made me a fried egg sandwich and stood over me until I ate it.

My granddaughter Olivia was already in the kitchen. She was sixteen, Kevin and Brittany's only child, and she had driven down from Columbus the night before to bake with me. Olivia wants to be a chef. That morning she was crimping the edge of a sour cherry pie with her tongue between her teeth, exactly the way I do.

"Grandma, is this tight enough?" Olivia asked.

"Your grandpa would have eaten it with the crust falling off," I told her, and she laughed and kept going.

Rachel drove over from Indianapolis by herself. She is my middle child, forty-one, a dental office manager, divorced the year before, and she hugged me a little too long in the doorway, the way she does when she feels guilty about something she hasn't done yet.

Kevin and Brittany came last, forty minutes late, in a leased black car about the size of my kitchen. Kevin is my oldest. He was forty-four and managed sales at a car dealership in Columbus. He kissed my cheek and said, "Traffic, Mom," in the soft, soothing voice he uses when he wants something or is sorry for something, and I have never been able to tell which. Brittany came in behind him carrying her tablet in a leather case, as if someone might need a spreadsheet at a memorial.

"Mama Di," Brittany said, and kissed the air beside my ear. I have asked her not to call me that. She has decided I was joking.

So that was my family, all under one roof for Walter, and that was how I came to be standing in my own hallway, careful to keep off the board that squeaks, while Brittany answered Rachel's question about my mother's ring.

"Just put your name in the Who column," Brittany said. "That's what it's for."

I counted to ten. Then I pushed the den door open with my hip and walked in smiling. "Who wants coffee?" I asked.

Brittany flipped the tablet face down on her knee so fast it slapped. Kevin jumped up to take the tray. "Mom, sit down," Kevin said. "You've been on your feet all day." Rachel stared at the carpet, and a red flush crept up her neck, the same flush she got at eleven when she broke the bathroom mirror and blamed the cat.

I did not sit down. I handed out cups and straightened Kevin's tie, and my hands did not shake once. I poured coffee for thirty-one years for truck drivers and the mayor on the morning after he lost an election, and I know how to keep a cup steady.

The lake cabin on Brittany's list almost made me laugh, because we do not own one. It belongs to Harold Greene, our family lawyer and Walter's best friend for forty years, and Walter only called it ours because Harold gave him a key in nineteen ninety and never asked for it back. I made a note to tell Harold he had been divided up too.

By four o'clock the neighbors had gone, Tommy was folding chairs on the porch, and Kevin and Brittany were outside arguing about who would drive. I wiped down the kitchen counter, which was already clean, because that is what I do when I am thinking. Then I went to the den for the cups.

Brittany's tablet was lying on the arm of the couch. She had forgotten it, or she had stopped worrying about me, and I still don't know which is worse. As I reached for a saucer, the screen lit up with a message, and behind the message was a spreadsheet. I lifted my reading glasses on the beaded chain Olivia made me and read the title.

It said "Mom," and then a dash, and then "Exit Plan." It was the same kind of dash they carve on a headstone, between the year you were born and the year you died. As far as my daughter-in-law was concerned, the second year was already filled in.
