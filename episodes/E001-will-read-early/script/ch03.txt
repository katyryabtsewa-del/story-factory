# The Man With the Laser Tape

On the Saturday Kevin had promised, the one when he was bringing someone, I was up at six baking a buttermilk pie. Whoever was coming up my driveway, I did not intend to meet them empty-handed.

They pulled in at ten in the big black car, and right behind them came a small white sedan. The man who climbed out of it was about fifty, in a quarter-zip sweater the color of oatmeal and loafers with no socks, in October, in Ohio. He had a little black box clipped to his belt.

"This is my friend," Brittany said. "He knows houses. He just wants to look, Mama Di."

In my experience, a man who knows houses is a man who sells them. He told me his name and shook my hand. I have since chosen to forget his name, and I recommend it.

The little black box turned out to be a laser tape measure. He pointed it at my walls, and a red dot jumped from corner to corner, and he typed numbers into his phone. In the front room he knocked on the plaster and said, "Great bones." In the kitchen he opened my pantry without asking and said it again. On the stairs he bounced on his heels and said it a third time. And in the upstairs bedroom, where Walter and I slept for more than forty years, he aimed his red dot at the wall above our bed and said, "Great bones," for the fourth time.

I was keeping count on my fingers behind my back, the way I used to count pies left in the case. By the fourth one, I wanted to tell him that I had great bones myself, and they were not on the market either.

Afterward we sat at Walter's table with coffee and the buttermilk pie. The man with the laser tape ate two slices and took his coffee out to the porch to make a call. Kevin folded his hands on the table, the way he used to as a boy when he wanted the car keys.

"Mom, I've got an opportunity," Kevin said. "A car wash franchise, brand new, right off the interstate. Cars get dirty, Mom. That's the whole business plan. I just need sixty thousand dollars for my share of the buy-in, and you'd have it back in two years, easy."

I kept the books at the Blue Kettle for thirty-one years. Every kind of salesman in Ohio has sat at my counter, and I know what a man sounds like when he is selling something he has not read.

"What's the franchise fee," I asked, "and what's the royalty they take every month, and is that on the gross or the net?"

Kevin said he would have to check.

"Who owns the land under it," I asked, "and how many years are left on the lease?"

Kevin said the franchise people handled all that.

"And what does one of their car washes clear in a year," I asked, "after payroll, water, and the loan?"

"A lot, Mom," Kevin said. "They showed me a chart."

"Then the answer is no," I said. "I'm sorry, honey. I don't buy anything I can't see the bottom of."

Something went across Kevin's face then, quick and hard, like a door slamming in another part of the house. He set his cup down so hard that coffee jumped onto Walter's table.

"Dad would have said yes," Kevin said.

"Your father would have asked me first," I said. "He always did."

Brittany laughed, a bright little laugh, and put one hand on Kevin's arm and the other into her big leather bag. "Okay! Nobody is deciding anything today," Brittany said. She slid a glossy folder across the table to me. On the front, under the words Maple Grove Senior Living, a white-haired couple was laughing at a salad. "We booked you a tour Thursday, Mama Di. Just to look."

I said yes, and I will be honest about why. Part of it was to keep the peace. The other part was that I wanted to see exactly where they planned to put me.

On Thursday I drove myself to Maple Grove, an hour and a half each way, and I did not get lost once, which I mention because it will matter later. It sat on a four-lane road outside Columbus between a mattress store and a dentist's office, and there was not a maple tree or a grove anywhere in sight. Brittany met me in the lobby with a young woman wearing a lanyard who had the whitest teeth I have ever seen.

The lobby fireplace was really a television screen with a crackling sound coming out of a speaker, and nobody sat near it, because a television does not give off heat. The dining room smelled like a steam table. I ran a diner for thirty-one years, and I can tell gravy from a packet at fifty feet. The menu board by the door said, "Chef's Choice, Salisbury Steak." I did not ask what the chef's other choices had been.

The apartment they showed me had one bedroom, a window facing the parking lot, and a kitchenette with a microwave and a two-burner cooktop that shut itself off after twenty minutes.

"For safety," the young woman said, smiling with all those teeth.

"I have had gravy on the stove longer than that," I told her.

The rent started at five thousand two hundred dollars a month, she said, before what she called the levels of care. Brittany wrote that down on her tablet, and I watched her do it.

While Brittany stepped into the hallway to take a phone call, I sat down in the lounge beside a woman in a lavender cardigan who was working on a jigsaw puzzle of a lighthouse. She had the whole border done and most of the sky.

"They giving you the tour?" the woman asked, without looking up.

"My son booked it," I said. "Just to look."

She fitted a piece of blue into the sky. "My kids booked me a tour too," the woman said. "That was four years ago."

Then she looked at me over her glasses. "The pie here is terrible," she said, "in case you were wondering."

I liked her very much, and I have thought about her often since. I drove home with the radio off and both hands on the wheel.

It was nearly dark when I got back to Linden Street. I set the Maple Grove folder on the counter and moved the fruit bowl to make room, and underneath it I found a sheet of paper folded flat. It had been sitting there since Saturday. I had been eating apples off the top of it for five days.

It was a page printed from a computer, with a photograph of my house taken from the street, my own red geraniums on the porch rail. Underneath the picture it said, "Two fourteen Linden Street. Charming farmhouse, great bones." That made five.

At the top of the page, in big friendly letters, it said, "Coming Soon." And under that, in smaller letters, as if it were only a detail, it said, "Spring." Nobody had asked me what season I would like to leave my house in. They had already picked one.
