# Walter's Letter

The envelope Harold held up at my Thanksgiving table was the same one I had refused to open in his office on Halloween, the one Walter had addressed to me in case the kids ever started circling.

I handed Harold the butter knife from the buttermilk pie, and he slit the envelope open. Inside were three pages torn out of one of Walter's spiral notebooks, with the little paper fringe still along the edge. I swept that fringe off the Blue Kettle counter every night for thirty-one years.

Harold read it in his own dry voice, but I heard every word in Walter's.

"Kids," Walter had written. "If Harold is reading you this, I'm gone, and your mother has had about enough of something. My guess is it's you. I was never any good at speeches, so I wrote it down. Memory gets tired. Paper doesn't."

Tommy laughed, one short wet sound, and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. Nobody had said those words out loud in my house since his father died.

"Kevin," Harold read. "You were six when we got you that red bike, and you wouldn't let me hold the seat. You fell off eleven times in the driveway and got back on eleven times, and you never once cried where your mother could see. I know it was eleven because I wrote it down. I've been proud of that boy every day since. I'd like to go on being proud of him, wherever I am."

Kevin stared at the table with his thumb pressed against the edge of the oak, as if the table might get up and leave.

"Rachel," Harold read. "You were in fourth grade when you made the county spelling bee, and you went out on meringue, of all words, a girl raised in a diner. You spelled it right three times in the car going home, and you cried the whole way. You knew the right answer, honey. You nearly always do. You just have to say it out loud the first time."

Rachel held on to the edge of the table with both hands. Walter could not have known about that group chat, but he had known his daughter.

"Tommy," Harold read. "You were five when you took your mother's toaster apart to find out where the toast came from. You couldn't get it back together, and you cried, and I helped you, and we had one screw left over. We swore we'd never tell your mother. She knew by Monday. By the time you were twelve you could put anything back together, and you've been putting this family back together ever since, mostly when nobody was looking."

I did know about the screw. I found it in Walter's shirt pocket on wash day, and that toaster rattled for nine more years. Tommy put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, and Olivia leaned over and laid her head against his arm.

Then Harold read the part I think about most.

"Your mother fed half this town for thirty-one years," Walter had written, "and she kept the books to the penny. Nothing in this world ever got past her, so if you are hearing this, you have already lost."

Brittany was already on her feet, putting on her coat one sleeve at a time, the way people do when they want everyone to know they are leaving but would still like to hear the end.

"I built that table so there would be room for all of you," Harold read. "You were loud at it, and you were hungry, and you looked after each other, and you were good. Be the people you were at that table. That's all I want. Love, Dad."

"Diane," Harold read from the last page, "the pie is better than mine. It always was."

Walter baked exactly one pie in his whole life, the week I was in the hospital having Rachel, so the house would smell right when I brought her home. It was apple, and the crust could have shingled a roof. For forty years afterward, he told anybody at the counter that his pie was better than mine. It was the only argument he ever refused to lose, and he had waited until he was gone to let me win it. I laughed out loud, and then I cried, and I did not care who saw either one.

Kevin stood up. Brittany took the leather folder off the hutch and held it to her chest like a hymnal.

"I'm going to get a real lawyer," Kevin said to the floor.

"Please do," Harold said. "Have him call me. I'll be fishing, but I'll call him back."

At the front door, Brittany turned around. "Olivia, get your coat," Brittany said.

Olivia looked at me, and before I could answer, Kevin did.

"Leave her, Britt," Kevin said. "She drove herself."

In seventeen years I had never once heard my son tell his wife no. He looked back from the doorway at the letter and the ring and his father's empty chair. "Drive careful tomorrow, kiddo," Kevin said. Then the storm door banged behind them, and Brittany's champagne stayed exactly where she had put it.

Harold let Olivia beat him at gin rummy in the front room, Tommy went out to sit on the porch in the cold, and Rachel rolled up her sleeves and came to the sink.

I washed and Rachel dried, the way we did when she was ten.

"Why aren't you yelling at me?" Rachel finally asked.

"I'm too old to yell," I said, "and you're too old to need it."

She dried the same plate around and around. Then she told me. Since the divorce there had been the lawyer's bills and the rent and the trumpet lessons, and Kevin had said it was for my own good, and she had wanted very badly to believe him.

"When I wrote that I didn't feel good about the doctor thing," Rachel said, "nobody answered for an hour. I sat in my car in the parking lot at work and waited. And when Kevin finally wrote back, I was so glad he'd answered me that I forgot what I'd asked."

"Can you forgive me, Mom?" Rachel asked.

"I expect so," I said. "But forgiveness is a recipe like any other, honey. It takes the time it takes, and if you rush it, it comes out tough."

"How long?" Rachel asked.

"Call me Sunday," I said, "and we'll find out."

After Harold went home and the girls had gone up to bed, Tommy came in off the porch with his ears red from the cold. I sat him down at Walter's table and told him the part Harold had not read aloud. Kevin had written that Tommy gets nothing extra, so the house would go to Tommy, and I would live in it until the day I die.

For a long time Tommy just turned his coffee cup around on the oak.

"I don't want a house, Mom," Tommy said. "I want you around for another thirty years."

"Then you'll get both," I said.

He laughed at that. Then he checked the lock on the back door twice and went home.

That was the end of Thanksgiving. What happened to Kevin over the next six months, I could not have planned if I had tried.
