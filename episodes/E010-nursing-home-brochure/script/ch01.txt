# The Brochure

The gift bag was the color of a robin's egg, with white tissue paper standing up out of the top like a corsage, and I remember thinking that somebody had put real care into it. It was the third of October, a Saturday, and I had just turned seventy. We were at the Elk Horn Supper Club, under the antler chandeliers, and in front of me sat a prime rib I had been looking forward to since Tuesday.

"Open it, Mom," my daughter said. "It's from both of us."

My son-in-law, a big, quiet man in a sport coat that had fit him better a few years ago, did not look up. He was studying his baked potato as if it had asked him a question and he was not sure of the answer.

Under the tissue paper was a glossy brochure. On the cover a silver-haired woman in a cardigan, a lot like mine, laughed at a bowl of soup, and over her head it said Silverleaf Terrace, Welcome Home to the Next Chapter. A yellow sticky flag stuck out of the pages near the back. I opened to it, because that is what the flag was for. Page nine was titled Birch Court, Secure Memory Care, and under that was a table of prices. Folded inside the cover was a single sheet that said Reservation Confirmation, and I did not read it yet, because Renata had started to talk.

"Mom, I only want you safe," she said. "I'm just taking it off your plate. Nobody wants to be doing this in the middle of an emergency."

Two tables over, somebody laughed at something. A waiter was coming toward us with a slice of chocolate cake and a single candle, and when he saw our faces he stopped, turned in a neat half circle, and carried the cake back to the kitchen. I have thought well of that young man ever since.

I will tell you now, so you can stop worrying about me, what I did not know yet. Before Halloween a state inspector would be standing in the lobby of that building, and I would be the reason. I also want to be fair to my daughter. For a while I truly believed she meant well.

My name is Margaret Okerlund, and everybody has called me Peg since the second grade. For thirty-six years I sat at a console at the Gunn Mesa County Communications Center in Alder Bench, Colorado, with a headset on and a cup of cold coffee at my elbow, and I answered the telephone on the worst night of people's lives. On the radio I was Dispatch Four. I had a low, even voice, and I was told more than once that people could follow it the way you follow a rope in the dark. I retired at sixty-four, and four years ago my husband Hank, who ran a grader for the highway department and sang along to the radio a half step flat, died of his heart at sixty-nine, in the garage, with the door open and the Broncos on.

Hank used to tell me to write things down. "Write it down, Peg," he would say, "you'll forget the good parts too." So I carry a small green spiral notebook in the pocket of my cardigan, and before I write anything else on a page, I write the time in the margin. It is the oldest habit I have. A call is only as good as its time.

There was one more habit, and it mattered that night. When somebody tells you something important, you read it back. You say the address and you say the problem, and you let them correct you, so that nobody out there is standing in the wrong driveway.

"Let me read that back," I said, and I laid my fork across my plate. "You've reserved me a room at a place with a locked wing, for my birthday, and you put a flag on the price of the locked wing."

"It's just so you have the option," Renata said. She is forty-four, brisk and tidy, with her father's jaw and her own good reading glasses, and she has run a business called Hearthstone Senior Advisors out of a strip mall in the valley for six years. "Mom, you left the kettle on in August. You said it yourself. You can't keep doing everything alone out there on Willow Bench Road."

I looked at my son-in-law. His ears had gone red. I looked at my daughter, who had her hand flat on the tablecloth, ready to be reasonable at me for as long as it took.

For four years, since Hank, I had let Renata help me with everything, because it was easier than arguing with her, and I will own that. I had let it get all the way to this table, and the chandeliers, and a robin's egg gift bag. It is a hard thing to see your own part in something while you are still sitting in the middle of it.

"Thank you," I said. "That was thoughtful. I would like to see the place properly before I say anything. The brochure says a person can stay for a week."

Renata blinked. She had come ready for tears, or for a fight, and she had a speech for each. She had nothing for a woman who asked for a tour.

"A respite stay," she said slowly. "Well, yes. They do offer that."

"Then I'd like that," I said, and the waiter, brave boy, came back with the cake.

I drove home alone in Hank's old tan Ford Ranger with the brochure on the seat beside me. The aspens along the road had turned and were mostly down, a yellow carpet in the headlights. At my kitchen table, the one with the cracked yellow top, I put the kettle on, which I noticed myself doing, and I unfolded the sheet that had been tucked inside the cover.

Reservation Confirmation. Silverleaf Terrace. Resident, Margaret Okerlund. Reservation deposit, two thousand five hundred dollars, paid in full. At the bottom, in small gray print, it said paid by card ending four-four-one-seven.

I knew those four digits the way I know my own telephone number. I had given that card to my daughter two years ago, so she could pick up my groceries and my prescriptions, and I had not thought about it since.

I sat there with the kettle starting to sing, and I did not get up to take it off the burner. For the first time in two years, I wanted to know exactly where else that card had been.
