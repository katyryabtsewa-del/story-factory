# A Tick Under the R

I walked back up the stairs of Teague Title and Notary with the right glasses on my nose and the face of a tired old woman on the rest of me.

"Found them," I said, and I held up the case like a child with a prize. "Right where I left them. Now I have a confession. I read that page four, and I am worn clean through. It's been a long morning, and I'd like to read the whole stack at my own table, with my own lamp, and sign it when I'm rested. Could we do it Friday?"

Curtis's smile slipped. It was only for half a second, the way a bad seam shows when the fabric shifts, and then it was back in place and as warm as a biscuit. "Josie, hon, of course," he said. "Friday works. The supplement forms are due Friday anyway, so Friday at ten it is. Gordon will hold the originals, and I'll see that you get a copy."

"That's kind of you," I said. I handed the clipboard across the table, and I did not look at the girl at the copier, and she did not look at me. But the machine started humming again, and I thought her shoulders came down about an inch.

Nicole took my arm on the stairs. She was worried and relieved at once, and she wore both on her face, which is a thing only a daughter can do. "Mama, you don't have to apologize for being tired," she said. "I'll drive you home. Curtis can bring the Buick by this evening. I don't like you on ice."

I let her. In her car, the heater smelled of peppermint, and the wipers beat their slow time across the glass. We talked about chicken soup, because it was safe. Nicole said she would bring a pot on Thursday, the same recipe as in November, and that I should eat it with the good bread. I looked at her hands on the steering wheel, at the nails bitten down to the quick, and I thought, she has not slept properly since Christmas, and I wondered what she had been told.

A few words about who I am, since you ought to know whose kitchen you are sitting in.

My name is Josephine Ledbetter, and nearly everyone calls me Josie. I was born in Sparrow Gap and have never lived anywhere else, which people take for a lack of imagination until they see how much a person can notice in one place. For forty-one years I ran Ledbetter Alterations on Depot Street. I took up hems and let out prom dresses at ten o'clock at night, and I sent every one of my brides down the aisle with a spare safety pin in the lining. I closed the shop four years ago, but I still take a few jobs in my sunroom, because my hands do not know how to retire.

My husband Earl was a lineman for the Marlow Electric Cooperative for thirty-eight years. He was a big, patient man who hummed hymns on top of ladders in the sleet, and he called me Needle from the day we met until the January three years ago when he died. When I signed the lease renewal on the shop, I was thirty-one and nervous, and I wrote my name the ordinary way. Earl looked over my shoulder and said, "Needle, a seamstress ought to knot her thread." So I put a tiny tick under the last letter of my name, a short back-stitch, like the knot at the end of a seam. I have signed that way for forty years, on every receipt and every check, and only Earl ever knew the joke behind it.

A tiny tick, and nobody would notice it unless they were looking.

I thought about Curtis the whole ride home. I had been thinking about him in pieces for a month without letting the pieces touch. In early December he had said it was a shame how much junk mail an older person had to wade through, and he had kindly put my mail on forwarding, so it would go to Nicole's house, where he could sort it for me. He had set up a pill organizer on my kitchen counter, seven little doors, for a blood pressure pill and a vitamin that I had taken without help for years. He had put a gadget on my thermostat that he could change from his telephone, and twice the house had gone cold at night and he had called to ask if I was warm enough.

And at church, on the first Sunday of January, in front of the whole fellowship hall, he had laughed and said, "Josie's on Josie time these days, aren't you, hon?" Everyone laughed along with him, kindly. I laughed too. A seamstress knows that you do not tear out a seam in public.

That was the first wave. It was small and friendly, and it came with a casserole dish. Each little kindness, I understood later, had been another pin, holding the fabric where he wanted it.

Nicole kissed my cheek at my door, and I stood in the cold hall until her taillights had gone. Curtis brought the Buick at six, with the keys on the little hook by the back door and a quick, practiced glance at the pill organizer. "Doors look right," he said. "You're doing great, Josie, hon." I thanked him, and I asked him whether he had eaten, and I watched him eat my leftover meat loaf standing at the counter, smiling the whole time.

When he was gone I went into the sunroom, turned on the lamp over the sewing table, and took the sticky note out of my cardigan pocket. I smoothed it flat. Then I pulled a pad toward me and wrote my name the way I have written it for forty years, Josephine A. Ledbetter, with the little tick under the last r. It sat there like a knot. I sat looking at it, and I counted what I knew.

I knew that someone had written a power of attorney with my name on it. I knew that I had not signed it. I knew that a person who signed herself M had risked something to tell me so. I knew Curtis wanted my signature on Friday, and I did not yet know why he needed it so badly. And I knew that the first thing a woman in my position wants to do, which is to pick up the telephone and call her daughter, was the one thing I could not afford to do.

I hemmed a pair of choir robes around midnight, because my hands needed a job.

At seven the next morning, someone knocked on the glass of my sunroom door. It was the young woman from the copier. She wore a gray parka with ice in her hair, and she held a garment bag over her arm like something she had stolen.

"Missus Ledbetter," she said, and her voice shook. "I need a hem."
