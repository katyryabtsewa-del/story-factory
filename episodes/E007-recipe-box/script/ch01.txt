# Dawn at the Dump

At twenty minutes to six on a rainy Tuesday in May, I was standing at the edge of the Larkin County transfer station in my late husband's rubber boots, holding a rake and looking at a hill of other people's garbage the size of a church.

Del Okafor stood beside me with his thermos. Del is fifty-eight years old, spent twenty years in the Navy, and speaks in sentences so short you could mail them with one stamp. He had unlocked the gate for me an hour before the place opened, which was against every regulation he owned.

"Thirty-yard rolloff," he said. "Came in at twenty past five. Dumped on the east side of the floor. Nothing's been pushed since."

"How long do I have?" I asked.

"The loader comes at seven. After that it all goes in the compactor." He handed me the rake and a blue tarp. "That's one hour and twenty minutes, Winnie."

"What am I looking for?"

"You tell me."

So I told him. A green steel tin, about the size of a bread box, dented on one corner, holding two hundred and twelve recipe cards, and I had never cooked a single one of them. Del looked at me for a long moment, and then he looked at the hill, and he did not laugh. That is the main reason I will think well of that man until the day I die.

What I can tell you now, and could not have told you then, is that by Saturday noon thirty people would be standing on my lawn, and not one of them would be there for a house showing.

But you cannot understand why a grown woman climbs a trash heap at dawn over some index cards unless you know what was written on them, so let me start with the route.

My name is Winifred Brandvold, though nobody but my mother ever used all of it. I am seventy years old. For thirty-four years I carried the mail on Route Three out of the Fenmore Post Office, sixty-one miles of gravel and county blacktop and two hundred and forty-odd mailboxes. Other people count sheep when they cannot sleep. I count mailboxes, starting at the post office and working out past the grain elevator, and I have never once made it to the end of the route before I dropped off, which tells you how well it works.

I knew every box, every dog, every porch and who was likely to be sitting on it. I knew whose husband had a bad hip and whose daughter had stopped writing from Phoenix.

And at Christmas, every year, somebody pressed a recipe card into my hand.

It started with a farm wife at mile marker six, in my second December, who said a person who drives sixty-one miles in the snow ought to eat something besides gas station crackers. The next year there were four cards. By the time I retired there were two hundred and twelve, each one written in a different hand, each one with a name and a porch. I kept every one of them in a green steel tin that had belonged to Walt's mother, Ruth, and Walt had cut little tabs from masking tape and labeled the dividers by mile marker.

Now here is the joke, and the whole county knows it. I am the worst cook in Larkin County. I once set off the smoke alarm making toast. Walt used to say that I owned the finest recipe collection in Iowa and the only kitchen where nobody had ever used it. I never cooked from that box. I read it. A card from Hilda Voss in violet ink was Hilda, sitting on her porch with her walker, telling me the rhubarb was early again. A card from the farm wife at mile marker six was a hand I had watched shake a little more every year. The box was the route, folded small enough to keep over my stove.

Walt died fourteen months before that morning, in March, at seventy-two. His heart, in the middle of an ordinary afternoon, in his workshop with the radio on. He taught shop at Larkin County High School, he built things and fixed things, and he hummed while he did it, and he never in forty-three years of marriage said a word to me about money or feelings. I found out how much I missed the humming by how loud the house got without it.

Our son, Greg, lives ninety minutes away in West Des Moines and adjusts insurance claims for a living. He is a gentle man who has never in his life started a fight, and he handled his grief the way he handles a hailstorm, with forms. His wife, Tessa, is forty-one and runs a business called Clear Space by Tessa. She is a certified home organizer, with a neat blond bob, a clipboard, and a label maker she carries in her purse the way other women carry lipstick.

She first came to help in October. She meant it kindly, and I want you to know that, because I did not always manage to know it myself. She stood in my pantry with her hand over her mouth and said, "Winnie, nobody needs eleven cans of cream of mushroom soup." Then she took out nine. She was gone by supper, and she left a label on every shelf.

"I'm just trying to help you let go," she told me at the door, and she squeezed my arm.

I did not know what I was supposed to be letting go of. I still had the house, which was paid off and in my name. I still had the truck, a faded blue Ford that had been Walt's. I still had the box.

On Monday, the day before the dump, a big green dumpster sat in my driveway. Tessa said it was for a few things from the garage and the back bedroom, and she and Greg asked me, very sweetly, whether I might spend the day at Hilda's so they could work without me underfoot. I spent it at Hilda's, eating soup and being told I was thin. When I came home the dumpster was full and the kitchen was spotless, and I went to bed without looking in a single cupboard, because I do not open cupboards for a living.

I woke at four forty the next morning, listening for humming that was not there. I went down to the kitchen for coffee, and at four fifty-five I turned on the light over the stove and looked up at the shelf where the green tin had sat for forty years.

There was a clean square in the dust where it had been, and nothing else. Under the magnet from the Fenmore Feed and Seed on the refrigerator was a slip of paper, a receipt for a thirty-yard rolloff, haul date this morning, destination the Larkin County Transfer Station.

Out the window, down on Elm Row, a pair of red taillights was just turning the corner with my dumpster behind it.
