# Bring a Pen

At nine o'clock on Tuesday morning, the thirtieth of September, I walked into the company conference room on four hours of sleep, with my purse on my arm and a pen in my hand, exactly as instructed.

The conference room is the nicest room in the building, which is not a high bar. It has a long laminated table, eight chairs, a window that looks out on the dolphin's tail, and a framed photograph of Dale shaking hands with a state senator. Somebody had set out a plate of pastries, and nobody had touched them. Two gentlemen from Houston sat on one side of the table in dark suits, with their phones face down and their hands folded, looking like men who wanted this over with before lunch. Tripp sat between them and the door, very pale and very straight. Dale was at the head of the table in his best golf shirt, with his sunglasses pushed up on his hair, as relaxed as a man waiting on a haircut.

Ray Beaumont stood in the doorway behind me with his cap in his hand. He had not been invited, and nobody had told him to go.

"Mimi, honey," Dale said, "there she is. Gentlemen, this is Mimi, who keeps this place running. She's been with me since the beginning."

I said good morning. The older of the two Houston gentlemen nodded politely. I sat down in the chair across from Dale and set my purse in my lap.

Dale had a manila folder in front of him. He opened it, drew out a single page, and slid it across the table to me with two fingers. At the bottom was a line, and under the line, printed small, the words qualifying agent.

"Just the annual renewal," he said, with the big smile. "Same as always. Sign right there, and we'll all get on with our day. Just sign, Mimi, like always."

It was nine-oh-one by the clock over the door.

"I'd be delighted to sign it, Dale," I said, in my best church-supper voice. "I always have. But I wonder if the gentlemen from Houston would like to know what it is I'm signing. It seems only polite, since it's their money."

"Oh, it's nothing," said Dale. "A technicality."

"It's the annual renewal of this company's contractor license," I said, turning to the older man. "It has to be signed by the company's qualifying agent. That's the person who passed the state exam and who answers to the state for the company's work. The company can't sign new jobs or pull new permits without one on file. And I thought you'd want to know, sir, that the person on file is me."

Nobody spoke. Out the window a gull landed on the dolphin's tail.

"The certificate has hung on the wall of the back office since the spring of two thousand thirteen," I went on, "in my name, Marilyn G. Tolliver. Mister Haverty has hung his blazer on a nail right underneath it for four weeks. I don't believe anyone has been unkind about it. I just don't believe anyone read it."

Tripp had gone the color of skim milk. "I assumed," he said, quietly, to nobody, "that Dale was the qualifier."

"I know you did, dear."

The older Houston gentleman turned his head very slowly. "Mister Tolliver, our lender's commitment requires a named qualifier in good standing on the day we close. Is Missus Tolliver your qualifier or isn't she?"

"It's a technicality, gentlemen," Dale said again, and a little of the shine was gone from his voice. "She's family. She does what I tell her. Mimi, sign the paper."

"I will gladly sign a paper," I said. "I just want to sign the right one."

I opened my purse. From it I took the state form Wanda Pickett had given me, with the seal in the corner, and beside it the letter Priya Nair had typed the Wednesday before, and I laid them side by side on the laminated table. I had practiced this the way a woman practices a pie crust, and my hands did not shake.

"This is my written resignation as qualifying agent," I said. "It's effective when I sign it. I'm sorry for the short notice, gentlemen, but I didn't think it would be right to let you wire a great deal of money without knowing."

I looked up at the clock. It said nine-oh-four. I signed my name and the date.

Then I photographed the page with my phone and sent it to a small office above a bakery, where a calm woman had her computer open and a cup of tea beside it. She wrote back in under a minute, a single word. It said, Filed.

"Dale," said the younger Houston gentleman, in a low, even voice, "who's the qualifier now?"

Dale opened his mouth, and no sound came out. I watched him look at the folder, at Tripp, and last of all at me, for the first time in some years.

"Mimi," he said, "you're doing this to hurt me."

"No, Dale." I said it gently, because I meant it gently. "I'm leaving you exactly what you always said I was. A clerk. And clerks can't pull permits." I turned back to the table. "Every open job in this company has its permits pulled and its bills paid. Ray's crews will finish every one, and nobody in this county will lose a pool. You have sixty days for that. After that you will want a qualifier."

The two gentlemen from Houston rose together. The older one buttoned his jacket. "Mister Tolliver," he said, "we'll be pausing this transaction pending verification of the license. Our attorneys will be in touch." He turned to me and nodded. "Ma'am. Thank you for your candor."

They walked out, and Tripp stood last, looking at me with a face I almost felt sorry for. "I should have asked," he said. "I read everything but that."

"Yes, you should have, dear," I said. "Now you know."

He nodded, and left. And then it was only Dale, and me, and Ray in the doorway, and a plate of pastries going stale.

Ray went down the hall and came back in two minutes carrying a cardboard box from the copy room. Inside were my World's Okayest Bookkeeper mug, the box fan with its cord wrapped neatly around its stand, and, on top, the framed state certificate, which he had taken down off the wall without my asking.

"Yes ma'am, Miss Mimi," he said. "That's yours."

I carried the box out to the parking lot with Ray behind me and set it on the hood of Gladys. I heard Dale come out of the building, his golf shoes quick on the pavement. He stopped about ten feet away, his sunglasses slid down onto his nose.

"Fine," he said. His voice was ragged. "Fine. I'll just take the test."

"All right, Dale," I said. "It's Tuesday, November eighteenth. In Montgomery. Bring a pen."
