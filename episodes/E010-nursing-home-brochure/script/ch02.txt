# Card Ending Four-Four-One-Seven

The morning after my seventieth birthday I sat in Lu Banks's kitchen on Cottonwood Street with a gray cat on one knee and a mug of coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. Lu is my oldest friend. I trained her at the console in nineteen ninety, when she was a new hire with a laugh like a screen door, and she has been laughing at me ever since, mostly with love.

I told her about the gift bag and the flag on page nine, and then I put the reservation confirmation on the table between the sugar bowl and a cat.

Lu held it at arm's length with her glasses down her nose. "Four-four-one-seven," she said. "That's your card."

"That's my card."

"Peg, in thirty-six years I never once heard you swear, and I'd like to hear it now."

"I would rather say it plainly," I said. "My daughter has a card of mine that she's been using for two years. She used it for a two thousand five hundred dollar deposit on a room I never agreed to. That's the problem as stated."

"That's a felony in some counties," Lu said cheerfully. "In others it's just Tuesday. Want me to call the sheriff? I still know a few of those boys. Most of them are dead, but I know the rest."

Lu has always made her jokes at the edge of a bad night. We both learned that at the console. You laugh at the right moment, or you carry the call home with you.

I should tell you how it had gotten that far, which is the part I am least proud of. After Hank died, Renata took over my pharmacy, which was kind, because I was tired. Then she took over my mail, because I had let some bills pile up, and then she began to sit in on my appointments, and one day I found out she had been calling my doctor's office to tell the nurse about her concerns. When I asked what concerns, Renata said, "Mom, just things."

In August I put the kettle on and went out to look at the tomatoes the frost was about to take, and I stayed longer than I meant to. Renata, who had stopped by with a casserole, found the kettle empty and black on the burner and the whole kitchen smelling of hot metal. Nothing caught fire. I turned it off myself, and I was embarrassed enough that I let her tell the story however she liked.

Then in September a woman I had never met drove up in a white sedan and said she was a friend of Renata's. She said she was a realtor, and she was only stopping by to look at the light. The light on what, I asked her. "On the house," she said. "It's lovely, the light." She admired my window for ten minutes and left a business card, which I still have.

"That woman was pricing your house," Lu said.

"Maybe. Or maybe she likes light. I don't know yet, and neither do you."

"Peg Okerlund, you are sitting there with your daughter's hand in your purse."

"I am sitting here with a card number," I said. "I don't know why she did it. A call can sound like one thing and be another. I've heard a man scream that he was killing his wife, and what he was doing was trying to get a splinter out of her foot. I want to know what's true before I say what it is."

Lu looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "You want to fight her?"

"No. I want to see what she saw."

So I did three things that Sunday afternoon. First, I called my bank, the nice one in town, and I asked them to cancel the card ending four-four-one-seven and issue me a new one, and a young woman said she would be glad to. They would send the new one to my own mailbox, which for two years had not been mine. I drove to the post office on Monday and filed a change of address with my own name on it. It felt like picking my own coat off the hook.

Second, I called Silverleaf Terrace.

"Silverleaf, where every day is a homecoming," said a bright voice. Then, when I gave my name, the voice lit up like a porch. "Miss Peggy! Gideon Pratt, admissions director. Your daughter has told us so much about you. We are so excited. You're going to love it here. Welcome home."

I had not been home there for a minute and a half, but I said thank you, and I asked about the week's respite stay, and he said it was two hundred fifty dollars a night, one thousand seven hundred fifty for seven nights, and that he would have a Garden Wing room ready for Sunday, October eleventh. He suggested I come at ten in the morning so we could do "a little intake chat." I said that would be fine, and I wrote down the time of the call, ten past three.

Third thing. At five o'clock my telephone rang, and it was Renata, as warm as a heating pad.

"Mom! Gideon called me. I can't tell you how proud I am. You're being so mature about this."

"Thank you, dear," I said.

"You'll see. It's not what you think. It's a home. And you'll have people your own age around, instead of that big quiet house."

"I do like to see things for myself," I said, which was true, and she took it for surrender, which was her right.

I spent the next week doing what a person does before a trip. I paid the electric bill and watered the geraniums. On Saturday night, the tenth, I packed one bag with three cardigans, my good slippers, a paperback, and my pill organizer, and I put the green notebook in my pocket with two sharpened pencils and a spare in case the first two went on strike.

Sunday morning the aspens along Willow Bench Road were bare except for one stubborn gold tree by the cattle guard. I drove Hank's truck twenty minutes out to the edge of town and parked at a beige two-story building.

Out front there was a fountain with a stone heron on top, its beak open as if it had been about to say something, and it was not running. Dead leaves lay in the basin. A banner on the porch said Welcome Home in blue letters, rippling in a good cold breeze, and the automatic doors sighed open when I walked under it.

On the front desk, beside a bowl of hard candy, lay a clipboard with a typed sheet headed Arrivals, Sunday. I read it upside down, the way a dispatcher learns to read everything. The third line said Okerlund, Margaret, Birch Court.

I had booked the Garden Wing. It was nine fifty-two, and nobody had yet shown me either one, and somebody had already typed me a room behind the locked door.
