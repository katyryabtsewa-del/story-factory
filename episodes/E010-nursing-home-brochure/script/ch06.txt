# Odette

On Thursday evening, with a blue folder in my drawer and a number in my notebook, I walked out to Hank's truck, sat in the driver's seat with the heater running, and called Lu.

"Who do we know?" I said.

"You'll have to be more specific, hon. Most of the people we know have been dead since the nineties."

"Someone who can do something. Not a lawyer, not yet. Someone who walks into buildings like that one."

Lu was quiet, and I could hear a cat jump off a counter. Then she said, "Odette Watanabe. From the casserole committee at church. She was a high school counselor for thirty years, and now she volunteers for the state, for the ombudsman program, the one for people in nursing homes. She hands you a granola bar before she says hello."

"Call her," I said. "Saturday morning, if she'll come."

Friday, I played the part of a confused old woman, and I will admit that I enjoyed it more than I should have. At breakfast I told Gideon I could not find my glasses, while they sat on top of my head. He patted my shoulder. "They're right there, Miss Peggy," he said, and I saw something in his face go slack with relief. After that he talked more freely around me. He left a spreadsheet open on his desk. He told the dietary manager, in front of me, that Birch Court had to be filled by the end of December, because the owner's lender had a little list. I wrote down every word in the bathroom.

That night Imelda stopped me in the hall at ten fifteen and pressed a telephone into my hand. On its screen was a page of Cyril's field book. "He wants you to have all of it," she whispered. "He said to tell you he has nine years of measurements and nobody has ever asked." She had photographed every page with his blessing, and Lu sent them on to Odette.

On Saturday morning I signed myself out at the front desk. Under Destination I wrote library, and the receptionist, who did not look up, said, "Have fun." The community room at the Alder Bench Library smells like coffee that has been on the burner since the Carter administration. In the corner, at a table with a plastic tablecloth, sat a woman in round glasses with a canvas tote bag on the chair beside her.

Odette Watanabe stood up and shook my hand with both of hers, and before I had even said my name, she held out a granola bar.

"Lu says you've had a week," she said. "Eat something first."

I ate it. Lu sat in the corner with a crossword, and I told Odette how it had gone. Then I laid the green notebook on the table, and I read it to her, the way I have read thousands of addresses to thousands of people at three in the morning. I read the times, the five words, the clock, and the line that said Mister Pratt turned the form over before he scored it. I read the call lights, eleven, nineteen, and twenty-seven. I read the fall, the call, the ambulance, the stamp at ten twenty, and Renata in the parking lot. Odette wrote it all down with a felt-tip pen, and twice, at the end of a section, she said back to me what she had heard. It was the first time all week that I felt a little less alone.

"First, I'll say what I am," Odette said. "I'm not a lawyer, and I'm not a police officer. I'm a person who walks into buildings and asks questions, and I speak for the residents. Residents have the right to see their own records, the right to refuse to be moved, and the right to complain without anyone punishing them for it. I can take a complaint to the state health department. They can inspect without telling anyone first. If they find enough, they can restrict a facility from taking on new residents."

"Enough of what?" I asked.

"Paper. Dates. A person who was there. Somebody who works there who'll say it, too." She looked at the telephone, at the picture of Cyril's field book, and I saw her face change. "Peg, I have a stack of complaints that say, in effect, he said, she said. You have times, and a ninety-one-year-old surveyor."

"I have an aide who's afraid," I said.

"Then don't push her. Just let her know that I'm here."

On Sunday morning at seven o'clock I walked out to the Silverleaf lot with my coat over my arm, and found a small gray hatchback with its engine running, and Imelda inside it with her scrubs under a parka, drinking from a thermos lid. I tapped on the glass, and she lowered it.

"I know what you're going to ask," she said.

"I'm not going to ask," I said. "I'm going to tell you there's a woman named Odette, and she carries granola bars."

Imelda laughed, one dry breath, and then she stopped. "Eleven years," she said. "I have changed those people. I will say it to anybody with a badge. Tell her yes."

At nine I told Gideon that I had enjoyed my stay so much that I would like to stay through his family care conference on Thursday, and I would pay for the extra four nights, of course. His whole face opened up like a garage door.

"Miss Peggy," he said, "you are going to be so happy here."

That night, at eight, Renata came back, alone, with a paper plate under foil. It was a slice of my birthday cake, which she had frozen, as she would, and thawed for the occasion. She sat on the edge of my bed with the cake on her knees, and she began to cry.

My daughter does not cry. She did not cry at her father's funeral. She cried now in short, furious breaths, and told me about her husband's trucking company, and the bank, and a loan of one hundred ninety thousand dollars with a balloon payment due on the last day of December. The banker would not even sit down with them unless they paid seventy-five hundred dollars in good faith by the thirtieth of October. "Silverleaf pays me when I place somebody," she said. "It's a referral fee, Mom, seven thousand five hundred. It's how the whole business works. Everyone does it. I'm not stealing from you, Mom. I'm only trying to get ahead of it."

I could have written her a check that night. I had two hundred ten thousand dollars in savings. I want you to know that I thought of it.

But she had not asked me for it. She had asked me to sign a folder, and I had in my drawer a sheet of paper that said I could not draw a clock.

"I know," I said, gently, and I held her hand until she stopped.

When she had gone, I opened the notebook and wrote the time in the margin.
