# Let the Record Show

At two minutes to ten on that Thursday morning, two cars pulled into the driveway of my own house, and I sat at the kitchen table with my hands folded and listened to my stepdaughter try to get in.

I heard her boots on the porch steps first, quick and light, and then a man's slower tread behind her. A key went into the lock. I heard it slide in and stop, and then try again, with the faint grinding of a person turning something the wrong way. Through the door a soft voice said, "Is there a timing issue with the lock, Tessa?" and I thought, that is the first thing he has said that I agree with.

I got up and opened the door myself.

Tessa stood on my porch in her Happy Tails jacket with her key in her hand, and she went the color of paper. Behind her stood a tall man in a camel-colored coat, with silver at the temples and a face as smooth as a bank statement. He was unwrapping a peppermint.

"Good morning," I said. "Please come in. Mind the boxes. The kitchen is straight ahead."

Tessa looked at the key, and then at me, and went past me down the hall without a word. Dale Voss paused on the threshold and smiled with real warmth, the smile of a man who expects to be thanked.

"Missus Kessler," he said. "So good of you to see us."

In the kitchen I took my seat behind my machine, which stood at the end of the table on its mended strap with the paper folding down into the tray. I had put out two chairs and a glass of water at each place, because I was raised right. I switched it on. It made the small, dry hum that I had heard five days a week for most of my adult life.

"Before we begin," I said, "I will state for the record who I am. My name is Loretta Kessler. I was the official court reporter for the County Court of Common Pleas for thirty-three years, and I am taking a verbatim record of this meeting. Anyone who remains in this room agrees to be recorded. Anyone who objects may leave now."

My voice shook on the first sentence. By the third it had stopped. It sounded like me.

Voss waved his peppermint. "Whatever makes you comfortable, Missus Kessler."

Tessa was staring at the machine. "Loretta, what is this?"

"A meeting about the sale," I said. "You are welcome to stay or go."

She sat down.

I put my fingers on the keys. "Please state your name and position."

"Dale Voss. Senior funding partner at Lakeshore Bridge Funding."

"Does Lakeshore hold a home-equity loan of one hundred eighty thousand dollars against the property at eighteen Birchwood Lane?"

"We do, in the name of Raymond Kessler. The balance, with fees, is one hundred ninety-one thousand." He glanced at Tessa, almost kindly. "There's a notice of default outstanding."

"Mister Voss, this house has been owned jointly by my husband and me for nineteen years, as husband and wife, which means that when he died it became mine in full. My name is on the deed. I have never signed any paper for Lakeshore. My husband's name appears on your agreement, and I will tell you, as someone who has looked at a great many signatures, that the R is wrong. He never made a loop, and he never signed in blue ink. I have a letter from the bank on Water Street that says the same."

I heard Tessa stop breathing. She did not look at Voss. She looked at me, and what I saw in her face was not guilt yet. It was fear, with a child's face under it.

Voss did not stop smiling. He rolled the peppermint to the other side of his mouth. It is the look of a man certain that nothing he says next will matter, which is exactly when people say everything.

"Missus Kessler," he said, "with respect, I'm sure we can sort this out. A signature question is a timing issue. We do the title review after funding, not before, and honestly our notary handles a high volume. We don't verify every signature."

"Then who verified the signature on this one?"

He laughed, lightly. "Well, nobody, in the sense you mean. We lend on the house. Who signs is between the family. That's the whole business model, if you want the truth. People in a hurry don't want questions." He lifted a hand toward Tessa in a gesture of regret. "Your stepdaughter was in a hurry."

The kitchen was quiet except for the machine, still whispering as I wrote.

Tessa moved first. She stood up so fast her chair went over behind her. Her voice came out high and cracked, and it was aimed at me, not at him. "You went through my father's things. You set this up. You had me come here so you could, so you could." She could not finish. Her hands were curled into fists inside the sleeves of her jacket, and I thought, she is nineteen again, at her mother's grave.

"I invited you to a meeting about the sale," I said, very gently. "The record shows that I said so. Please sit down, Tessa. I am not finished."

She did not sit. But she did not leave.

I took the long white paper in both hands and read it back, in the flat, even voice I had used in court ten thousand times. "Question. Who verified the signature? Answer. Nobody, in the sense you mean. We lend on the house. Who signs is between the family. That's the whole business model. People in a hurry don't want questions."

I laid the paper on the table. Voss was no longer smiling. For the first time he looked like a man who had walked into a familiar room and found the furniture moved.

"I think," he said, "that I would like to consult counsel."

"Of course," I said. "Let the record show that Mister Voss left the meeting at ten forty-one."

He took his card from the table and buttoned the camel coat. At the doorway he looked at Tessa, and then at the machine, and then he walked down the hall. The front door closed behind him with a soft and perfectly polite click. A peppermint wrapper lay on my table, folded very small.

Tessa had not moved. She stood with her back against the refrigerator and her arms wrapped around herself.

I took the cream envelope out of my bag and slid it across the table. It stopped in front of her place.

"He wrote this for you," I said. "It was in the box at the bank. I have not read it, and I will not."

She looked at her name in her father's square capitals and did not touch it.

I left the machine humming, walked out of my own kitchen into the hallway, and closed the door behind me. I stood with my back against the wall, the way I used to stand outside a jury room.

Through the door, I heard a chair scrape. Then the sound of paper, the slow tearing of the seal, and the long rustle of a page being unfolded.

After that, I heard nothing at all, for a very long time.
