# The Quiet

On Wednesday afternoon my son and his whole family were forty minutes away in a heated pool, and I was alone at the lake house with a twenty-two-pound turkey and three beds I had made for nobody. I had been told I would love the quiet. I was finding out what a quiet house sounds like when only one person is in it.

I put the bird in a bucket of brine, the way Frank always did, and set the bucket on the back porch, where November keeps things just above freezing. On the kitchen counter, propped against the fruit bowl, sat Craig's old tablet with a charging cord running to the outlet behind the bread box. I assumed it was the one he'd left last summer, and that somebody had plugged it in and forgotten it. I moved the cider out of its way and thought no more about it, which I would like the record to show was my one real mistake of the week.

Then I made the beds. I put the good sheets on the big front bedroom, which had been mine and Frank's and was now just mine, and the flannel set on the small room where Craig slept as a boy. Last, I made up the room at the top of the stairs, where Tyler and Brooke sleep, under a ceiling of glow-in-the-dark stars Craig stuck up the summer after his divorce. Nine still glowed when I turned off the light, which was nine more than I expected.
By four o'clock the sun had dropped behind the ridge and the cove had turned the color of an old spoon. I lit the wood stove and sat down in Frank's chair, and my phone buzzed on the arm. It was the family group chat, which Brooke named Ellery Family Feast two Thanksgivings ago.

The first picture was the pool at Pine Ledge Lodge, with a glass wall and steam rising off the water. The second was Dana in a white robe, smiling the wide, glossy smile of a woman who has been photographed professionally. Tyler sent a single word, "Sick," because Tyler has never sent a second word when a first one will do. Then Craig wrote, "Mom! Miss you. Pool is awesome, you'd love it."

I know a sentence that is meant to close a door. I typed, "Glad you're warm," and put the phone face down.

Around five, I carried a cup of tea out to the screened porch. Across the cove, the window in Amos Thibodeau's kitchen was already yellow. Amos is seventy-four, and he wears a plaid hat with earflaps in every season, including July. He was out on his dock in a red barn coat, and when he saw me he lifted one hand off the bucket and held it up. That is how men in Maine say hello across the water: one hand, no wave.

That night I ate soup standing at the counter, because I had forgotten how to sit down for supper alone. I checked my own pulse out of habit, against the clock over the sink. Sixty-four. Fine. Somewhere out on the ice a fox barked twice, and I caught myself listening for a car door.

Thursday morning I did what I always do when I am hurt. I cooked.

I started at seven. I rinsed the bird, patted it dry with a dish towel I will never see again, and got it into the oven. Then came the rest of it: stuffing with onions and celery and a lot of sage, because my mother-in-law believed sage was a moral requirement, potatoes mashed with the skins left on, cranberry sauce slid onto a saucer with the ridges still in it, and a green bean casserole made of two cans and a stubborn sense of tradition. The pies were from the Harrow market, in plastic domes. Pies were Frank's department, and Frank is gone, so we buy them.

When the turkey came out at a quarter past twelve, the whole house smelled like a place where people were expected. I set the kitchen table for four. Then for three. Then for two. Then I took the extra forks back to the drawer and set it for one, because a heated pool was not going to undo my record.

At one thirty I fixed a plate for Amos, dark meat, because he likes it and has never once admitted that, with stuffing, potatoes, cranberry, green beans and a roll. I wrapped it in foil, put on my navy fleece, and carried it up the shore path, a quarter mile through birches and along a stone wall Frank rebuilt the year Brooke was born.

Amos's house is a small white cape with a green door and a swing set in the side yard. His granddaughter Jenna and her husband had moved in with him in September, after the rent went up in town, and they had taken the two little ones to the husband's mother down the coast for the day. Amos answered the door in his stocking feet with the hat in one hand.

"Frank would've made you a pie," he said.

"He would have, Amos. And he would have told me it was easy."

"Mine's worse." He stepped back to let me in. "Sit down, Dot. You look like you walked up here on purpose."

I sat at his kitchen table, which has a vinyl cloth printed with little red lobsters. He ate a forkful of turkey, slowly, and nodded to himself.

"Frank and I had that cove between us thirty-some years," he said. "He used to say it was one piece of water with two deeds. The day he finished your dock, he came over with a paper he'd written out on the back of a boat invoice. If you ever sold, I got first look. I said, Frank, she'll never sell. He said, that's what I'm counting on."

I put my fork down. "I never saw a paper."

"It's in the deed, too. He liked a clause." Amos turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the lobster cloth. "I never asked him for the cove. A man doesn't ask." Then, in the same slow voice, "Your boy came by Tuesday."

"Craig?"

"Stood out by the road with his phone for a good while, taking pictures of your house. I walked down and asked what he was looking for. He said, 'Just the view, Amos. It's going to be great for the family.' I didn't ask him twice. I never ask anybody anything twice."

Something cold went down the back of my neck and stayed there.

I walked home in the last of the light and turned on the lamp over the sink. The tablet sat where I had left it against the fruit bowl, and as I reached past it for the switch, the screen woke up by itself. A small bell chimed, and a white box slid down from the top of the glass.
