# Brochures and a Label Maker

After the day of the white canisters I told myself I had a few weeks of peace coming, and I got exactly seven days.

On the first Saturday of May I woke up at five, as I have most of my life, and walked out across the wet grass to Walt's workshop with my coffee. It is a converted machine shed with a pegboard wall where every tool hangs inside its own painted outline, because Walt could not abide a chisel that did not know where home was. I opened the door and flipped on the lights, and stood there.

Every tool had a little paper tag tied to it with string. They were small and neat, and they were printed by a machine. Table saw, fifteen hundred. Planer, four hundred. Lathe, nine hundred. Band saw, fifty. Walt's father's brace and bit, one hundred twenty. On the lathe, someone had added a yellow sticky note that said Sold, pickup Tuesday.

I stood in my husband's shop and read the price of his life one tag at a time, and I remember thinking, quite clearly, that nobody had asked me.

Marnie came at nine with Greg. She found me at the kitchen table, and she saw my face and sat down across from me and folded her hands.

"I need to explain," she said. "I listed some of Walt's equipment last night. On an online marketplace. It's all just sitting there, Bernie, and you can't use any of it, and there are people who would give those tools a good home."

"They already have a home," I said.

"The lathe sold in an hour. A man wants the table saw too. And the red toolbox."

My stomach turned over. The red steel toolbox with the brass padlock had been Walt's father's, and it sat under the bench with the padlock closed for as long as I had known him. I had never once seen it open. I had assumed it held old drill bits. "That's not for sale," I said.

"It's locked, so I put no key, sold as is." She held up a hand. "A man from Ames wants it for his shop. Three hundred dollars, forty down, and he'll pick it up on the fourteenth with a trailer. It's just a listing, Bernie. If you don't like it, we can talk."

"I don't like it," I said. "I am not selling Walt's tools, and I am not selling that box. That is a no. I'd like you to take the tags off."

Marnie nodded as if I had given her a very interesting piece of feedback. "I hear you," she said. "Let's give it a few days."

That is how it went. I said no, and she heard it like a weather report, something to plan around. Greg stood in the kitchen doorway with the coffee pot still in his hand, and when I looked at him he said, "Mom, let's just look at it."

It is not a bad sentence. It is only that for Greg it has always meant the first step toward doing whatever Marnie had already decided.

That afternoon the brochures arrived. I found them in my own mailbox, which tells you how sneaky it was, because I can spot a piece of mail from three counties away and this one had been hand-delivered. Fieldstone Terrace, said the cover, in a script like a wedding invitation. Active Living, No Stairs, No Yard. On the front a silver-haired couple laughed over a salad.

I read all eight pages standing at the box. There was a two-bedroom unit with a patio the size of a bath mat, and a clubhouse. Somebody had put a yellow sticky note on page six, in Marnie's hand, that said This one is perfect for you.

The next evening they took me to West Des Moines for dinner. Their house was clean in a way I can only describe as ironed. The refrigerator had labeled shelves, and there was not one object in the living room that was not either useful or on its way out. I ate a chicken breast with nothing on it and admired a vase. Midway through dessert Greg slid a brochure across the table, face up, and said, "Mom, just look at it. We could drive over one Saturday. No pressure."

"No," I said.

It was my second no in two days, and I said it the way I used to tell a customer that a package could not be left in the rain. Pleasantly, and with no room to argue.

"Of course," said Marnie. "That's fine. It's only information."

Then I made my small mistake in the other direction. I tried to be friendly. I mentioned that I had a stack of old Fenmore Gazettes on the porch, because the paper's last twenty years of obituaries were in there and I liked to look people up. I thought it would make her laugh.

Marnie put down her fork.

Her face went white around the nose, a color I knew from the route, from people who have just opened a letter. "A stack of newspapers," she said, "on a porch. You really shouldn't, Bernie. Things like that, they get so heavy, they just fall." She stopped. Her hand had gone flat on the table. "My mother kept things," she said, very quietly, and then she looked at Greg.

"Marnie," Greg said.

She shook her head and picked up her napkin, and the moment closed like a screen door. Within a minute she was passing me the carrots. I noticed it, the way I would notice a dog lying very still behind a fence, and I thought of Walt beside the woodstove, and still I could not see the shape of it.

I was wrong about that, and I would stay wrong about it for nine more days.

The rest of that week was quiet in the way a field is quiet before hail. The tags stayed on the tools, though I did phone the number on the lathe note and tell the buyer that the lathe was not for sale, and he took it better than I had any right to expect. The man from Ames messaged Marnie and said the fourteenth was still good. On Friday evening I sat on my front porch in the dusk, listening to the peepers start up in the ditch, and Greg came out and lowered himself onto the step beside me. He had driven out alone. He kept looking at his hands.

"Mom," he said, "I need to tell you something and I need you not to be mad."

I waited. You get good at waiting, on a route.

"I signed a paper today," my son said. "For the house. So we could talk about it."
