# Playing Along

On the third of November, four days after Olivia drove down in the dark with that iPad, I climbed the stairs to Harold's office again and signed the change to my trust.

Harold had the amendment typed on paper every bit as heavy as the Columbus lawyers had used. His secretary and the young man who does his filing sat on either side of me as witnesses, and a notary walked over from the bank with her seal. I signed with the heavy old pen from the Blue Kettle register. When I came to the one sentence on page two, I read it to myself one more time before I put my initials beside it. Then the notary pressed her seal into the last page, and it made a sound like somebody cracking a walnut.

Harold put the originals in his safe, beside Walter's envelope. Then he asked whether I had an answer to his question yet.

"I do," I said. "Keep the fourth Thursday of this month free, Harold, and bring your reading glasses."

The next morning I took my test. My doctor gave me the longest one she had, the way I had asked, and it took most of two hours. I drew a clock with all twelve numbers and set the hands at ten past eleven. I remembered five words, and then remembered them again twenty minutes later. I counted backward from one hundred by sevens, which for a woman who made change at a diner register for thirty-one years is like being asked to walk downhill.

She called me herself the next Wednesday.

"Diane," my doctor said, "it's completely normal. Better than normal. You are sharper than most of my forty-year-olds."

She put that in a letter and signed it. I drove one copy straight over to Harold, and I put the other in my purse, next to the printout of Brittany's phone call, so the two of them could keep each other company.

Then I started playing along.

I called Kevin the next night. I had thought hard about what to say, because I was raised not to lie, and I did not intend to start at sixty-nine.

"Kevin," I said, "I've been thinking about the papers."

That was true. I had thought about very little else.

"Mom, that's great," Kevin said, and I could hear him sit up straight. "That's really great."

"I'd like the whole family together at Thanksgiving," I said, "so we can talk it all through at once. All of you, and Tommy too."

"Tommy doesn't really need to be part of this, Mom," Kevin said.

"Tommy has come to Thanksgiving every year of his life," I said. "He'll be there."

"Okay," Kevin said. "Sure. Okay."

"And I had another idea," I said, as sweet as buttermilk pie. "Since everybody seems so interested in my will, I've asked Harold to come to dinner and read it to all of us. That way there are no surprises later."

There was a short silence. Then Kevin laughed, the big relieved laugh I imagine he saves for the moment a customer finally signs.

"Mom, that's a great idea," Kevin said. "That's really mature of you."

I have been called a great many things in sixty-nine years, but that was the first time my own son ever called me mature. Kevin assumed the papers said what most papers say: three children, three equal shares, and nothing to worry about. Nobody had told him otherwise. Then again, he had never asked.

Then Brittany came on the line.

"Mama Di," Brittany said, "Kevin just told me, and I think it's wonderful."

I picked up a pencil and made a mark on the back of an envelope.

"We'll bring everything," Brittany said. "Kevin will bring the papers, so you can sign them after pie, Mama Di, and then nobody has to worry about a thing."

That was two marks.

"After pie," I said. "That sounds about right."

"And don't you go to any trouble for us, Mama Di," Brittany said. "Not at your age."

That was three. I pressed so hard on the third mark that the pencil point broke.

When we hung up, I telephoned Harold and told him Kevin was bringing the power of attorney to Thanksgiving, for me to sign over dessert.

There was a pause on the line. "I'm sorry, Diane," Harold said quietly. "I know you hoped he wouldn't."

"So did I," I said.

Rachel called on Sunday. After ten minutes about the weather, she asked her real question, in the careless voice she used at fourteen to ask what time I would be home.

"Kevin says Mister Greene is coming to Thanksgiving," Rachel said. "Is he really coming? Like, actually coming?"

"He is," I said. "I've counted him in for pie."

"Okay," Rachel said. "That's nice. I was just wondering."

In all of human history, nobody has ever just been wondering.

"Rachel," I said, "don't forget your grandmother's ring."

There was a pause. "I won't, Mom," Rachel said.

The Saturday before Thanksgiving, Olivia drove down, and the two of us planned the dinner the way we used to plan the Blue Kettle's Thanksgiving special, on green order pads with carbon paper. I still keep a stack of them in the drawer by the stove. We wrote one ticket for every dish, and she hung each one with a clothespin on a string over the stove, like the ticket rail above his grill.

One ticket said, "Bird, twenty-two pounds, in at seven." Another said, "Mashed, double butter, no lumps or else." There was one for Walter's sage stuffing, one for green beans with bacon, one for sweet potatoes, and three for pies: sour cherry, buttermilk, and the apple with the cheddar crust. At the bottom of the last pie ticket, Olivia wrote, "Table of seven, plus Grandpa's chair." Rachel's boy would be with his father that year.

While she clipped up the sweet potatoes, I told her as much as I thought she ought to carry. Mister Greene would read some papers at Thanksgiving, I said, and some of what she had shown me might come up at that table.

Olivia went still with a clothespin in her hand. "Will they know it was me?"

"Only if you decide to tell them," I said. "That's your choice and nobody else's. You can be in the room or out of it, and either way I'll be proud of you."

She thought about it. "I want to be in the room," Olivia said.

"Then you'll be in the room," I said, "and you won't be standing in it alone."

I did not tell her the rest. Some news a girl ought to hear from a lawyer in a bow tie.

She drove home before dark, and I stood under that string of tickets for a while. At ten o'clock that night, my phone buzzed on the kitchen table.

It was Olivia. She had written, "I wasn't looking, Grandma, I promise. It popped up while I was watching a pie crust video." Under that was a picture of the iPad screen, taken with her own phone, the way she had watched me do it.

Brittany had written, "Champagne's on me. Thanksgiving is payday."

I read it twice, and then I set the phone face down on Walter's table. Brittany was right about that much. Thanksgiving was going to be payday.
