# The Boiler Thing

An hour out of Fort Wayne, I was driving north through blowing snow toward a building where nobody had asked for me, with a doorman on the speaker of my phone and a pipe wrench sliding around in the trunk.

The interstate was a gray ribbon with white smoke crossing it. The wind came off the open fields in long, low sheets, and every few miles it threw a handful of snow across the windshield, so that for a second I was driving blind. I kept it at fifty and stayed behind the tail lights of a freight truck, whose driver clearly knew the road better than I did. Isaiah had put me on the speaker because I asked him to. He talked, and I listened, and between us we kept the night company.

"Dmitri's got the cover off the float chamber," he told me. "He says there's something in it like black pudding."

"That's sludge," I said. "Years of it. It sits in the bottom and makes the float stick, so the cutoff thinks the water's low when it isn't. Or it thinks it's fine when it isn't, which is worse. Tell him not to poke at it with a screwdriver."

"He's using a spoon, ma'am."

"A spoon is fine."

I should explain that a low-water cutoff is a small, humble device that sits on the side of a boiler, and its whole job is to shut the fire off if the water gets too low. A boiler with no water and a burner still going is a bomb with a gas bill. So the cutoff is the one part you never argue with. But it can be fooled, and that is the part I was trying to teach on the phone, because I knew something Isaiah did not. I knew exactly how it got that dirty. It got that dirty the way a gutter does, from years of nobody looking up.

I had seen it for the first time on Friday, the fourteenth of November. I had driven to Chicago to spend the weekend with my son and his wife, which was the first time in a year, and I had come up the stairs of the Ostrander with my overnight bag already knowing something was wrong, because the whole building was talking. The radiators in four-B knocked and clanged all evening like a man hammering from inside the wall, and the north stairwell was cold enough to see my breath. There was a smell in the air that every boiler operator knows, the smell of scorched dust, which is the smell of a system that has been asked to do more than it can.

That night I took Neil's spare keys and went down to the basement, and I found the boiler room door propped open with a bucket. That is the first thing I'd have written up, back in my own plant.

Inside I met Dmitri Lazar, who was twenty-seven years old, polite, and visibly frightened of me, and Isaiah, who had brought him a cup of coffee and then, seeing me, brought me one as well. Isaiah wrote my number on the back of his hand, the way he writes down everything, and he called me Missus Rudd, which I told him was unnecessary and which he has kept doing ever since.

I looked at the gauges for a long time without touching any of them. The glass on the water column was dirty enough that you could not honestly read it. The condensate tank, which catches the water that comes back as steam cools, was overflowing across the floor and smelled like a pond in August. I told Dmitri to blow down the cutoff every week and check the feed pump, and he wrote it all down on the back of a feed-pump receipt, along with his own phone number, which he pressed on me as though I might need to report him.

At Neil's desk that night I wrote a letter to the condominium board, two pages, with photographs I had taken on my phone and a short recommendation at the end. I won't say it was gentle. It was accurate.

Vanessa read it at breakfast, over her coffee, before I'd even finished my toast. She is thirty-eight, tall, and always looks as though she's just been ironed. She did not raise her voice, because she never does. "Constance," she said, "with respect, you're a guest. I'd appreciate it if you didn't do the boiler thing."

Her phrase for the whole business was the boiler thing. I want you to remember that, because I did.

The board met at ten o'clock that same Saturday morning, in the big party room off the lobby that they call the Gallery. There were folding chairs and a table at the front, and I stood up in the open comment period with my two pages and spoke for two minutes. I said that the plant had one working boiler and that the working boiler had a dirty safety. I said the fix was cheap now and would be very expensive in a cold snap.

Vanessa was sitting at the head table, as treasurer, and she smiled at me. "Thank you," she said to the room. "Missus Rudd is my mother-in-law. She is a retired hospital engineer visiting for the weekend. The engineering report speaks for itself."

There was mild laughter, the kind that is meant kindly and still stings, and I sat down. Right afterward the board passed Vanessa's motion to put off the capital plan until the second quarter, four votes to one. The one was an old woman in the second row with a cane hooked over her chair, Esther Bloom, who said no in a voice like a chalk line. Then she turned all the way around in her seat and looked at me, a long, level look that said, I heard you, even if they didn't.

On Sunday, in the elevator, with Neil standing between us studying the floor numbers, Vanessa said, "You made me look like I can't run my own building in front of my own board."

I said I'd done no such thing. Neil said nothing at all, which I understood was his answer, and then the doors opened on the lobby and I went and got my bag. By noon I was on the road home, and I did not turn on the radio.

Now I was on the Skyway, which is the high, windy bridge that carries the traffic over the south end of Chicago, and the lights of the city were laid out ahead of me, orange and cold. My phone lit up on the dashboard, and it wasn't Isaiah. It was the number from that receipt, which I had saved under his first name.

"Ma'am?" It was Dmitri, in a whisper, as though he were hiding in a closet. "Ma'am, I'm sorry. I cleaned the float chamber like you said, and I relit it, and it ran twenty minutes and locked out again. That's four."
