# Fine Print

On the Tuesday after the tire shop, the young man my husband had sent to take over my office walked in carrying a leather bag and a coffee that cost more than my lunch, and the only thing I could think about was a bag of chocolate peanuts.

I should explain that. Boyd had spent the weekend moving out, which took him two suitcases, a foam roller, and a framed photograph of himself shaking hands with a state senator. He did not say goodbye. He sent a text message with a smiling face on it. So I had two days of quiet in a house with the good towels still in the closet, and I used them to remember the spring of two thousand thirteen.

That was the year a state inspector came through the shop on a routine visit and discovered that our contractor license was no good. It was held in the name of Gene Arceneaux, Boyd's old partner, who had retired to a fishing camp on the Tensaw River, and the state wanted a licensed qualifying agent on file, a living person who had passed the exam and who answered for the company's work. I remember the inspector's clipboard, and I remember Boyd's face, which went the color of a boiled shrimp.

"I don't test well, Mimi," he said, in the parking lot, once the inspector had left. "You do it. You're the smart one."

I was fifty-two years old and had not taken a test since high school. I studied at the kitchen table every night for four months, with flash cards and a highlighter, and Boyd watched television in the next room and called out, "How's it going, professor?" on his way to the refrigerator. The test was given in a testing center in Montgomery, in a room full of twenty-eight-year-olds in baseball caps, and I brought a bag of chocolate peanuts in my purse for courage, which the proctor made me leave in a locker. I passed on my first try. When I came home, Boyd kissed my forehead and said I was a lifesaver, and the state certificate came in the mail, and I hung it on the wall of the back office myself. He never asked what it said. He said it was just paperwork.

I should be honest about Boyd, because he was not always this way. In nineteen eighty-nine he built Nettie Rhodes her pool himself, with a borrowed backhoe, and charged her little more than cost because she had once kept our daughter from failing algebra. At our twenty-fifth anniversary supper he cried into his napkin and told the whole restaurant I was the best thing he ever signed for. I believed every word, and I think he did too, which is the trouble with a charming man.

Which brings me back to Tuesday, September second, at seven-fifty in the morning, and Mister Tripp Haverty.

He was thirty-four years old, slim as a pool cue, with a haircut that looked as if it had its own appointment book. He shook my hand with both of his and said, "Missus Lanier, I want you to know how much I respect what you've built here."

"That's kind of you, Mister Haverty," I said. "Boyd tells me you're here to look at the books."

"And, ah, to make a few space adjustments." He glanced around my office. "Boyd suggested that I'd have better light in here. And that you might be more comfortable in the supply room, just for the transition."

I looked at him for a while. The supply room was a windowless closet of chlorine tablets, spare pump housings, and a shelf of unclaimed customer umbrellas. Tripp had the grace to go a little pink.

"Why, I'd be happy to," I said.

I want you to understand that I did not say it through my teeth. I had made up my mind over the weekend, in my kitchen, with my third cup of coffee, that Boyd Lanier wanted to see what the company looked like without me, and I was going to be as helpful as a woman can be. I took Tripp to the filing cabinet and showed him the system. I showed him the color-coded tabs, the accounts payable binder, the aging report on customers who paid late, the passwords, which I had written out on an index card in a neat hand, and the key to the cabinet where we keep the insurance certificates. I told him which supplier would give him a discount if he called before ten in the morning and which one would not, and why. I told him the name of every customer who would need a gentle voice on the phone.

By ten o'clock he was staring at me with the look of a man who has braced himself against a door and had it opened from the other side.

"Missus Lanier," he said, "I was told you might be, um, difficult about this."

"Mister Haverty," I said, "I have never obstructed a man in his work in thirty-seven years. I wouldn't know how."

Nobody in that building was as calm as I was, which is saying something, because Otis Beaumont came to the office door around noon with his Cypress Point Mudhens cap in both hands. Otis is fifty-nine years old and built like a refrigerator, and he has fixed more pumps than anyone in the county, and he has never once raised his voice at a customer or a machine. He had heard. Everybody in that shop had heard, in the way people hear things in a business with eleven employees and one coffee pot.

"Miss Mimi," he said softly, "you want me to say something to somebody?"

"No, Otis. Not yet."

"Yes ma'am," he said, and he stood there another moment, turning the cap around, and then he went and carried the box fan to the supply room for me without being asked. He plugged it into an extension cord and set it on a stack of chlorine buckets, and it turned its slow head across my new office as if it too had been reassigned. I sat down on an upside-down bucket. Through the open door I could see my old desk, and Tripp's blazer hanging on the nail right underneath the framed certificate, and Tripp not reading it.

That was the thing I kept noticing that first day. Nobody read anything. Tripp read the spreadsheets, because those were numbers. Boyd read the sales reports, because those were flattering. But nobody read the walls.

Around four o'clock, the phone rang at my old desk, and I heard Tripp answer it in his smooth voice. "Lanier Blue Water, this is Tripp, how may I direct your call?"

There was a pause. I could hear a particular voice come out of that receiver all the way across the hall, because I have known that voice since nineteen eighty-nine, and it carries like a church bell.

"Young man," said Nettie Rhodes, "I don't know who you are. Put Mimi on. And tell Boyd Lanier I'll wait."
