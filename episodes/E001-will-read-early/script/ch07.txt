# Harold's Office

The morning after Olivia showed me Operation Sunset, I sent her back up the highway at six with a thermos of cocoa, and by nine o'clock I was climbing the stairs to Harold Greene's office.

Harold has practiced law above the hardware store on Main Street for as long as I can remember, and the stairway smells of birdseed and fresh-cut keys. He was waiting at the top. He is seventy-four, tall and thin and unhurried, and he has worn a bow tie every working day of his life. It was Halloween, so this one was orange.

"Diane," Harold said, and took both my hands. "You've brought a bag. Is it pie, or is it trouble?"

"Both," I said.

One whole wall of his office is covered with photographs of Harold and Walter holding up fish. In the middle is one of Walter laughing so hard at something Harold said that he nearly dropped a bass back into the lake. I have never asked what Harold said. Some things belong to the men in the boat.

Harold poured me a cup of his coffee, which has been terrible for forty years. Then I laid everything out on his desk: Walter's spiral notebook with every dot counted, the realtor's listing, the law firm's letter, the doctor's printout, my green notebook, and last of all my phone, with forty-one photographs of a group chat called Operation Sunset.

Harold read every page without a word. Then he took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"You should know there's a lake cabin on Brittany's spreadsheet," I said. "I'm afraid you've been divided up too."

"Who got it?" Harold asked.

"I didn't see that column," I said.

"Well, whoever it is," Harold said, "tell them the roof leaks over the bunk beds, and the mice have a lease."

I laughed out loud for the first time since Thursday.

Then Harold folded his hands on the desk. "Diane, do you remember what you and Walter signed in this office six years ago?"

"The trust," I said. "After his first heart scare."

"The Whitaker Family Trust," Harold said. "Your children call it your will, and I doubt they have ever read it. I want you to know it better than they do."

He explained it the way Walter used to explain the grill to a new cook, slowly and without making anybody feel dumb. A trust, Harold said, is like a big strong box with a lid. Six years ago, Walter and I put our house into that box, along with the money from selling the Blue Kettle. After Walter died, his life insurance went in too. The box owns those things now, and the person who holds the key is called the trustee. Since Walter died, the only person holding that key was me.

"And it's a revocable trust," Harold said. "Revocable means you can change it. You can change it any day you like, Diane. That's rather the point."

"Then what is that law firm after?" I asked.

"The key," Harold said. "If a judge decides you can't manage your own affairs, somebody else gets put in charge of you and of everything in that box. You can guess who would volunteer."

"Then let's change it," I said.

Harold pulled a yellow legal pad toward him and uncapped his fountain pen. "I have been waiting six years for you to say that," Harold said, "and hoping for six years that you never would."

We started with one sentence. Harold wrote it out in his careful, old-fashioned hand, and then he read it to me slowly, the way he would read it to a judge.

"Any beneficiary who, during my lifetime, attempts to have me declared incompetent, or attempts to take control of my person or my property without my consent, shall be treated for all purposes of this trust as though he or she had died before me."

"Now say it in plain English, Harold," I said.

"It means this," Harold said. "If any of your children tries to get a judge to call you incompetent, or tries to take charge of you or your things without your say-so, then as far as this trust is concerned, that child died before you did. Not really dead, of course. Dead on paper."

"And what happens to the share of a child who dies before me?" I asked.

"It doesn't disappear," Harold said. "Under your trust, it goes down one step, to that child's own children."

I sat very still. Kevin has one child, and the night before, she had been crying at my kitchen table over a slice of apple pie.

"Olivia," I said.

"Olivia," Harold said. "Held in trust until she is twenty-five, so nobody can talk a girl out of it at eighteen. I would look after it for her, and later Tommy would. Mostly Tommy. I don't intend to live forever, but I intend to give it a good try."

"Harold, everything they've done so far happened before this sentence existed," I said. "Does it reach back that far?"

"During my lifetime is a long time, Diane," Harold said. "It covers this October as well as next month, and I doubt Kevin is finished."

I told him I would give a great deal to be wrong about that.

"You could simply cut him out, Diane," Harold said. "But then Kevin's lawyer tells a judge you were an angry mother with hurt feelings. This way, if Kevin loses anything, he'll have done it with his own hands."

Then we did the rest. Kevin had written that Tommy gets nothing extra, so I told Harold I would like Tommy to get the house, with my right to live in it for as long as I live. I wanted fifty thousand dollars set aside right away for any Harlan Creek High student who wants to cook for a living, and I wanted it called the Blue Kettle Scholarship. Harold wrote it all down, and partway through he had to stop and clean his glasses.

"And Rachel?" Harold asked.

I thought about my daughter's one brave sentence in that chat. "Rachel hasn't signed anything or called anyone or filed anything," I said. "Going along is wrong, but it isn't what your sentence covers. Leave her exactly as she is."

"I'll have it typed," Harold said. "Come back Thursday at ten, and I'll have a notary and two witnesses."

"My doctor is giving me the longest test she has on Friday," I said.

"Good," Harold said. "Sign on Thursday, test on Friday, and let them argue with both."

Then Harold went to the old green safe in the corner and came back with a long envelope, yellowed at the edges. He did not hand it to me. He laid it on the desk between us, and I saw Walter's square, careful handwriting across the front.

It said, "For Diane, if the kids ever start circling. Read it to all of them."

"He gave it to me the week you two signed the trust," Harold said. "He told me I would know when. I believe this is when."

I wanted to open it more than almost anything. I reached for it, and then I stopped. Walter had written "all of them," and in all our years of marriage I never once opened his mail.

Harold nodded as if I had answered a question, and he put the envelope back in the safe.

"Walter made me promise to read it out loud," Harold said. "All of them in one room. So the question is, Diane, how do you want to get them there?"
