# Clear Space

The green tin did not vanish in a day. The dumpster was only the last step, and the first step that mattered was Walt's closet, in January.

I should tell you first that one person saw all of it coming, and he was gone before it arrived. The Christmas before Walt died, Greg and Marnie drove out for the day. Walt had a stack of his father's old Popular Mechanics magazines tied with twine beside the woodstove, forty years of them, and when Marnie came in with an armload of firewood she stopped short of the stack and walked a wide circle around it, like a woman going around a hole in the ice. She did not say a word. That night Walt hung his barn coat on its hook and said, "She's scared of something, Bern."

"Of magazines?"

"I don't know of what," he said. "But you watch how she walks around them." Then he put on the kettle and whistled his two bars, and I told him he had spent too many years reading teenagers. I would think of that sentence again at dawn on a hill of garbage, when it was far too late to ask him what he meant.

So in October Marnie did the pantry, as I told you. In January she did Walt's closet. She arrived on a Saturday morning with Greg, two flattened boxes, and a roll of blue painter's tape. Greg carried things up the stairs and did not meet my eyes.

Walt's side of the closet was exactly as he had left it. Eleven flannel shirts, three pairs of work pants with sawdust still in the cuffs, a tweed jacket he wore to every school board meeting and every funeral, including the last two, and the canvas barn coat with a pencil stub in the pocket. He wore that coat every Thursday for years. After breakfast he would put it on, pick up his tool bag, and say, "I'll be at the shop, Bern," and come home at supper with sawdust in his hair. I took the shop to be the school shop, where he still helped the young teacher. I never asked. I had not touched any of it in ten months. I would stand in the doorway some nights and breathe in, and it still smelled like cedar and machine oil and him.

Marnie made three piles on the bed. "Keep, donate, let go," she said, and she wrote the words on the tape in her small tidy hand. "We'll keep three shirts. That's plenty. A memory doesn't need eleven shirts, Bernie."

"It might need eight," I said.

She smiled at me the way you smile at a child who has asked if the moon follows the car. "Smell is the hardest thing to let go of," she said gently. "But it's only in the fabric. It isn't him."

She was not wrong that it was only fabric, and she was not unkind. She simply had no idea that for ten months the cedar and the machine oil had been the only part of the house that still talked back.

I saved the barn coat and the tweed jacket by standing in front of them. I lost eight shirts. The next morning I bought back five of them at the church thrift store for a dollar apiece, and the woman at the register rang me up without a word. That was the day I learned something about Marnie. She worked in wholes. Every one of her kindnesses was a whole room, or a whole closet, or a whole life, and she never noticed the one small thing holding it up.

Then came the twenty-sixth of April, a Saturday, and my dentist.

I had a crown on my calendar. Greg offered to drive me, which should have warned me, because Greg does not offer to drive anybody ninety minutes. I took the truck. Marnie said, "Of course, Bernie, take your time," and I remember thinking it was the nicest thing she had ever said to me.

I was gone from eight in the morning until a quarter after one, and on the way home I stopped for a furnace filter, because Walt had always kept a spare. When I pulled into my driveway there was a silver minivan with Clear Space by Marnie on the door, and Greg's sedan behind it.

I walked into my kitchen with half my face still asleep and stopped in the doorway.

Somebody had lined up six white canisters along the counter in a row as straight as a fence. They were labeled, in black lettering that came out of a machine, Flour, Sugar, Coffee, Tea, Pasta, and Odds and Ends. I read the last one twice. My kitchen had been the same for forty years, a jumble of crockery and wooden spoons, and it had never in its life contained anything labeled Odds and Ends.

The cupboard over the sink was bare, and the hen cookie jar was gone. The corkboard where Walt pinned my route schedule, my dentist cards, and the phone numbers of everyone I had ever delivered to had been replaced by a little chalkboard that said Today in a looping script.

"Where's the corkboard?" I said, and it came out lopsided from the numbness.

"It was just cluttering the wall," said Marnie. She stood at the table with her clipboard against her chest. "Bernie, look at the counter. Look how much room you have."

I opened the cabinet for Walt's coffee mugs, the mismatched ones, the Fenmore Feed and Seed one with the cracked handle, the one the high school seniors gave him in nineteen ninety-four, and found six matching white cups on hooks.

"Where are Walt's mugs?"

Marnie's face did something small. "They went in the donation box, Bernie. With the plates. We're at the church thrift every Monday, so Greg already took the box."

"All twelve."

"They were chipped, honestly. Some of them were a hazard."

Greg appeared behind her in the doorway with a trash bag in each hand. He looked at the floor, and he said the thing he had been saying since he was nine years old and the neighbor's cat got into the pie.

"Mom, she means well."

I have spent thirty-four years walking up to strange dog yards. You do not run, you do not shout, and you keep the mail held out in front of you like a little paper flag of peace. I did that. I put my furnace filter on the counter, where it looked like a theft among all that white, and I said, "Thank you, Marnie, for the canisters."

That was my first mistake. Marnie heard a thank-you. What I meant was that I was going to survive her, but she had no way to hear that part.

She hugged me hard, with the clipboard between us, and she said, "That's all I want, Bernie. I just want you to be able to breathe."

Then she let me go and looked past me out the kitchen window, across the yard, toward the long white machine shed where Walt had spent every afternoon of his working life.

"Next," she said, smiling, "is the workshop."
