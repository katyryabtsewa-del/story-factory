# The Heron Doesn't Argue

By eight o'clock on Thanksgiving night I had two full pages on Frank's legal pad, and I had not yet decided a single thing. People imagine that a woman in my position stands up and throws a plate. I sat very still and did the arithmetic, which is worse.

The first column was what Craig had done. The second was what I could do about it, and for an hour the second column held exactly one line, in my own handwriting: Tell him no.

That is a perfectly good plan. I could meet him at the door on Friday, hand him a cup of coffee, and say no, Craig, this is my house and I am staying in it. I knew exactly how it would go, because I had watched it go that way for forty years. He would be stung. He would say, Mom, you've misunderstood, and he would cancel the listing with a great sigh, and Dana would be gracious, and I would be thanked for being so reasonable. Then in a year or two the plan would come back, wearing a friendlier hat. A man who has been told no for a month has only learned which door to try next.

I got up and walked through the house with the lamps off, the way you walk through a ward at night. I put my hand on the back of Frank's chair. I stood in the doorway of the room with the glow-in-the-dark stars, and then in the mudroom with the little rubber boots that have faded ducks on the toes. Every room had a person standing in it who was not there anymore, and I had been keeping the whole house the way a nurse keeps a bed for a patient who is not coming back.

That was the truth I had not wanted. I loved that house. I also had been using it, for six years, to avoid deciding anything about my own life. Frank put my name on that deed so no one could sell my roof out from under me. He never said I had to sit under it forever.

And I could not stay and pretend the plan had not been made. I would be watching for the next email every time the tablet lit up.

If I sold, I would need somewhere to go, and that was the question that stopped me at the foot of the stairs. I had never thought about it, because I had never thought I would leave. Then, the way a name comes back to you in the shower, I remembered a house on Mill Street.

It is a small white farmhouse with a deep porch, a plum tree in the side yard, and a sign out front with Nan's face on it, because Nan Ouellette puts her face on everything she sells. The woman who owned it died in August. I had driven past it a hundred times since, and I had said to myself, out loud, in the Subaru, that a person could grow old in a house like that without asking anybody's permission. I had said it as a joke.

I went back to the kitchen and sat down. On the legal pad, in the second column, I wrote Mill Street, and then I wrote something I had not expected to write, which was the word Amos.

I should say that I am not a reckless woman. I once made a seventh grader wait forty minutes in my office for a nosebleed to stop rather than send him back to class too early. But there is a kind of calm that comes after a long fear, when the fear has finally been looked at straight, and it feels a great deal like clear water. I put on my barn coat, took the flashlight from the hook by the door, and walked down to the dock. The night was cold and clear, and the stars were coming out over the cove one at a time.

At the far end of the dock, standing on one of the old piling posts in the glow from Amos's porch light, was a great blue heron. I stopped where the planks began and held the light low so I wouldn't blind it. It was standing on one leg with its neck folded down into its shoulders, as still as a post, and I stood just as still, and we both waited.

I watched it for a long time. A heron does not argue with the water. It stands there until the thing it wants comes close enough, and then it takes it, once, and does not give a speech about it. I thought about how many times I had argued with Craig in my head and how few times out loud. Nobody on that tablet had ever once thought to ask me a question, and I had gone along for so long that the yes had worn a groove in the floor.

Then the heron lowered one foot into the shallows, very slowly, and I knew what I was going to do. It was not a feeling. It was the way a diagnosis arrives, a thing already true that you finally say aloud.

"All right," I said to the heron. "I'll make the first move."

I walked back up the dock with the flashlight pointed at my own boots. I was frightened, and I was not going to pretend otherwise. I was about to give away a house that held thirty-eight Thanksgivings, three notebooks of Frank's handwriting, and the porch swing where I sat the night after the funeral. A woman who sells a house like that does not do it lightly, and she does not do it without a few pieces of herself coming off.

I stopped on the porch and looked at the water one more time. Then I went inside and took out the tackle box, because I wanted something in my hands that was mine.

Under the thermometer and the box of adhesive bandages was the letter I had been carrying since September. My own doctor had written it after my checkup, because I had asked for a cognitive screening, which I did every year after Frank's stroke, on principle. It said, in plain type, that I had scored perfectly, that my memory and judgment were sound, and that I was a pleasure to examine. I had laughed about the last part. I did not laugh now. I folded the letter into my pocket, because I had a feeling that a woman about to do something sudden would need a piece of paper that said she was in her right mind.

It was two minutes to nine. I found Nan Ouellette's number in my phone, under her full name and a note I had typed years ago, which said, Answers on the first ring. Then I sat down at Frank's table, put the legal pad in front of me, and pressed the button to call.
