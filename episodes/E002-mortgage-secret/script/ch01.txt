# Not Really Family

On the last Saturday in June, I carried two dozen lemon bars through the side gate of my son's house, and the first thing I heard on the other side was my daughter-in-law explaining who I was.

"Oh, that's Trevor's mother," Cassidy said, in the bright voice she saved for her followers. "She helps out. She's not really family, more like support staff."

A woman I had never met, holding a glass of white wine, laughed out loud. A man beside her laughed too, and then a few more people near the drinks table, not unkindly, the way people laugh when they have been given permission. I was standing about ten feet away, behind a trellis wound with white string lights, with a baking tray in my hands and the gate latch still clicking behind me.

I set the tray down on the nearest table, because I was raised not to drop food in anger. I straightened the foil. Then I stood there and did arithmetic, which is what I do when my feelings have somewhere to go and no place to put them.

By the first week of September, I would stop paying for that house. And the people who had laughed on that patio, or the ones who mattered most among them, would hear every single number, out loud and in order.

My name is Marlene Dunmore. I was sixty-six that summer, and I live in Pelham Landing, Michigan, in a small brick bungalow on Orchard Row, twelve minutes from that patio. I spent thirty-four years as a bookkeeper for the Kellerman County school district, which means I spent thirty-four years being the person in the building who knew where every dollar went and was never once invited to the party where it was spent. I keep my records in green clothbound ledgers, pencil first and pen afterward, and I hum a hymn while I add. My late husband, Gerald, used to say he could judge the state of our checkbook from the tune. "Blessed Assurance" meant we were fine. "Rock of Ages" meant the roof was leaking.

Gerald taught high school shop, and he died seven years ago this past March, of a stroke, at sixty-four, on an ordinary Tuesday with a stack of birdhouse plans on the counter. I had been a wife for most of my life, and in one afternoon I was a widow with a pension, a life insurance check, and a son twelve minutes away.

The party was called Six Years in Our Dream Home. I know that because the name was printed on a banner strung across the back of the house at forty-one Tamarack Court, a four-bedroom place in brick and gray siding with a two-car garage and a patio that Cassidy had staged like a magazine. There were thirty guests, a table of cheeses cut into shapes, and a sign that said, in curly letters, "Gather Here." I looked around for a spot where I could gather and did not see one.

My son, Trevor, was at the grill in an apron that said "Grill Sergeant." He is thirty-eight, with my late husband's shoulders and my own habit of hoping a problem will leave if it is not looked at. He saw me, lifted his tongs in a small wave, and then looked at the burgers as if they had said something important.

Soon I heard a horn I would know anywhere, because it plays the first four notes of a song nobody has ever identified. Odessa Wray had arrived in her nineteen ninety-eight Buick. She is sixty-eight, a retired loan officer from the Harbor Light Credit Union, and my friend of thirty years. She came through the side gate carrying a bag of peaches nobody had asked for, looked at me once, and sat down in the chair next to mine.

"What did I miss?" Odessa asked.

I told her, in a low voice and in the exact words, because accuracy is a habit. Odessa said a word I will not repeat on this program, and then she said, "Want me to pour that wine on her?"

"No," I said.

"Want me to pour it on someone?"

"Odessa."

"You're doing the face," she said. "Your bookkeeper face. Somebody is about to be audited."

I did not answer. I sat very straight with my hands in my lap, and I watched my son's wife work the patio like a woman who had been born to it. Cassidy is thirty-six, bright and quick, and she smiles with every one of her teeth. She runs an online business styling other people's homes, she has a great many followers, and the house at Tamarack Court is the backdrop for all of it. She calls it our dream home.

Around six o'clock, Cassidy tapped a fork against her glass, and the whole yard went quiet. She thanked everyone for coming. She said that six years ago she and Trevor had taken a leap, and that it had only worked because of Trevor's hard work and "our amazing community, who have made this house a home." The guests clapped. A man whistled. Cassidy looked directly at me for half a second, and she smiled, and then she looked past me at someone else.

She did not say my name. She did not say Gerald's name, either, although it was his insurance money that had put the first ninety thousand dollars into those walls. Trevor looked at his shoes and rubbed the back of his neck, which is what he does when he hopes to disappear.

"Marlene," Odessa said quietly, "say the word and I will drive that Buick through the cheese table."

"Eat a peach," I told her.

That was when I felt a tug on my cardigan. My grandson, Jonah, had crawled under the string lights and between the folding chairs, the way he does when he does not want a grown-up to see him coming. He is eight, with a gap in his front teeth big enough to post a letter through, and he wore an expression of great seriousness.

"Gran," he said. "You came the sneaky way."

"I did," I said. "Would you like a lemon bar?"

He took two, which is his habit, and ate the first in three bites. Then he leaned in close to my ear and whispered, so that nobody else could hear.

"Gran, Mommy said you're not on the sign."

"What sign, sweetheart?"

He took me by the hand and led me around the corner of the house, past the garbage bins and the hose reel, to the front steps. He pointed to the porch post beside the door. There, mounted on a slab of polished oak with brass letters, was a plaque that had not been there the Sunday before.

It read: The Dunmore Family, Established Here.

I read it twice. And I stood on the steps of a house I had paid for, under a sign that did not include me, holding the hand of the one person in that yard who had noticed.
