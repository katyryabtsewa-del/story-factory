# The Spreadsheet

I was carrying a tray of coffee cups past the den when I heard my daughter-in-law put a price on my house. It was the fourteenth of September, the day of my husband Walter's memorial lunch, and the whole house smelled of baked ham and coffee. The den door was half open. Brittany was sitting on the couch with her tablet on her knees, reading aloud in the bright, sing-song voice she saves for waiters.

"Okay, so," Brittany said. "The lake cabin, the jewelry, the house. She won't last five years, and the house alone is three-forty."

My son Kevin laughed, the easy laugh of a man hearing a good weather report. Then my daughter Rachel asked, in a small voice, "What about Grandma's sapphire ring?"

A little over two months later, I invited all three of them to Thanksgiving dinner to hear my will read early. My lawyer read it aloud at my own kitchen table, over pie and coffee, and only one sentence in it mattered.

But that is the end of the story, and I was raised to start a recipe at the top.

The top of this one is that hallway. My name is Diane Whitaker. I was sixty-nine that fall, and I have lived my whole life in Harlan Creek, Ohio, a town small enough that the hardware store knows your furnace filter size. For thirty-one years, Walter and I ran the Blue Kettle, the diner on Main Street, until we sold it eight years ago. He worked the grill, and I worked everything else: the counter, the register, the books, and the pies.

Nobody answered Rachel right away. Brittany was scrolling, and I could hear her fingernail tick against the glass.

Through the gap in the door I could see Kevin in the armchair by the window. He is my oldest, forty-four, a sales manager at a car dealership in Columbus. He and Brittany had come forty minutes late, in a leased black car about the size of my kitchen. He had bought a boat that spring, too, and as far as I know it had been in the water twice. At the door he had said, "Traffic, Mom," in the soft, soothing voice he uses when he wants something.

Rachel sat at the far end of the couch. She is my middle child, forty-one, a dental office manager in Indianapolis, divorced the year before. She had hugged me a little too long that morning, the way she does when she feels guilty about something she hasn't done yet.

Brittany had kissed the air beside my ear and called me Mama Di. I have asked her not to call me that. She has decided I was joking.

Behind me, in the front room, I could hear Tommy unfolding more of the twenty chairs he had brought from Saint Anne's at eight that morning. Tommy is my youngest, thirty-six, with a two-bay repair shop and very few words.

Down the hall, my granddaughter Olivia was crimping a sour cherry pie at the oak table Walter built in nineteen eighty-four, with her tongue between her teeth, exactly the way I do. She is sixteen, Kevin and Brittany's only child, and she wants to be a chef.

At the head of that table was Walter's chair, and nobody sits in it. He died fourteen months before that lunch, on a hot July morning on our porch. It was his heart. He was seventy-two, and he went quick and quiet, the way he did most things. I have been grateful for the quiet part and angry about the quick part ever since.

In the den, Brittany found the line she wanted. "Just put your name in the Who column," Brittany said. "That's what it's for."

That ring had been my mother's. I counted to ten, careful to keep off the board that squeaks. Then I pushed the den door open with my hip and walked in smiling. "Who wants coffee?" I asked.

Brittany flipped the tablet face down on her knee so fast it slapped. Kevin jumped up to take the tray. "Mom, sit down," Kevin said. "You've been on your feet all day." Rachel stared at the carpet, and a red flush crept up her neck, the same flush she got at eleven when she broke the bathroom mirror and blamed the cat.

I did not sit down. I handed out cups and straightened Kevin's tie, and my hands did not shake once. I have poured coffee for truck drivers, and for the mayor on the morning after he lost an election, and I know how to keep a cup steady.

The lake cabin on Brittany's list almost made me laugh, because we do not own one. It belongs to Harold Greene, our family lawyer and Walter's best friend for forty years, and Walter only called it ours because Harold gave him a key in nineteen ninety and never asked for it back. I made a note to tell Harold he had been divided up too.

By four o'clock the neighbors had gone, Tommy was folding chairs on the porch, and Kevin and Brittany were outside arguing about who would drive. I wiped down the kitchen counter, which was already clean, because that is what I do when I am thinking. Then I went to the den for the cups.

Brittany's tablet was lying on the arm of the couch. She had forgotten it, or she had stopped worrying about me, and I still don't know which is worse. As I reached for a saucer, the screen lit up with a message, and behind the message was a spreadsheet. I lifted my reading glasses on the beaded chain Olivia made me and read the title.

It said "Mom," and then a dash, and then "Exit Plan." It was the same kind of dash they carve on a headstone, between the year you were born and the year you died. As far as my daughter-in-law was concerned, the second year was already filled in.
