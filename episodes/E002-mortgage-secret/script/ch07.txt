# The Family Meeting

On the first Saturday of September, at two o'clock in the afternoon, three people sat down at my kitchen table, and one of them had not been invited.

The courier had come to Tamarack Court the afternoon before, at four, as Naomi had promised. At nine that night, Trevor called, his voice as thin as wire, to say there was a letter from a law office and that Cassidy thought it was a mistake. I told him I would be happy to explain it at my house at two o'clock tomorrow, and that he should please bring his wife and the envelope.

I had set the table that morning, with a pot of coffee, a plate of lemon bars on the good china, four cups, and four clean spoons. In front of my own chair I had stacked the three green ledgers in a small tower. Odessa had taken Jonah to the depot museum to see the old locomotives, with orders not to bring him back before five.

Trevor and Cassidy arrived together, and so did Bernadette, in a lavender blouse, carrying her purse in front of her like a shield. "I hope you don't mind," Bernadette said. "I heard there was a family meeting, and I am family."

"Of course, Bernadette," I said. "Sit down. Have a lemon bar."

Cassidy sat very straight and set the envelope on the table like evidence. "Marlene," she said, "I'd like to understand why we're getting letters from lawyers."

"I'll read you something first, dear," I said, "and then I'll answer every question you have."

I pulled my glasses down, opened the first green ledger, and read aloud in the voice I used at school board budget meetings, which has never once been interrupted by anyone who understood it.

"Six years ago in April, the house at forty-one Tamarack Court was purchased for three hundred twelve thousand dollars. The down payment was ninety thousand dollars, from the life insurance of my husband, Gerald Dunmore. The mortgage was two hundred twenty-two thousand dollars, for thirty years, at Harbor Light Credit Union. The mortgage is in one name. The deed is in one name. The name is Marlene Dunmore."

Bernadette's lemon bar stopped halfway to her mouth.

"The monthly payment, with taxes and insurance, is two thousand four hundred dollars. Trevor and Cassidy have paid eight hundred dollars a month, which was entered as groceries. Over seventy-two months, that is fifty-seven thousand six hundred dollars. I have paid the remaining sixteen hundred a month, which is one hundred fifteen thousand two hundred dollars. I paid twenty-eight thousand dollars to finish the basement suite, nineteen thousand five hundred for the roof, and five thousand eight hundred for the furnace. The total I have paid, beyond what was paid by Trevor, is one hundred sixty-eight thousand five hundred dollars."

I looked up. "About one hundred sixty-eight thousand dollars, if you would like it in round numbers."

"That's not possible," Cassidy said. Her smile was gone, and what was left looked younger and frightened. "Trevor said it was a loan, and a little family help."

"Trevor?" I said.

My son sat with his hands flat on the table, looking at the lemon bars. For a long moment he stood at the edge of a diving board he had been standing on since he was nine years old.

"Mom paid, Cass," Trevor said. "She's been paying the whole time."

A fly bumped against the window screen, and the clock struck the quarter hour.

Cassidy stared at him, and then she turned on me, much louder. "You tricked us. You sat in our house every Sunday and let me decorate it and put my name on it, and you tricked us into thinking we owned it."

"Nobody tricked you, dear," I said. "Nobody told you, and that is a different thing. That part belongs to Trevor, who asked me to keep quiet, and to me, for agreeing. I will own my share. I am only asking you to own yours."

"Cass, honey," Bernadette said, in a smaller voice than I had ever heard from her. "I gave notice on my apartment. They're renting it to somebody else on the first of October."

Cassidy turned and looked at her mother, and something went out of the room, like air out of a tire.

I slid an envelope across the table. "This is my copy of the notice you received yesterday," I said. "It ends the family rent arrangement, in writing, according to the law. And this," I said, and I set the pink sticky note beside it, "was stuck to the broker's cover sheet in the packet Trevor brought me."

Cassidy picked up the note. Trevor leaned over her shoulder and read it twice.

"Don't let her read it, she'll make it a thing," I said, from memory, and I took off my glasses. "I read it, Cassidy. And I am making it a thing."

"Now," I said. "Here is what I propose, in writing, and Naomi Feld will answer any questions you like. I will sell you this house for three hundred sixty thousand dollars, which is thirty thousand below what it is worth. You would take out your own mortgage, on your own credit, with a cosigner who is not me, and we would close within ninety days. Trevor's credit has recovered enough to qualify. If you choose not to, the notice stands, I sell on the open market, and the rent that has been called groceries will be called rent until then."

"You're taking our house," Cassidy said.

"I am offering you my house, below its value, so that for the first time you can truly say it is yours," I said. "I am not asking for a profit. I am asking for the arithmetic to come out even."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then a chair scraped, and Trevor was standing.

I have watched my son graduate, carry a coffin, and hold his boy for the first time. I had never watched him stand up to anyone. He did it badly, with his knees unsure of the job, but he did it.

"Cass," Trevor said, "I lied to you for six years. I lied every month when I sent the transfer, because I was ashamed, and I'm sorry. That's mine, not Mom's. And there's one more thing." His voice shook. "You owe my mother an apology. A real one. Not a boundary. Not a guideline. An apology."

Cassidy looked at him as if she had never seen him before. Then she stood, picked up her purse, and walked out of my front door without a word, which for Cassidy was the loudest thing she ever said. Bernadette looked at me and followed her with the lemon bar still in her hand.

Trevor stood alone at the table. He picked up two of the coffee cups, one in each hand, and said in a very small voice, "Mom, can I at least wash these?"

"The cups can wait," I said. "Sit down, son. We are going to talk about why you never told me you were drowning."
