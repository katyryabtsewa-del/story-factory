# Page Four of Four

At ten o'clock on Friday morning, I climbed the stairs of Teague Title and Notary for the second time that week, with a recording running in my left pocket and a clear envelope in my purse.

A telephone shaped like a black starfish sat in the middle of the table, and out of it came the bright voice of a woman from Cumberland Home Lending, who sounded like a flight attendant announcing a smooth landing. Gordon Teague sat at the head in a red bow tie, clicking his pen cap. Curtis sat across from me in his camel coat. Nicole had come straight from the dental office and sat at my right, and she took my hand under the table and squeezed it. I squeezed back, and it went through me like a pin.

The stack lay in front of me again, and this time nobody called it Medicare. I looked at it as a confused woman would, with my head tilted.

"Curtis, dear," I said, "remind me. What were we here for?"

"Josie, hon." He said it with great patience. "We're closing a loan on the house. We talked about it, remember? You gave me the power of attorney in November so I could handle the details, and today you only have to confirm it."

"The power of attorney," I said. "Remind me, when did I sign that?"

He glanced at Nicole and then at the telephone. "On the eighteenth of November. A Tuesday. You wore your blue church dress, and you signed twice, because the first pen smeared. You were in very good form, Josie. You made a joke about the pen."

"Did I?" I said. "Gordon, tell me what you remember."

Gordon Teague's pen cap clicked. "Two-fifteen," he said. "I remember it clearly. A Tuesday afternoon. You were very much yourself, Josie."

I felt the telephone in my pocket, a small warm weight, and I thought of Detective Hatch behind the louvered door. I said, "I can't recall a thing. Gordon, was it raining that day?"

"It was dry," he said, too quickly. "Dry as a bone."

"Mister Teague, it rained until five," said a quiet voice from the doorway. Marisol stood there with her green lanyard and her spiral notebook. "I mopped the stairs twice."

Nobody moved. Out of the speaker the bright voice said, "For the record, I need to confirm that the borrower personally appeared before the notary on the date of the original power of attorney, and that she is signing today of her own free will."

"Of course," Curtis said, and he put a hand on the stack.

"One moment," I said. I took off my glasses and folded them. "I would like to answer that one myself."

I laid the clear envelope on the table and slid out the discharge packet from Riverbend Eye Surgery Center, turned to face my daughter. I laid Fern's photograph beside it, with the time in the corner.

"On the eighteenth of November," I said, in the voice I use to tell a bride the zipper will not close, "I checked in at an eye surgery center in Cleveland, Tennessee, at eleven in the morning. They operated on my left eye at one-thirty. At two-fifteen I was in a recovery chair with a plastic shield taped to my face, drinking apple juice from a paper cup. They discharged me at ten past three. Fern Albright took that picture at twelve minutes after, in the parking lot. She sent it with one word. Pirate."

Nicole picked up the photograph with both hands. Her eyes went from the picture to the date on the discharge sheet, and I watched the whole of it arrive in her face, one piece at a time, the way a pattern comes together on a cutting table.

"You brought me chicken soup the next evening, Nicole," I said. "You fed me with a spoon."

"Mama," she whispered. "The nineteenth. You were. I did."

"Josie, hon." Curtis's voice had not changed, but his hand had come off the stack. "You got your days mixed up. Nicole, she's been doing this for weeks."

"I have not been confused about anything today," I said. "Marisol, did anyone named Ledbetter come into this office on the eighteenth?"

"No, ma'am. I was at the front desk all afternoon." She held up her notebook. "And I kept a receipt."

I asked Gordon to pass me the first paper, the power of attorney in his file, and after a long moment he did, with fingers that would not hold still. I drew a blank page from the bottom of the stack and signed it slowly, so that nobody could miss a stroke. Josephine A. Ledbetter. And under the last r, I made my little flick.

I slid it across the table to Nicole, with the forged page beside it. "Look under the r," I said. "For forty years I have signed with a knot. Earl said a seamstress ought to knot her thread. Now look at the paper that says I signed in November."

Nicole looked. Gordon looked. It took hardly any time at all. The knot was in the page I had just written, and in the receipt Marisol laid beside it. On the forged page there was nothing but the flat end of the r.

"This is a lot of fuss over a flourish," Curtis said, and his voice rose a little, like a man stepping on a loose board. "Nicole, she's confused. You saw the glasses in the refrigerator."

"She just read me a hospital paper out loud, Curtis," Nicole said. "Word for word. Times and everything."

From the starfish telephone, the bright voice said, with a great deal less brightness, "Mister Brandt, in light of what I've heard, Cumberland Home Lending is declining to proceed. The application is withdrawn and the lien is being released and recorded as of this call. Please do not contact this office."

Gordon Teague picked his pen cap up off the table, held it against his chest, and began to cry, softly, like a man who has been holding something for seven weeks.

The louvered door at the back of the room opened. Detective Hatch came out with his thermos in one hand, as unhurried as a man arriving at church.

"Nicole, honey," Curtis said, turning to her with a face I had never seen on him. "Let's not do this here."

My daughter stood up. She did not say anything at all. She walked to the window and stood with her back to us, looking out at the gray square and the ice on the courthouse steps.

"Mister Brandt," said Detective Hatch, mildly, "I'd like you to come with me for a conversation."

Behind me I heard the scrape of Gordon Teague's chair as he got to his feet, before anyone had asked him to, and in the quiet that followed I understood that I had not raised my voice once.
