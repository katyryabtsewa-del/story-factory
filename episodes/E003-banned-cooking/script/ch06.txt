# The Professionals

My son stood at the head table in the lantern light with a microphone in his hand, and I folded my napkin once and laid it in my lap, the way you do when you know a long course is coming.

Gregory began well. He thanked his new father-in-law for the wedding, and Richard raised a hand from table one as if he were stopping traffic. He said that Courtney was the smartest, funniest person he had ever met, and that every day he woke up amazed that she had picked him. That part was sweet, and I smiled at him across the tent.

Then he took a sip of champagne, and I watched his shoulders change. When Gregory is nervous and the room is warm and people are laughing, he reaches for whatever gets the biggest laugh, and it is never kind.

"I have to say something about the food," he said. "I think we can all agree it has been unbelievable. And I want to tell you, that is not how I grew up eating." A few of Courtney's friends giggled. "My mom is here tonight, somewhere over there by the kitchen. She's probably checking on the gravy."

A bigger giggle went around the tent. I sat very still.

"So I just want to say," Gregory went on, lifting his glass, "to my mother, Nora, who for once let the professionals do the cooking."

The laughter came all at once. It was Courtney's friends, mostly, and some of the men at the long tables who had not been listening closely and laughed because the others did. It rolled across the tent, bright and loud, and it did not come from table fourteen. At table fourteen there was complete quiet. The cousin in lavender put down her wine. The banker had stopped moving his fork.

People always ask what I felt. It was not rage. It was older and quieter, the way you feel when a stair is missing in the dark, and underneath it was a small, clear certainty that the sentence was going to cost him.

I smoothed the tablecloth with the flat of my hand.

"And speaking of professionals," Gregory said, and he was beaming now, drunk on the sound of himself, "let's have a huge round of applause for Chef Marco Reyes and his team, who are back there making magic. Chef, come out here! Come take a bow!"

Courtney clapped and cried, "Bring him out!" I would like it noted that my son invited him. I did not arrange it.

The swinging doors opened and Marco walked out in his white jacket, wiping his hands on a towel, with Priya a step behind him holding her tablet against her chest. The guests applauded politely. Marco came up to the head table and shook Gregory's hand, and then he asked, quite gently, "May I?" and Gregory, grinning, handed him the microphone.

"Thank you, Gregory. Thank you, Courtney, and Mister Ashford, and every one of you." Marco's voice is the kind that fills a kitchen, and it filled that tent without trying. "I have never been prouder of a dinner than I am of this one, and I need to tell you why."

He turned toward the kitchen doors, and then he turned toward table fourteen.

"When I was nineteen years old," he said, "I walked into a restaurant on Willow Street called Pruitt's Table with everything I owned in a duffel bag, and I asked for a job washing dishes. A woman named Nora Pruitt showed me how to take apart a chicken. She taught me everything I know, and she did not charge me one dollar for it."

The tent had gone very quiet.

"The short rib you ate tonight is her pot roast," Marco said. "Horseradish cream and all. It came from an index card in her mother-in-law's handwriting. The brown butter apple tart, the one that is coming out in a few minutes, is her kringle, which people in this town used to line up for on Thursdays. The little sour cream raisin pies at the late-night station are hers. I renamed them, because I was told the guests would be very food-literate." He paused. "I think you are."

I looked up at the head table. Gregory's smile had slid off his face like a plate off a wet counter. Courtney had gone white.

"Nine years ago," Marco said, "Missus Pruitt sold her restaurant, and she took every dollar she had left and put it into mine. She holds forty percent of the company that fed you tonight. I promised her I would say nothing, and I am breaking that promise, and she can fire me on Monday, because I could not stand there and let anyone call her a woman who worked in food service." He held out his hand toward me, palm up. "Ladies and gentlemen, my teacher and my partner. Chef Nora Pruitt. Table fourteen, by the kitchen door."

I would like to tell you that I was angry with him. I was not. I sat there with my face as hot as a griddle and Sam's watch ticking on my wrist, and all I could think was that a boy I had taught to break down a chicken had just stood up for me in front of a hundred and eighty strangers.

It was Richard Ashford who stood first. He pushed back his chair at table one so hard that it fell over, and he stood in his gray suit, and he began to clap, slowly, one clap at a time, looking straight across the tent at me. Then the banker at my table stood, and the cousin in lavender stood beside him and said, "I knew it. It tastes like Sunday." Then the whole tent was on its feet, a hundred and eighty people, clapping and turning their heads, and some of Courtney's friends were clapping too.

When it quieted, Richard's voice rolled out across the tent. "Nora," he said, "would you say a few words?"

I stood. My knees were not what they were, but they held. I smoothed the tablecloth one more time and waved off Priya, who was hurrying toward me with a cordless microphone, because I have never needed one in a crowded room.

"Thank you, Marco, and thank you all for eating it," I said. "The recipes were Sam's mother's, and all I ever did was keep them warm. Gregory and Courtney, I wish you a long marriage, and I hope there is always something on the stove."

I sat down. It was not much, but it was dignified, and it was mine.

Up at the head table, a chair scraped back. Courtney stood with her napkin crumpled in her fist, and she did not look at me, or at her father, or at the hundred and eighty guests watching her. She walked out of the tent through the side flap with her gown gathered in both hands, and my son stared after her for one frozen second. Then he set down his glass, and he ran after her toward the terrace.
