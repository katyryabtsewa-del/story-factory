# Pears in September

The gavel came down at four minutes before noon by my watch, which was eight minutes before by the courthouse clock, and I have been telling you the rest of it from my porch six months later, with the first Bartletts coming in.

Nobody made a scene when it was over. Pearl crossed the carpet in her tennis shoes and hugged me so hard that my glasses slid, and she said into my shoulder, "I looked at the floor the whole time, Vera, like a Methodist." The three farmers each shook my hand and put their caps back on. My young lawyer told me he had never been so glad to be quiet in his life, and I told him that quiet was most of the job. Danny held the door and said, "Miss Vera," and nothing else, which was exactly right. A week later Imani Okafor sent me a postcard with a picture of a copper kettle, and on the back she had written, I enjoyed it again.

Summit Row Homes withdrew within the week. A letter came to my young lawyer saying that the company would no longer pursue any interest in the property. The option agreement Brent had signed was not worth the paper it was typed on, because a man cannot promise what he does not own, and I asked for nothing further from anyone. The company that hired the surveyors came out on a Saturday morning with a plain white van and pulled up all forty-one stakes without being asked. I stood at the fence and counted them going into the van, and I got forty-one, and the man doing the pulling said, "Yes, ma'am, that is all of them."

I thought a good deal that spring about what Lloyd said on the porch, that you do not own an orchard, you borrow it from the next fellow. In May two people from the Rogue Basin Land Trust came and sat at my kitchen table. They were a woman with a canvas bag and a man with a clipboard, and they ate my pear bread and did not hurry me. I had called them myself. I told them I wanted the farm to stay a farm, always, no matter who held the deed.

It took three weeks of paper. In the end I signed a conservation agreement that says the orchard can never be built on, divided, or sold for houses, and that it must stay in trees. The woman from the trust witnessed my signature, and then she cried a little, and I gave her a pear. It was my choice, because a person who wants to get even does not need three weeks of paper. I did it because I wanted to hand it on with the trees in better shape than I found them, and now nobody can undo that, not Brent, not me, and not the next fellow.

In July I put up a hand-painted sign at the end of Orchard Hollow Road. It said, Pick Your Own Pears, First Saturday in September, All Children Welcome, and under it, in smaller letters, Former Passengers Especially. I thought perhaps six people would come. On the first Saturday there were sixty-three.

I counted them, naturally. I counted them getting out of cars, and they were the children I used to drive, grown up and gray at the temples, with children of their own on their hips and sticky at their knees. The postmaster came in a straw hat with her husband and her two boys. A man I had last seen at nine years old, with a gap in his teeth, shook my hand and said that he still could not hear the words, Sit down, without feeling that I was behind him.

They took baskets from the barn and went off along the rows. I stood with my arms folded and counted heads going out, forty-one adults and children, and twenty-two more behind them, and then I counted heads coming in again at the end of the afternoon, the same way I did for thirty-seven years. Sixty-three out, and sixty-three in, and one small girl asleep in a wheelbarrow, which I counted as one.

Pearl was at the stand with her honey, selling more than I sold, and she gave me a peppermint at four o'clock. It was her last one. She turned the purse upside down to prove it, and a button and a roll of tape fell out.

"I will have to restock," she said. "This is the first time in forty years I have run out."

"Pearl," I said, "you have outdone yourself."

Now I should tell you about the phone. Brent left me a voicemail on the Sunday after the hearing, and another two weeks after that. I have saved both of them. I played the first one once and put the phone in a drawer. The second one I have played many times, because it is short and because I can hear in it that he has been practicing. It says, "Mom, it's Brent. I know you do not owe me a call. I read the transcript twice. I am paying back what I took, a little each month, and I am not asking you for a thing. I only wanted you to hear me say that you were right and I was wrong. I am sorry about the sevens." Then there is a quiet of about four seconds, and he says, "Okay. Bye, Mom," and hangs up.

I have not called him back. I am not punishing him. I am a woman who counts, and I have not yet got the number to come out. Some evenings I think that he is my only child, and that I held his hand at every crossing until he was nine. Other evenings I think of the stakes in my grass, and a man in a blazer patting my hand for the gallery. I do not think those two things cancel each other out. I think a person carries both, like a basket of pears on each arm, and walks very carefully.

It is evening now. The porch light is on, and the phone is on the rail beside my tea. Out in the rows the first Bartletts are coming in, and the air smells of them, sweet and a little sharp, and the ridge is going blue the way it does. I walked up there before supper, as I always do, and nobody was in my trees but the trees.

I will decide tomorrow, and I may well call him. But I would like to ask you something before I do. If it were your son, or your daughter, or your sister, would you call back? Would you let that person carry the sevens a little longer, or would you pick up the phone and see what he has to say? I would honestly like to know, and I would like to know where you are listening from, too, whether it is a kitchen or a car or a porch of your own. Whatever you are counting tonight, I hope it comes out the way you hoped. Good night, and thank you for keeping me company.
