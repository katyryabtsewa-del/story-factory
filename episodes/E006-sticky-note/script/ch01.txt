# The Sticky Note

The pen was already in my hand when my fingertips found the yellow square under the clipboard clip.

It was a gray Tuesday in January, the thirteenth, and I was sitting at the end of a long conference table in an office I had never entered in my life, looking at a stack of paper an inch and a half thick. My son-in-law Curtis had set it in front of me with the little flourish a waiter uses for dessert. "Just Medicare housekeeping, Josie, hon," he said. "Initials on page four, a signature on the last page, and we're done by lunch."

I leaned toward the first page and squinted, the way anyone does who has been reading fine print for seventy-one years. My daughter Nicole put her hand on my sleeve. She spoke gently, in the voice you use on a child who has wandered toward the street, and she spoke loudly enough for the notary, the receptionist and every other soul in that room to hear.

"Mom, your glasses are in your purse, remember?"

Curtis patted my other hand. Nobody looked at me, which is how I knew that everybody was looking at me.

I was not angry yet. What I felt was close to what I feel when a customer hands me a ruined gown and says it only needs a little taking in. I kept my face still, and I counted what I could see. By Friday morning every person in that room would know exactly what they had done, and I would not have raised my voice once.

Let me tell you about that room. Teague Title and Notary sat on the courthouse square in Sparrow Gap, Tennessee, between a vacuum repair shop and a place that sold fireworks in July and tax help in March. I had driven past it perhaps ten thousand times and had never once gone up the stairs. The conference room was on the second floor and smelled of carpet cleaner and scorched coffee, and the ice rain ticked against the window like somebody throwing rice.

Gordon Teague sat at the head of the table in a green bow tie, clicking the cap of his pen on and off, on and off, until I wanted to take it away from him like a toy. Across from me sat Curtis in his good camel coat, which he had not taken off, because Curtis always dressed as though he might be called away to something more important. Nicole sat beside me with her purse in her lap and her work cardigan buttoned wrong by one button. At the back of the room a young woman in a green lanyard stood at the copier, feeding it pages that did not seem to need copying. She had her back to me. She had not turned around once since I came in.

The stack was clamped to a clipboard, which Curtis said would save me from wrestling with it. I tilted it toward the window, the way I tilt a hem to see whether the line runs true, and the yellow square slid out into the light.

It was a sticky note, the ordinary kind, pressed against the back of the top page where only the person holding the board could see it. The words were written in ballpoint, in block capitals, every letter pushed hard into the paper by a hand that was not quite steady.

Don't sign. The first one wasn't you. Say you need your glasses.

Under that was a single letter, M.

A seamstress learns to keep her face quiet. A bride swears she has not gained an ounce, and you go on pinning. A mother says the hem is fine, and you go on smiling. So I smoothed the top page with the flat of my hand, the way I do when I am thinking, and I laid the pen on the table as if it were nothing at all.

"Josie, hon," Curtis said, "it's only initials on page four. Let's get ahead of it, and I'll buy everybody lunch at the Depot Grill."

"I'm sure it is, Curtis," I said. "But I can't read a word of it. I believe I need my glasses."

"They're in your purse, Mom," Nicole said. Then, softer, "Mama, we looked this morning."

"Those are the old ones, from before my eye. The ones for paper are in the Buick. I'm nearly certain I left them in the glove box."

The clicking stopped. Gordon Teague looked at Curtis, and Curtis smiled with every one of his teeth. Nicole was already half out of her chair, saying she would run down and get them.

"No, hon, I'd like the air," I said, and I stood up. "I've been sitting since the stairs, and my knees would like a word with me."

Without thinking, I picked up the clipboard and took it along. It was only the habit of forty-one years, carrying a garment with me until I was finished with it. I saw Gordon glance at Curtis again, and I saw Curtis give the smallest shrug, which said as plainly as a sentence that an old woman could hardly read anything without her glasses. At the back of the room, the copier stopped humming.

I went down the stairs one step at a time, holding the rail, in case anyone was watching from above. I was slow on purpose, and it was the first part of that day I enjoyed. Outside, the square had gone the color of pewter, and the courthouse clock said eleven minutes to ten. My gray Buick sat where I had left it, glazed with a thin shell of ice. I got in, shut the door, and let the silence settle around me like a quilt.

The glasses were in the glove box, in their case, exactly where I had put them in December and forgotten them. For once the old woman's memory and her story agreed with each other. Curtis's pen was still in my fist, and I set it in the cup holder as if it were evidence.

Then I peeled the yellow note off the page, slowly, the way you lift basting thread, and turned it over. On the back, in smaller letters, the same hand had written, I'll explain. Come to Hollis Street? No, I'll come to you.

I put on the right glasses and turned to page four, the one Curtis called initials. It was not initials. It was one long paragraph in the sort of type meant to be skipped, and it said that I, Josephine A. Ledbetter, did hereby ratify and confirm the durable power of attorney that I had signed on the eighteenth day of November, in the presence of Gordon Teague, notary public.

I read it three times. I had never signed a power of attorney in my life, and I had never been inside that office before this morning. And on the eighteenth of November I could have told you where I was to the very hour. It was not on the courthouse square.
