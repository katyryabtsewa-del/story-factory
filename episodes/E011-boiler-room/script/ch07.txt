# Open Meeting

At noon on Tuesday, with the building finally warm and my son's wife and her boss still talking behind a closed door in four-B, Neil walked into the boiler room.

I knew his step before I turned around. He still came down stairs on the outsides of his feet, like a boy trying not to wake the house. Mister Ruiz from the service company had come at nine, asked who did the blowdown in the voice of a man who has found a twenty-dollar bill, and gone. I was wiping my hands on a rag.

Neil stood in the doorway in a wrinkled gray suit that he had slept in on an airport floor. He looked at the red-tagged boiler, and the logbook open on the desk, and then at me in Dmitri's coveralls, and his face came apart a little.

"Mom," he said. "Isaiah left me eleven messages. I listened to them at the gate, one after another, and I kept thinking, my mother's down there with a wrench, and I'm sitting in a plastic chair."

"You couldn't have helped, honey. The planes weren't flying."

"That isn't what I mean." He came in two steps. "I should have answered the email."

"You answered it. You wrote, Looks great, V."

"I know what I wrote." He was holding the strap of his bag so tightly that his knuckles had gone pale. "I answered it, Mom. I just answered it to the wrong person. It was your name in the gray box, and I sent my reply to her. I did the same thing in November, in the elevator, looking at the numbers over the door. And we were going to list the apartment in the spring, Mom. I knew an assessment would hurt the price. I never once asked what the deferral was for."

I let that settle. "I heard you," I said. "I'm glad you said it."

"What do I do now?"

"At three o'clock you go and sit beside your wife in the Gallery, where people can see you. You don't speak for her, and you don't speak against her. You just sit there. That's the whole assignment."

He nodded, and he hugged me, hard, grease and all. I have raised exactly one child, and I know the difference between a hug that is meant and one that is only polite.

At three o'clock, the Gallery held about a hundred and thirty people on folding chairs, with more standing along the walls and some of them in the doorway. I had slept two hours on a couch by the fireplace, and I sat in the back row in my own clothes, between Isaiah, who had not gone home, and the young man from five-C, who had at least changed his sweatshirt. Dmitri sat in the front row in a clean shirt with his log in his lap. Graham Tolliver took the aisle seat in the second row, with his arms folded. Neil sat beside Vanessa near the middle, and I will say that he did exactly what I had told him to do.

Esther Bloom ran the room from the head table, with a coffee can in front of her for a gavel and her cane to strike it with. "This meeting will come to order," she said, rapping the can twice, and there was a great clang. "Each person gets two minutes, and that rule will be used fairly. Under our bylaws fifteen owners can call a special meeting, and I had forty-one signatures by breakfast. The board will ratify whatever you decide by Friday, and I expect it to behave."

Before she could call on anyone, Vanessa stood up.

I will always give her this. She stood the way she does everything, upright and fast, but the paper in her hand trembled just slightly, and her voice was not the one she used on boards. "I recommended a deferral that I believed was prudent," she said. "I was wrong about the risk. And I presented the engineer's report as smaller than it was. I'm resigning as treasurer, effective now, and the books will be turned over to the board today."

She sat down. Nobody clapped, and I don't think she expected anyone to. It cost her more than any speech she had ever given, and it was the one honest thing she did all day. Out of the corner of my eye I saw Neil's hand settle on hers, and she let it stay.

Then Dmitri stood, and he read his log, with the times, in a voice that shook on the first line and steadied on the third. When he got to the part about the pump seal still sealed in its plastic in a toolbox, I heard Tolliver breathe out through his nose, once.

Isaiah got up next, and he took exactly three sentences. "I've worked that door for twenty-two years," he said. "Last night forty of our neighbors sat by one fire because nobody thought the heat was an emergency until it stopped. I would like it to be one before it stops."

After that the room found its voice. A woman asked about the assessment, and Esther told her plainly that it would come to about three thousand five hundred dollars an apartment, and that she would move to spread it over twelve months. There was grumbling, until a retired man in the third row said, "It's cheaper than a burst pipe," and the grumbling dropped by half.

Esther put three motions to the room. Replace Boiler Two. Restore the quarterly service contract. Hire an independent engineer, one who answered to the residents and not to the managing agent. The ayes were so many that she did not trouble to count the nays, and then, being Esther, she counted them anyway. It was a hundred and eleven to seven. She was voted chair by acclamation, and she banged the coffee can so hard that it rolled off the table.

Graham Tolliver stood last, and he did not raise his voice. "I live on the sixth floor, in the penthouse," he said, "and I learned the condition of this building's boiler at seven forty this morning. I am the managing partner at the firm that employs your former treasurer, and I won't pretend that those two facts are unrelated. Effective Monday, she will be moving to a learning assignment in operations review, under someone who does not report to me." He paused. "That is all I'll say about a colleague in public. It is more than she would like, and less than some of you would."

Then Esther looked at the back row. "Missus Rudd, would you care to say anything?"

I stood up, a little stiffly, and I said two sentences. "A boiler is there so that nobody upstairs ever has to think about it. That is a promise, and you keep it in a basement where nobody sees."

They clapped, and I sat down quickly. Afterward, in the aisle, a small hand took hold of my sleeve, and a voice like a chalk line said, "Dear, you still weren't invited to Christmas. Come to mine."
