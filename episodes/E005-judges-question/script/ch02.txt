# The Young Man in the Baseball Cap

I was still sitting in Courtroom Two with my son's hand on mine, but my mind went back to a Thursday almost two weeks earlier, at my farm stand, when a young man in a baseball cap walked up to the counter and apologized before he had said a single word.

It was a cold, bright morning in the middle of March, and the trees behind the stand were bare and tight. In that season I sell Bosc pears from the cold room, pear butter, and dried slices in paper bags, along with whatever honey and jam my neighbor Pearl Dunbar trades me for them. Pearl was there in her coat with the fur collar, setting eighteen jars of blackberry jam on my shelf in a row she said was an improvement on mine, and we were arguing pleasantly about where the honey should go when the young man stepped up to the counter and took off his cap.

"Ma'am, are you Vera Halvorsen?" he said. "I am so sorry. I have to hand you this."

He was perhaps twenty-three, with red ears from the cold, and he held out a thick envelope as though it were a casserole he had dropped. I told him I was Vera Halvorsen. He said he was very sorry again, and that it was only his job, and then he put his cap back on and walked to a small gray car and sat in it for a full minute without starting the engine.

I opened the envelope on the counter, between a jar of pear butter and the cash box. The first page said Petition for Appointment of Guardian and Conservator, and under it were the names Brent Halvorsen, Petitioner, and Vera Halvorsen, Respondent. Pearl, who has a whisper that carries across a county fair, leaned over my shoulder and read it once and then said, much too loudly, "Well, I will be switched."

"Pearl," I said.

"Guardian? Of you?" She snatched the page. "Vera, you are the most guarded person I have ever met. You counted the change on a church bake sale table twice and found a nickel."

"It was a dime," I said, and my voice was steady, and I was a little proud of that.

I read the rest standing up while three customers came and went and bought pear butter. The petition said I had made delusional calls to the sheriff. It said I had left a pot burning. It said I wandered. It was written the way a letter from a bank is written, with every sentence kind enough that you could not point to the unkind part. Nobody in that paper was blaming me. They were only collecting me.

Brent called that night at seven, while I was standing at the stove watching a pot with great attention.

"Mom, I know you got the papers," he said. "I wanted to call before you heard it from someone else."

"I did hear it from someone else," I said. "I heard it from a young man in a baseball cap, and he was kinder about it than you are being."

"It's for your own good, Mom. You're alone out there. You're sixty-nine. What happens when you fall in the orchard and nobody finds you for a day?"

"Then I suppose Pearl will find me and complain about it for a week," I said. "Brent, what is this really?"

"It's a safety plan," he said. "That's all. We'll talk when you're calmer."

I told him I had never been calmer in my life, and hung up, and stood there for some time with the phone in my hand.

I had refused him twice. The first time was in the summer, at my own kitchen table, when he came out in his good car and talked about unlocking value. He said the farm was worth more as houses than as pears, and I told him it was worth exactly what it was. The second time was at Christmas, after the dishes, when he said it again with a folder under his arm. I said, flat and kind, as I would say it to a child who wanted to ride home on the wrong bus, "No, Brent. I am not going to sell, and I would rather we had a nice Christmas."

He had smiled and put the folder down. I thought that was the end of it. I did not know that a man who has been told no twice by his mother may decide that the trouble is not the word but the woman who says it.

The courtroom came back to me all at once, with its heater and its floor wax. Judge Marsh had asked the petitioner's lawyer to call his first witness, and he rose and said, "The petitioner calls Brent Halvorsen."

My son stood, buttoned his blazer, and walked to the witness stand, and on the way he gave me a small, sad smile, the sort a person gives a relative at a funeral. The clerk swore him in, and Brent said, "I do," in a voice so warm and clear that three of the church ladies in the gallery sighed.

By my watch it was nine twenty-six. The clock over the door said nine twenty-two.

The lawyer began gently. He asked my son to state his occupation, and Brent said he was a regional manager at Larch Hollow Land Brokers in Camas Junction, and that he had come before the court as a loving son. The lawyer asked how he had come to be concerned, and Brent folded his hands and looked at me with what I can only describe as tenderness.

"It started with the stove," he said. "Your Honor, I want to be very clear that I do not say this to embarrass her."

"Please go on, Mister Halvorsen," the judge said.

"One afternoon last fall, my mother put a pot on the stove and forgot it," Brent said. "It burned dry. The kitchen filled with smoke. If a neighbor had not noticed, I truly believe we would be talking about a fire, and maybe worse."

It had been one afternoon. I had been on the porch with Pearl, drinking iced tea, and when the smoke alarm went off Pearl had gone in with a dish towel and fanned the kitchen out the back door in about forty seconds. Brent had not been in my house since Christmas. I learned later that Pearl had told the story at a church supper as a funny one, and that in Tamarack Flats a funny story travels faster than a school bus. My son had taken that one small true thing and been adding rooms to it, like a man adding rooms to a house he did not own, until two of the church ladies were shaking their heads.

Then Brent reached down beside the witness chair, opened a folder, and took out a glossy brochure with a smiling gray-haired woman on the cover. "Your Honor," he said, "I have already visited a place for my mother. They are holding a room for her until Friday."

I knew that brochure from across the whole width of the courtroom. It had been in the folder he put down on my kitchen table at Christmas.
