# The Suite

The morning after the party, I got up at six, as I always do, and I put on my good blue cardigan, because Sunday dinner at Tamarack Court was at two o'clock and I had decided to go.

You may ask why. I had gone every Sunday for six years, and I wanted to see what they would do on the first Sunday after Cassidy said I was not family. Gerald used to say you cannot read a room from the parking lot.

I arrived at one thirty with a fresh pan of lemon bars. Trevor opened the front door, which was the only door I was still sure of, and he said, "Hey, Mom," in a voice like a man carrying a full cup of coffee across a room. I told him I was going to run downstairs for a minute, to collect a few things from the basement suite, as I had been instructed in writing.

The suite was my favorite part of that house. I had designed it myself, on graph paper, at my kitchen table, with a bedroom that caught the morning light, a little bath with a walk-in shower, and a kitchenette with a counter just my height. Twenty-eight thousand dollars, in my ledger, in green pen.

When I came down the last step, there was a woman in the middle of the bedroom holding a yellow measuring tape against the wall.

"Oh," Bernadette Vance said. "Marlene. Hello."

Bernadette is Cassidy's mother. She is sixty-three, divorced, and sells cosmetics, and she has a sweet voice that gets sweeter when she is about to say something sharp. She had a pencil behind her ear, and somebody had already taped paint samples to the wall in four shades the cards called Whisper, Mist, Cloud, and Dove, which are all white.

"I'm just measuring for curtains," Bernadette said. "Cassidy said you'd stopped coming down here."

"Did she," I said.

"She said you hadn't been in months. Which I think is a shame, because it's such a lovely space. Honestly, with a little love, it's going to be darling. Although I do wonder who chose that carpet."

"I did," I said.

"Well," said Bernadette, "it's very practical. Cassidy lived in four rentals before she was twelve, you know. She deserves a place that is really hers."

I walked to the corner of the room. Gerald's folding chair was where I had left it, a canvas camp chair with a cup holder he had built himself, and beside it, folded on a shelf, was the quilt my mother made. I picked up the chair under one arm and the quilt under the other. Bernadette watched me do it with her pencil in the air.

"You can leave those, if you like," she said. "I'm happy to find a use for them."

"I'm sure you would be," I said. "Thank you, Bernadette. These are mine."

I climbed the stairs with my husband's chair and my mother's quilt and put them in my car. Then I went back inside for dinner, because I was raised to finish what I started.

Dinner was a pot roast, which Cassidy had bought from a caterer and plated as though she had made it. There were five of us, with Bernadette on my left and Jonah across from me, kicking the table leg. Halfway through the meal, Cassidy tapped her water glass with her knife and said she had a little surprise for the family. She handed each of us a sheet of cream paper trimmed with a border of tiny leaves.

It said, across the top, "Dunmore Home: Family Guidelines."

There were six of them. Shoes off at the door. No unannounced visits. Dinner is at six, not two, "to support our evening routines." Photography of the home only with permission. Quiet hours after eight. The line that Cassidy had put in bold was number six. It said, "Extended family visits by invitation."

I read it silently, for the record, and then aloud, in a pleasant voice, to make sure I had it right.

"Extended family," I said. "Cassidy, dear, would that be me?"

"It would be everybody, Marlene," Cassidy said. "It's not personal. It just helps us all keep healthy boundaries."

Trevor looked into his pot roast. Jonah looked from his mother to me and stopped kicking the table.

"I think it's very well typed," I said. "Thank you for including me."

I folded the sheet in thirds and put it in my cardigan pocket. I ate all of my roast.

That night, I did what I do. I made a pot of tea, which I did not drink, and I sat at my kitchen table with the ledger open to June and replayed the whole party in my head, from the gate latch to the plaque. I could hear the exact pause Cassidy had left before the word "support," as if she had practiced it. I was not angry in the way I had feared I would be. What I felt was colder and more useful, and it had columns.

I decided nothing that night, and I am proud of that. A person who decides while her hands are shaking has to fix the decision later. I only wrote the date, and under it the words "Guidelines, number six." Then I thought of what Gerald used to say, so often it was practically our family motto. Family is who shows up with a casserole and a ladder.

I sat with that for a while, and then my phone rang. It was a quarter to nine, and the name on the screen was Trevor's, but it was not Trevor.

"Gran?" Jonah said, in the smallest voice I have ever heard him use. "Dad said I could call you for two minutes. Are you mad at us?"

"Never, sweetheart," I said. "Not at you."

There was a pause, and I heard him breathing. "Gran, Mommy said you're not family. She said you're extended. Are you still my grandma if you're not family?"

I took off my glasses and rubbed my nose. A hundred wrong answers went by, and I let all of them go.

"Jonah," I said, "I was your grandmother before anyone put a sign on a door, and I will be your grandmother after every sign is gone. That is not a thing anybody gets to vote on. Do you understand?"

"I think so," he said. "Gran, can I still have a lemon bar on Sunday?"

"You can have two," I said. "Now go to sleep."

At eight o'clock the next morning, a Monday, there was a knock on my front door, and when I opened it, Trevor stood on my porch with a leather folder under his arm and a smile on his face that was a size too large for his face.

"Hi, Mom," he said brightly. "I know it's early. It's just some paperwork, for the refinance. The title company needs you to sign one little thing."
