# Lakeside Cedar Retreat

A white box had just slid down the top of my son's old tablet, and I stood there a full minute before I touched it. A nurse learns to look before she puts her hands on anything.

Then I put on my reading glasses and picked it up.

Craig never set a code on that tablet. My son has never once believed a thing could be taken from him, so the screen opened for me like a door opens for a neighbor. The notification was from a booking service I had never heard of. The page it led to was titled Lakeside Cedar Retreat, and it was written in the bright, breathless voice of a person who sells candles on the side.

Historic cedar lake house on Wassumkeag Cove. Sleeps eight. Private dock, wood stove, classic red canoe. Six hundred dollars a night, two-night minimum. Book your memories now.

Historic. I read that word twice. Frank built the house in nineteen eighty-nine, and he was so proud of it that he made everybody stand on the porch for a picture, including the mailman.

The photographs were worse. There was the red canoe, lying in the grass with a bowl of lemons set on the middle seat, which is the most ridiculous thing I have ever seen done to a boat Frank Ellery built with his own hands. There was the mudroom, with the forty years of boots along the wall. The caption under it said, Rustic charm, ready for your adventure.

Then came my bedroom.

They had taken off my quilt, the one my sister-in-law made the year we married, and put on a plain white comforter, folded back in a triangle at the corner the way they do it in hotels. Somebody had moved my reading glasses off the nightstand. I could see the faint ring in the dust where the lamp used to sit. The caption read, Primary suite, with lake views and a king bed.

The bed is a queen. I know that because Frank carried it up those stairs himself.

I scrolled. There was a calendar, the kind with colored bars across the days. One bar ran from the thirtieth of December through the third of January, and beside it, in a little box, was a name I knew: Whitcomb Events. Eight guests. Deposit paid in full, four thousand eight hundred dollars. Somebody had been very organized about my New Year's.

After the calendar came an email thread, and the name at the top was Pinecrest Commons. I know that place. Every nurse in Maine knows the places where people go to be looked after. I had visited three of my old teachers there, with a box of paperback mysteries and a bag of hard candy, and I had watched each of them learn to ask for help one humiliating small piece at a time. The first message in the thread was from Craig.

We're looking for a room for my mother, starting in January. She's very independent and very private, and she'll need someone to look in on her a couple of times a day. Her lake house is going to be a short-term rental, so she'll need something smaller in town. I'm sure she'll be thrilled.

The reply came back in under an hour from an admissions coordinator who signed herself with a first name only. We have a room available January fifteenth. Would your mother like to come see it?

Craig had written back, "She'll come around. She always does."

I took my glasses off and cleaned them on my fleece. Then I put them back on, because a chart is no place to quit halfway.

The last thing on the tablet was a group chat, the same one called Ellery Family Feast, except that this one had only three people in it. Tyler, Brooke, and Craig. The messages were from Thursday afternoon, while I was basting a turkey for nobody.

Tyler: Grandma's house is going to be insane for New Year's.

Brooke: wait does grandma know about any of this

Craig: Not yet. I'll tell her Friday. It's no big deal.

Tyler: lol

Craig: She won't even be there, she'll be settled by then.

Brooke: that's not what I asked

Craig: Brooke.

That was the end of it. No one had written anything else. I sat there with the tablet in my hands and the stove ticking in the other room, and I thought about the boy with the sprained wrist, and his mother saying, Dot, he does that.

Here is how I read when I'm frightened, which is not often. I read the way I read a chart at the nurses' station, top to bottom, dates first, then numbers, then the words people use when they think nobody is checking. I got the legal pad from the drawer by the telephone, the one Frank used for dock measurements, and I wrote everything down in two columns. I wrote the word primary and underlined it twice.

I did not cry. I would like to tell you that was strength. Mostly I was too busy counting bedrooms.

By eight o'clock the legal pad had two full pages on it. I put on my barn coat, took the flashlight from the hook by the door, and walked down to the dock. The night was clear and cold, and the stars were coming out over the cove one at a time.

Out at the end of the dock, standing on one of the old piling posts in the glow from Amos's porch light, was a great blue heron. I would have sworn it was asleep. I stopped where the planks began and held the light low so I wouldn't blind it. It did not move. It was standing on one leg with its neck folded down into its shoulders, as if it had been waiting a long time for somebody to make up their mind.

I watched it for a while. A heron doesn't argue with the water. It stands still until the thing it wants comes close enough, and then it takes it, once, with no speech. Nobody was going to make a speech for me, either. Nobody on that tablet had ever once thought to ask me anything. I had been saying yes for so long that the yes had worn a groove in the floor.

"All right," I said to the heron. "I hear you."

It didn't look at me. It lowered its head a fraction, as if that had been the answer it needed, and I walked back up the dock with my flashlight pointed at my own boots.

In the kitchen, I looked at the clock over the sink. It said two minutes to nine. I found Nan Pelletier's number in my phone, under her full name and under a note I'd typed years ago, which said, Answers on the first ring. Then I sat down at Frank's table, put the legal pad in front of me, and pressed the button to call.
