# Table Fourteen

The week before the wedding, when Richard Ashford asked why the mother of the groom was at table fourteen, I told him to leave my seat exactly where it was. He grumbled, but he agreed.

The wedding was on Saturday, September twentieth. I put on my navy dress, the one with the three-quarter sleeves that Sam always said made me look like a senator's wife, and I fastened his wristwatch over my cuff, and I drove out to Harbor Point Estate with a box of tissues and no food of any kind. I had promised.

It is a beautiful place. A long green lawn ran down to Lake Mendota, and the water was the color of a new nickel, and on the lawn stood rows of white chairs with sprigs of sage tied to each one. Beyond them was the tent, enormous and white, with lanterns strung inside it. A boy in a vest handed me a program, and I looked for the word kringle in it, and of course it was not there.

The ceremony was at four. They seated me in the front row, which I appreciated, and which I think was Richard's doing, because Courtney had her mother sit up there with me. Gregory came down the lawn in a gray suit with his best man beside him, and I could see from fifty feet away that his hands were shaking. He kept opening and closing them, the way he did when he was nine years old and about to recite something at school. When he reached the front, he looked at me for exactly one second, and I gave him the smallest nod I had. He swallowed and nodded back.

Courtney came down the aisle in a gown that must have cost more than my first car, and she was very lovely. I want to say that plainly. I was not blind to it, and I did not begrudge it. She said her vows in a clear voice, and Gregory said his in a voice that cracked on the word promise, and I cried a little into a tissue, because whatever else was true, he was my boy, standing in the sun, getting married.

Then came the cocktail hour on the lawn, with servers in black going around with tiny things on spoons. I stood holding a glass of sparkling water, and three of Courtney's friends drifted over. They wore linen and pointed their phones at everything they ate.

"You're Gregory's mom," said one of them, a tall girl with a gold necklace shaped like a fork. "So lovely. And what do you do?"

"I worked in food service," I said.

"Oh, like a cafeteria?"

"Something like that," I said. "For a good many years."

She patted my arm and said that must have been so interesting, and drifted away with her friends. The forty thousand dollar card was in my purse, I should tell you. I had carried it all the way from home, and I did not yet know what I would do with it.

It was then that Richard found me. He is a large man, built like a refrigerator in a gray suit, and he walked across the lawn with a paper plate in each hand and no patience for the spoons. He set one of the plates down on the bench beside me, and I saw that it held six of the little crab cakes. He sat. The bench groaned.

"I do not do fuss," he said. "Eat, Nora. There are seconds."

We ate crab cakes in the sun and said very little. Then, looking at the lake, he said, "Gregory told me once that his mother makes the best pot roast in the state of Wisconsin. He said it at Easter, in front of Courtney, and then he looked as if he had said a swear word."

"He was being kind," I said.

"No. He was being honest, which is rarer." Richard wiped his fingers on his napkin. "I started as a stock boy, Nora. I know what it is to have people ashamed of where you came from." He looked at me with his small, shrewd eyes. "Do you know anything about this caterer?"

"I've heard they're very good," I said.

"Hm," said Richard Ashford.

At six o'clock, the guests went into the tent, and I found table fourteen, which was exactly where the map had put it. It stood near the corner where a canvas wall met two swinging doors, and every time a server pushed through, the doors flapped and I got a draft of warm air that smelled of browned onions and sherry. My table companions were the two bankers, who were polite, and Richard's cousin, a quiet woman in lavender who ate her bread roll in three exact bites. We introduced ourselves. I said my name was Nora and that I was Gregory's mother, and the cousin said, "Oh, you poor thing, you're by the doors," and I said I rather liked the doors.

The beet and burrata salad came first. It was a pretty plate, and it was Marco's own, and I would not have changed one leaf of it.

Then the doors swung open, and the second course came out on a tray balanced high on a server's hand, and the smell reached me before the plate did. I knew it with my whole body. It was the horseradish cream and the browned onion and the sweet, dark pan gravy that my husband's mother had written out on an index card in nineteen fifty-one. The plate was set in front of me, and I looked down at my own pot roast, wearing a little sprig of thyme and a new name.

The banker on my left took one bite and stopped chewing. "I'm sorry," he said, "but this is the best thing I have ever eaten at a wedding."

"Is it?" I said.

"It tastes like Sunday," said the cousin in lavender, almost to herself, and she put her fork down and then picked it up again.

From the head table I heard Courtney's voice, high and bright, carrying across the tent. "This is what I mean!" she cried to her friends. "This is what authentic actually tastes like!" Her friends held up their phones and photographed the short rib from above, as though it were a small animal that might get away.

I cut a piece, and it was tender, and the gravy was exactly right. I ate it all. Over by the kitchen doors, one of the swinging panels opened a few inches and a big man in whites looked out, found me in the crowd, and winked. Then the door swung shut again.

The plates were cleared. The lanterns came up. And at the head table, the best man tapped a glass and told a long story about a canoe, and finished with a joke that landed softly, and sat down. A little ripple of applause went around the tent.

Then my son stood up, a glass in one hand, and reached out for the microphone.
