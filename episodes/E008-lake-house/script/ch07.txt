# The Kitchen Table Closing

Friday morning I had a kitchen table, a percolator full of coffee that would make a grown man weep, and a stack of papers that would turn a neighbor into an owner before nine o'clock. I had not slept more than an hour, but I had been awake long enough to feel clear, which is a different thing.

Nan came in at a quarter to six with a thermos under her arm and snow in her hair. "Happy Black Friday," she said.

"I don't do Black Friday."

"Neither do I, Dot. I do closings." She laid out the papers in an order I could follow: the purchase agreement, the deed transfer, the disclosure form, and a one-page waiver for Amos. Then she pulled a single sheet from the bottom of the stack, headed Reserved Property.

It listed the red canoe. It listed one wood stove, cast iron, installed in nineteen eighty-nine. It listed three notebooks, marked dock and tide. It listed nineteen sacks of boots.

"You want the boots in writing?" Nan asked.

"I want the boots in writing, Nan."

"Then you have them in writing."

At eight forty, Ray Gagnon came up the drive in a blue pickup with an envelope held flat against his chest. He was Amos's son-in-law, a broad, quiet man. He shook my hand at the door and said, "Missus Ellery," and I said, "Dot, please, Ray. I pulled a splinter out of your thumb when you were fourteen."

"I remember," he said, looking, for a second, embarrassed about his thumb.

He opened the envelope and handed me the check. It was a cashier's check, printed on heavy paper, from the credit union in town. I read the number out loud, the way I used to read a child's temperature back to the nurse, so there would be no question about what I'd heard. "Six hundred and fifty thousand dollars."

"You want to count it?" Ray said.

"I trust Nan."

"Good," said Ray. "Me too."

Amos came in behind him, stamping snow off his boots, the plaid hat still on. He had a fountain pen in his shirt pocket, the kind he uses to keep the ledger on his boat, and he took it out and uncapped it before he sat down. He laid one hand flat on the table first, the way Frank used to touch a hull before launching it. Then he signed the waiver in big, slow letters, the same way he marks his lobster tags. It took him a long time, and nobody hurried him.

"He'd have liked the check," Amos said quietly, looking at the signature rather than at me.

"He'd have made a joke about the roof."

"He would," said Amos. He capped his pen. "He'd have said it was a good roof."

Then it was my turn. I signed where Nan put the little yellow flags, and I initialed the boot list, which I was pleased to do. I counted the signatures out of habit. There were eleven. Frank used to say a signature was just a promise with a tail on it. "You're a professional," Nan said.

Nan pressed her rubber stamp onto each page, and each time it made a soft, flat sound, like a small door closing somewhere in the house. When the last page was stamped, she looked up at me over her reading glasses.

"Done," she said. "Dorothy Ellery, you're no longer the owner of a lake house on Wassumkeag Cove. Amos Thibodeau and Raymond Gagnon are. Congratulations, both of you."

Ray let out a breath, as if he'd been holding it since ten the night before. Amos said, "Well," and put his hat a little straighter.

The moving truck arrived at nine thirty, driven by Ray's son Kyle, who is nineteen and has his grandfather's shoulders. He backed it down the gravel with his arm out the window, which Frank would have admired. Then he looked at the canoe.

"Missus Ellery," he said, "this thing weighs as much as a Labrador."

"It's a Frank Ellery canoe, Kyle. It has opinions."

It took the two of them to strap the canoe over the cab. The wood stove took longer. Ray went up a ladder with a wrench, and Kyle wrapped each section of pipe in a moving blanket, and soot came down like black snow onto the rug. I didn't mind. The rug was going to the dump, and I had decided that before breakfast.

At ten, Jenna pulled in behind the truck in an old minivan. She is Ray's daughter and Amos's granddaughter. Her husband was at the boatyard, so she had come with the children, a boy of five with a cowlick and a girl of three holding a plastic flashlight, and a brown puppy named Biscuit, who went straight for the dock.

The children ran down the planks shrieking, and the puppy chased them, and the heron, which I had not seen since dawn, lifted off a piling at the far end of the cove and went away low over the ice without any hurry at all. I stood at the head of the dock and watched it go.

The house got louder in that hour than it had been in six years. Two small voices came through the screen door, and the puppy's nails clicked on the kitchen floor, and somebody dropped a spoon in the sink. Frank would have liked that. He never wanted a house that was only for him.

I went back inside to the kitchen table and took out an index card from my purse. I had been thinking about what to write since two in the morning, and I had got it down to the smallest number of words I could manage. I wrote it in my neatest school nurse printing, the kind parents could read from across the room.

Frank Ellery built this house with his own two hands in nineteen eighty-nine, and it was loved here every single day. Please keep loving it on purpose, because a house is a place to be loved in, not a thing to hold on to.

I propped the card against Frank's brass compass on the mantel, where the first person through the front door would see it. Then I picked up my cocoa and went out to the dock.

Ray and Kyle pulled out at ten past ten with the canoe on top and the stove in the back. Across the cove, Amos raised one hand off his bucket, and I raised mine back.

At ten thirty-five I was standing on my dock with my back to the house, holding a mug of cocoa that had gone lukewarm, when a silver SUV came around the bend in the gravel. I could see my son's face through the windshield before he could see mine.
