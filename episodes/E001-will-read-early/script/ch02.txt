# Little Colored Dots

For weeks after Walter's memorial lunch, I did not say one word to anybody about the spreadsheet on Brittany's tablet. I had only seen it for as long as it takes to read a recipe card before she came hurrying back for it. But I had seen enough. There were three columns, called Item, Value, and Who, and my whole life ran down the left side of the screen.

I did not have it out with them. A kitchen teaches you to find where a bad smell is coming from before you start throwing things away. So I waited and watched and kept my mouth shut, which my children will tell you is not my natural state.

The Saturday after the lunch, Brittany called me from the highway.

"I'm coming to help you declutter, Mama Di," Brittany said. "This house is just so much for one person. We'll lighten the load a little."

In seventeen years of marriage to my son, Brittany had never once driven down to see me on her own. Now she came three Saturdays in a row, with plastic bins, garbage bags, and a label maker she carried like a purse. She went through my closets and the cedar chest at the foot of my bed. She held up Walter's flannel shirts one at a time and said, "Donate?" in a voice that was not really a question, and one at a time I took them back and hung them up again.

On the second Saturday, after she had gone, I found the first dot. I was dusting the back of the china hutch, which I do twice a year whether it needs it or not, and low on one side, where nobody would look, was a round sticker the size of a dime. It was blue, the kind of sticker you buy by the sheet to price things for a yard sale.

Once I knew to look, I found them everywhere. There was a blue dot on the back of the grandfather clock. There were green dots on my jewelry box, my sewing machine, and my grandmother's quilt. And out in the garage, there were yellow dots on the lawn mower, the chest freezer, and Walter's workbench.

It did not take a bookkeeper to work it out, but it helped to be one. Brittany had admired that hutch and that clock for years, so blue was Kevin. Rachel had asked about the ring, and the jewelry box was green, so green was Rachel. The yellow dots were all in the garage, on things with grease on them, and I knew without being told which of my children the others thought belonged out there.

"What are all those little stickers, honey?" I asked Brittany on the third Saturday.

"Oh, that's just my inventory system, Mama Di," Brittany said. "For insurance. Everybody should have a home inventory, especially at your age."

I told her that sounded very organized, which was true. Brittany is the most organized person I have ever known. So is a squirrel in October.

That same week, Rachel called on a weeknight, which she never does.

"Mom, a friend from work is getting married at the end of October," Rachel said. "I've got a navy dress, and I was thinking Grandma's sapphire ring would be perfect with it. Could I borrow it, just for the wedding?"

I knew exactly why she wanted that ring. I had heard her ask for it from my own hallway. But a mother is allowed one test, and I decided this would be mine.

Rachel drove over that Sunday to get it. I set the ring in her palm at the kitchen table and told her what she already knew: that my father bought it in nineteen fifty-one with a summer's worth of hay money, and that my mother wore it every day until her knuckles got too big, and then on a chain around her neck until she died.

"Bring it back to me after the wedding," I said.

"Of course, Mom," Rachel said, and she closed her hand around it.

I will tell you now that the wedding came and went, and the ring did not come back. When I asked, Rachel said she kept forgetting and would bring it at Thanksgiving. She could not stay for supper that Sunday, either, because it was a long drive.

Tommy stayed. Tommy always stays. That morning I had found him on his knees on the porch with a new board and his drill. The second step had been soft since August. I had not mentioned it to a soul, because Tommy does not need to be told things.

Olivia had come down for the weekend too, and that afternoon the two of us made a sour cherry pie, Walter's favorite. She rolled the crust and I talked, which is how I like to teach. While it baked, Olivia found the blue dot on the hutch and touched it with one finger.

"Mom uses these," Olivia said quietly. "She color-codes everything."

I said I had gathered that, and she was quiet for a while afterward.

We ate supper at Walter's table, the three of us, with the pie still warm enough that the cherries ran. Tommy had two pieces and pretended it was one.

"Grandma, what was the craziest day you ever had at the Blue Kettle?" Olivia asked.

"The blizzard," Tommy said, smiling.

The winter Tommy was six, a snowstorm closed the interstate, and by suppertime we had thirty-eight truck drivers in a diner with twenty-two stools. Walter cooked for two days without going home. I baked pies at two in the morning until we ran out of cherries, and then I made vinegar pie, and not one of those men complained. Tommy slept in the back booth under his father's coat. One of those drivers sent us a Christmas card every year for twenty years.

Olivia wrote the whole story in her recipe notebook. She gets that from her grandfather.

That night, after Tommy and Olivia had gone, I took Walter's big flashlight, a pencil, and one of his spiral notebooks, and I went through my own house like an insurance adjuster. I counted twenty-six blue dots, eleven green, and four yellow. I wrote each one down, room by room, like Sunday inventory at the Blue Kettle.

The last one I found by accident. I dropped the pencil under the kitchen table, and when I got down on my knees to fetch it, I shined the flashlight up at the underside. Walter had burned his name and the year into the wood there, where nobody but the two of us knew to look. Right beside his name was a little blue dot.

I stayed on my knees on that kitchen floor for a long time. Getting up off a floor is a project at sixty-nine, but that was not why I stayed. I left the dot exactly where it was, because I wanted to remember where Brittany had put it.

The phone rang the next morning while I was still in my robe. It was Kevin, and he was using the voice.

"Mom," Kevin said, soft and soothing, "Brittany and I are coming Saturday. We need to talk about your future. We're bringing someone."
