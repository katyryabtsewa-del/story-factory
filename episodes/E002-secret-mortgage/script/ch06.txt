# Seven Candles

On Saturday, October twenty-fourth, at a quarter to two in the afternoon, I parked my Buick on Larkspur Court with a lemon cake on the passenger seat and a blue folder in my purse, and I walked toward the party I had been told not to attend.

You could hear it from the corner. There was a pink and gold balloon arch over the driveway and a bounce house shaped like a castle swaying in the front yard. Forty guests, I would learn later, which included neighbors, mothers from Piper's school, a photographer, and what I can only call Vanessa's audience, a dozen women who held their drinks in one hand and their phones in the other.

Vanessa met me at the bottom of the porch steps. She wore a white dress and a ribbon in her hair, and she had arranged herself so that her body filled the gap between the railing and the door.

"Helen," she said, in a voice made entirely of sugar. "This is a private event."

"I know it is," I said. "I brought a cake."

"We have a cake. We have a whole dessert table." She glanced over my shoulder at the guests on the lawn, then leaned in, and the sugar went out of her voice. "I told you not to come. I won't have a scene."

"I'm not here for a scene, Vanessa. I was invited."

"By whom?"

"By the birthday girl."

The screen door banged open before she could answer, and out shot a small person in a lemon-yellow dress with her hair in two lopsided braids. She hit me at the knees so hard that the cake carrier swung on my arm. "Nana! You came! You came, you came, you came!" She wrapped both arms around my waist. "That's one hug. That's two hugs. That's three."

"Seven hugs, sweetheart, because it's your birthday," I said, and she squeezed me for four more, counting each one out loud, while behind her the whole lawn turned and looked, and a dozen telephones lifted a little way into the air.

Vanessa's smile came back on like a porch light. She always knows where the cameras are. "Of course," she said, loudly enough for the lawn. "Piper wanted her grandmother. Helen, put the cake on the side table, and please, help yourself to some punch."

Help yourself. It was another way of saying sometimes. I took the cake around to the backyard, where the swing set stood among the string lights and a banner over the long table said, Piper Is Seven. Derek himself was at the grill in an apron, turning hot dogs. He saw me, and the tongs stopped in the air. He did not smile, but he did not look away either, and I took that for something.

The children went off to the bounce house in a shrieking wave. I put the cake down at the end of the dessert table, beside a tower of pastel macarons, and I stood quietly under a maple tree with my purse in both hands. I waited.

At half past two, Bruce Whitfield stepped up onto the patio with a champagne flute and tapped it with a fork. He wore an orange golf shirt and the shine of a man who had been toasting since noon.

"Everybody, everybody, quiet down. A few words." The yard obeyed him, as yards do. "Today my granddaughter is seven years old, and I want to say, I could not be prouder of my daughter and her husband. They've built something beautiful here." He swept the flute at the house, the lawn, the deck, the glass. "And I'll tell you something. My wedding gift to these two, the money my wife and I put in at the start, is in every board of this house. Every nail. Best money I ever spent. To family!"

"To family!" shouted forty people, and the glasses went up, and Vanessa put her hand to her heart and beamed at her father with wet eyes. Beside the grill, Derek stared at the hot dogs.

I walked across the grass. I did not hurry. I came up beside Bruce Whitfield, and he turned with the flute still in his hand, and I said, in my ordinary voice, "Mister Whitfield, I wonder if you'd like to see the deed."

He laughed, a big hearty laugh, because he assumed I was being cute. "The deed! Hey, everybody, Derek's mother here has the deed! Well, let's have a look!" He had decided it was a joke.

Vanessa had crossed the yard faster than I thought she could, and she took hold of my elbow. "Helen, not now," she said through her teeth.

"Oh, let her, honey," said Bruce, delighted. "I'd love to hear it. Nobody's afraid of a piece of paper."

The children were all in the bounce house, and I was glad of that. I stepped up onto the first stair of the patio, took out my reading glasses, opened the blue folder, and used the voice I used to use across a lunchroom of four hundred children, which has never needed a microphone.

"This is the deed to four hundred eighteen Larkspur Court, recorded in November of twenty twenty. It names the owners as Derek Brandt and Helen Brandt, joint tenants with right of survivorship. That is me. Those are the only two names on it."

The yard had gone very quiet.

"This is the loan. Two hundred eighty-six thousand dollars. The borrowers are Derek Brandt and Helen Brandt. This is the agreement my son signed in that same month, which says that the fifty-two thousand dollars of down payment came from me, from my husband's life insurance, and is to be repaid to me first." I turned a page. "And this is a card Derek wrote me, in his own hand. It says, Mom, thank you. Please don't tell V. I'll make it right when I'm back on my feet."

Vanessa made a small sound.

"And these," I said, lifting the stack of stamped pages, "are my bank records. One thousand six hundred and forty dollars, on the first of every month, for seventy-one months. More than a hundred and sixteen thousand dollars, from my savings. Not one dollar of it came from anyone named Whitfield."

"That's a lie." Vanessa's voice cracked down the middle of the word. "She's lying. It's forged, she's confused, she's a lonely woman who." She spun around, and her ribbon came loose. "Derek. Derek! Tell them. Tell them she's lying."

Forty people turned toward the grill. My son stood there with the tongs hanging at his side and the smoke going up past his shoulder. He looked at his wife, and at his father-in-law, and at me. A hot dog began to burn.

Then Derek put down the tongs. "It's true," he said. His voice was thin, but every person in the yard heard him. "Van, it's all true."

I will remember Vanessa's face for the rest of my life. It did not crumple. It simply went blank, like a screen with the power cut. And in that stillness Bruce Whitfield lowered his glass, and in a voice I had not heard from him all year, small and flat and very quiet, he said, "Vanessa. Did I give you a dime?"
