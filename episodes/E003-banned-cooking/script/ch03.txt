# Corso

When my son said the word Corso over my pot roast, I set my fork down on the edge of my plate very gently, the way you put down something that might go off.

"That's wonderful, honey," I said. "I hear they're very good."

"They're the best in the city, Mom." Gregory glanced at the porch, where Courtney was still arguing about peonies, and he lowered his voice. "I almost said no, on account of you. But Courtney wants the best, and it is the best." He rubbed the back of his neck. "She does not know Marco used to work for you and Dad. She thinks he is just a famous chef. Could we leave the old days out of it? It would be easier all around."

I had not promised him anything, and I noticed that he had not noticed. I asked if anyone wanted more horseradish cream, and said nothing more about it. Gregory looked so relieved that I almost felt sorry for him.

After they left, I washed the dishes by hand, dried each one, and put it away. Then I sat down at the yellow kitchen table and laughed, quietly, for about a minute, because I could not think of anything else to do with it.

Let me tell you about Marco Reyes.

He walked into Pruitt's Table on a Tuesday in the fall of nineteen ninety-six, nineteen years old, carrying a green duffel bag that looked as if it belonged to somebody else. He asked if we needed a dishwasher. Sam looked him over and said, "Can you stand up for eight hours, chief?" Marco said he could stand up for twelve. Sam hired him that afternoon.

He washed dishes for six months, and he watched my hands more than he watched the sink. One slow Wednesday I caught him studying a whole chicken as if it were a puzzle, so I handed him my boning knife and said, "Come here. I'll show you where it comes apart." He broke down that bird badly, and then forty more that week. I taught him pot roast and gravy and how to bring a pie crust together without warming it with your hands. Within five years he ran the line, and for fifteen years after that he ran my whole kitchen. He taught my nine-year-old son to crack an egg with one hand, and he came to Sam's funeral in a borrowed tie. He called me Chef, which nobody else ever had, and sometimes Missus Pruitt when he wanted a favor.

Marco is the closest thing to a second son I ever had, which is a hard thing to say about a man when the real one has just asked you to leave the old days out of it.

When Sam got sick, we sold the building. You can love a place with your whole heart and still not own the stove. The sale left me one hundred eighty thousand dollars after the debts. A smaller woman would have bought an annuity. But Marco had a dream of his own, a restaurant with copper pans on Williamson Street. He came to my kitchen one night and did not ask me for money. He only said he wanted me to eat in his place someday.

I put the whole sum into it. He insisted on every piece of paper. We signed in blue ink at a lawyer's, and when it was done I owned forty percent of Corso Hospitality, a limited liability company, which means the restaurant and the catering company both. Sam signed as a witness at our kitchen table with a blanket over his knees, and he joked that he had finally gotten a restaurant he did not have to clean. That page lives in my fireproof box.

In nine years I have never attended a Corso event as an owner. I get a check every year, last year it was ninety-four thousand dollars, and I go to the owners' meeting every January, just Marco and the accountant and me, at the restaurant, before the lunch rush. Afterward I eat in the back booth, where nobody bothers me. Gregory knew Marco had opened a place, and he believed I had lent him a small sum that was long since paid off. I had never corrected him, because he had never asked. When you spend years learning that your son is embarrassed by the work you did, you stop volunteering the work you do.

The Monday after the pot roast, I drove to Williamson Street at ten in the morning with no appointment. The restaurant was closed, so I went around the side to the Corso Events office, where I had been exactly twice.

A young woman looked up from a tablet. She had a clipboard under her arm, black hair pulled back, and a lanyard that said Priya Nair, Events Manager. "Can I help you?" she said, and then her eyes went past my shoulder to the photograph on the wall behind me. It was a framed picture of a young man with a mustache and a woman in an apron, standing in front of Pruitt's Table. She looked back at me, and at the photograph, and at me again.

"Oh my goodness," she said. "You're her. You're the Chef."

"I'm Nora," I said. "Is Marco in?"

Priya had been at Corso for two years and had never met me, because my January meetings fell on mornings when she was on the road. Marco had been away in Spain most of April, and she had signed the Ashford contract on her own. She had seen a Missus Nora Pruitt listed as mother of the groom and had wondered, but had not wanted to bother anybody on a guess.

Marco came out of the walk-in cooler in his whites, wiping his hands, and he saw me and opened both arms. "Chef!" he said. "Is it January already? Did I lose a whole season?"

"No," I said. "Sit down, Marco. I need to ask you about a wedding."

We took the back booth in the empty restaurant. I told him about the lunch, the text, and Gregory leaning across my table. Marco listened with his big hands folded, and the longer I talked, the redder his neck got. When I finished, Priya slid into the booth beside him and set a folder in front of me without being asked.

"I think you should see section nine," she said.

I read it twice. The clause read, "No outside food or beverage. Family members of the couple may not enter service or kitchen areas." In the margin, someone had typed a note that read, "Added at client's request, Courtney Ashford."

"Courtney was very specific," Priya said quietly. "She did not want any relatives wandering into the kitchen trying to help."

Marco pushed his chair back so hard that it scraped. "Chef," he said, "say the word, and I walk out of that contract today."

I smoothed the tablecloth with the flat of my hand. It was an old habit, and it told them both to listen.

"Marco," I said, "don't change one thing."
