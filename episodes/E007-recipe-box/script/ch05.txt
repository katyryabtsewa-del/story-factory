# Card One Hundred Seventeen

The pale thing against the back of the tin turned out to be a sandwich bag, but before I could get at it I had to get two hundred and twelve cards out of the weather.

Del Okafor, who runs the Larkin County transfer station the way other men run a submarine, took one look at my lap and went into the scale house. He came out with clean brown paper and binder clips, wiped the picnic table under the little tin awning with his sleeve, and said, "Lay them out. One layer. Don't stack."

So I did. I lifted the cards out in handfuls and laid them down in rows like a woman dealing the world's biggest hand of solitaire. The ink had run on nearly all of them. The blue ballpoint had gone to clouds, the felt tips to bruises. One card in violet had bled out across its whole face like a pressed flower, and I held it a long minute while Del went and stood by his truck so I could have the table.

It was card one hundred seventeen, Hilda's rhubarb crisp, half-pulped and soft as a leaf. I turned it over carefully, and underneath it, where the dividers in the tin had held it flat for forty years, the cardboard lining at the back of the box had a slit cut in it, as straight as a ruler. I had put my hand in that tin every Christmas for decades and had never once known there was a slit.

Out of it I drew the sandwich bag.

It was a plain zip bag, folded over twice, and inside it were three index cards and a folded sheet of lined paper, all of it dry. Walt knew about damp. I opened the bag with fingers that did not feel like mine and unfolded the paper, and there it was in carpenter's pencil, in the blocky, leaning capitals I would know on the moon. The pencil had not run at all.

I read it standing at the picnic table in the rain, and I will give it to you the way it was written.

"Win. If you are reading this, the box got thrown out, or it got found, either way you're holding it, which means you went looking. Good. Don't be mad at Tessa. Folks who throw things out are usually afraid of something. Go and get the red toolbox before somebody sells it. The dots are porches. You know every one of them better than I do. I never could tell you, because you'd have told them thank you, and they'd have felt small. Walk the route first, Win. Then decide."

And down at the bottom, squeezed in sideways along the edge: "P.S. Card one hundred seventeen is the best crisp in the county and you still can't make it."

I laughed. People expect a woman in my position to cry, and I did that later. First I laughed, out loud, at a county dump at seven in the morning, with the rain dripping off my braid. Walt had written to me from beyond the grave, and the first thing he told me was that I couldn't cook.

Then I cried, a good, long, loud cry. Del came over, set his thermos at my elbow without a word, and went away again.

When I could see again, I read the letter a second time. Don't be mad at Tessa. He had written that before the clipboard and the canisters and the dumpster, but he had seen something in her, and he had known before any of us.

Then I looked at the dots. In the top right corner of card one hundred seventeen, in the same pencil as the letter, there was a very small dot. I had never noticed it. Nobody would have. It was no bigger than a pinprick, and on all those soaked cards, pencil was the only mark that had not run.

I went down the rows with my reading glasses on their beaded chain and counted. A dot on the Voss card. A dot on the corn fritters, Ila Fenn, mile marker twenty-two. A dot on Dewey Marsh's cabbage rolls, and one on a card from the Lund twins' mother. I counted twice, because when you carry the mail you count everything twice. There were nineteen cards with a dot, and I did not need the letter to tell me what they were. They were porches, and I had stood on every one.

I was holding the last of them when a pair of headlights came across the lot, slow, with their wipers going.

It was a gray sedan I knew, and the man who climbed out wore a yellow rain jacket still creased from the store. Greg did not wave. He walked across the wet gravel and stopped at the end of the picnic table and looked at the rows of ruined cards and then at me, in my dead husband's boots, with garbage in my braid.

My son did not say one word, and neither did I. He took off the jacket, which was the only dry thing for a mile, and held it over the cards so the rain would stop falling on them. He stood like that for ten minutes with his shirt going dark, and he did not once look at his watch. I have thought about it every day since.

"The kitchen light was on when I got there," he finally said. "I'd come to tell you I talked to her. The receipt was on the counter."

"I figured," I said.

We dried the cards. We pressed them between Del's brown paper, and Greg held the clips and I laid the sheets, and neither of us mentioned the letter, the listing, or the paper he had signed. It was the quietest hour I have ever spent with my son, and I would not trade it. When the tin had drained I tipped it up to shake out the last of the water, and something small slid across the lid and clinked on the table. A brass key, taped inside the lid with a strip of masking tape, the same tape Walt used for my mile marker tabs.

Greg picked it up. "It's a padlock key, Mom," he said. "A small one."

"I know," I said. I looked at the letter, at the line near the middle. Go and get the red toolbox before somebody sells it.

Inside the scale house window there was a paper calendar from the Feed and Seed, and I walked over to it with the key still in my hand. Today was Tuesday, the thirteenth of May.

Which meant the man from Ames was coming tomorrow at nine o'clock, with a trailer, for my husband's toolbox.
