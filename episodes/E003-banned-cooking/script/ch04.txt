# Don't Change One Thing

I had just told Marco not to change one thing, and now, in the back booth on Williamson Street, I had to explain myself to a man who was ready to burn his own contract down for my sake.

"Chef, with respect," Marco said, "that makes no sense."

"It makes more sense than anything I've done since March," I said. "Sit down, Marco, and let me tell you what I want."

I want you to know that I had thought about revenge by then. I am not a saint, and I had lain awake more than one night imagining a wedding that fell apart in the tent. But I had run a restaurant for a long time, and I knew something about people at tables. A man can argue with a speech. He can argue with a lawyer and a letter and a scene. What he cannot argue with is a plate of food he has already finished and loved.

So I laid it out for them, calmly. I did not want the wedding ruined. It was my son's wedding, and he would only have one of them, God willing, and some part of the boy in the back booth still lived inside him. What I wanted was for every guest at Harbor Point Estate to eat the best meal of their lives, and for Gregory and his bride to praise it with their own mouths, in front of everybody, not knowing whose hands it had come from. Then, whatever happened next would be the truth, told by them.

"And nobody says a word," I said. "Not to the Ashfords, not to the couple. I go as a plain guest. I wear my navy dress, I sit where they put me, and I don't go anywhere near your kitchen. I'd hate to break section nine."

Marco stared at me for a long, slow moment. Then the corners of his mouth began to move, and he put both of his big hands flat on the table. "Then I'm cooking your food," he said.

"I did not say that."

"Chef, you didn't have to. They want elevated and food-literate? I will give them your pot roast, and your gravy, and your kringle, and I will rename every one so nobody can see the seams." He turned to Priya. "Are you following this?"

"I'm following it," Priya said. She had her pen out. "Short rib for the pot roast. Brown butter apple tart for the kringle. Sour cream raisin pie bites for the late-night station." She looked up at me, and her face was entirely serious. "Missus Pruitt, is this legal?"

"Nobody is being cheated," I said. "The client is paying for dinner, and the client is getting the finest dinner Corso can make. I hold forty percent of the company that makes it. Where is the harm?"

Priya smiled for the first time that morning, a small, firm smile. "I'll draw up the menu cards," she said.

That night I told Edith, over lemon bars, with the stove light on. She listened to the entire plan with her glasses on top of her head, and at the end she pulled them down and looked at me.

"Let me make sure I have this right," she said. "You're going to sit at table fourteen, by the kitchen doors, and eat your own pot roast."

"With horseradish cream," I said.

"Under another name."

"Wisconsin braised short rib."

Edith began to laugh, a low rolling sound that shook her whole chair, and she laughed until she had to take off her glasses and wipe her eyes. "Oh, Nora," she said. "Sam would have loved this. He'd have bought a tuxedo."

That was the part I had not let myself feel, and it came up in my throat. "He would have said, chief, let the food do the talking," I said.

"He did say that," Edith said. "Every Friday."

Now I must tell you how the summer went, because for a while nothing at all happened, and that was the hardest part. I answered Gregory's texts, which came every few days and always began with Hi Mom, hope you're feeling good. I did not mention the caterer. I wrote a note for the wedding card, then another, then tore both up. In July I mailed back my reply card and checked the box marked beef, because Priya had told me it would be the short rib.

In August, Courtney and Gregory went to the tasting at Corso without me. I was not invited, and I would not have gone. Priya called me from her car afterward.

"Do you want it word for word, or the short version?" she said.

"Word for word, please."

Priya took a breath. She said Marco had served the three courses on white plates, with the right wine, and had stood at the end of the table with his hands behind his back, not saying anything he did not have to. Courtney took one bite of the short rib and closed her eyes. "This is so elevated," she said. She asked what the sauce was, and Marco said it was a pan gravy with cheese curd and a little sherry, and Courtney said, "That's exactly what I mean by authentic. It has depth. It tastes like somebody's grandmother, but a really talented one."

"A really talented one," I said. "Well. She'd have liked that."

"There's more," Priya said. "Courtney asked where the recipes came from. Marco told her they were old Wisconsin recipes from his family, which is true in a way, Missus Pruitt. He grew up on them in your kitchen."

I did not say anything. I smoothed my kitchen table, though nobody could see me do it.

"And Gregory?" I asked.

"He ate the whole plate," Priya said. "He got quiet. At one point he put his fork down and said it reminded him of something, and he could not think what. Courtney told him it was probably a good restaurant he had been to once. And he said, yes, that must be it."

The summer ended, and the days went shorter and gold, and the week before the wedding, my telephone rang on a Monday evening. It was a number I did not know, with a gruff voice on the other end.

"Nora? It's Richard Ashford, Courtney's father. Gregory gave me your number. I hope that's all right." He cleared his throat. "I'm calling to ask what a man is supposed to wear to one of these things, and I figured you were the only person in the family who would tell me the truth."

I almost laughed. "A dark suit, Richard, and comfortable shoes. You will be standing a long time."

"That's what I thought." He paused, and I could hear a television somewhere behind him being turned down. "One more thing. I looked at the seating chart last night, and I would like to know why the mother of the groom is at table fourteen."
