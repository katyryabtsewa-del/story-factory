# Overflow, Revisited

Nobody tells you how ordinary the rest of it is. After the very polite quiet, there is still a car to drive home.

I stayed one more night at Esther Bloom's, in a spare room lined with chemistry textbooks, until Dmitri swore the one boiler would hold and Mister Ruiz agreed. On Christmas Eve morning I drove home to Fort Wayne in the sun, with the snow squeaking under the tires. I heard later that the cocktail evening in four-B never took place. Mister Tolliver took his eight clients to a restaurant, and Neil and Vanessa spent that night in their living room not saying much, which I gather was a first.

I had told Esther I would take her up on her invitation another year. She said she'd hold me to it, and in March I went, for coffee and a lecture on acidity.

Christmas Day was quiet, and I had no complaint about it. I wore my slippers until noon. About one o'clock my neighbor from across the fence knocked on the back door with a plate under foil, and said, "I heard what you did. I made too much of everything anyway." We sat at the kitchen table, and she told me about her sister in Dayton, and I told her about a pump seal, and neither of us pretended to understand the other's half. The space heater stayed off.

At nine that night the phone rang with the Chicago number, and I answered it a little faster than I'd like to admit. "I wanted you to know, Missus Rudd," Isaiah said, in that slow voice, "that I've been walking the north stairwell every hour with my hand on the pipe. It's warm. All the way up. I just wanted somebody to know."

"Isaiah," I said, "I'm glad you called."

On the twenty-sixth, Neil drove down alone, over the Skyway and the toll road in the thin gray light, and he arrived on my front step at dusk with a bag and a small brass radiator key he had bought at a hardware store in Chicago. He slept on my couch under Ferris's old afghan, and in the morning, in the cold back bedroom, I showed him how to bleed it. You fit the key, you turn it a quarter turn, and you listen. First there is a hiss, a thin sigh of trapped air, and then a splutter, and then, quite suddenly, a bead of hot water, and you close it.

"It works," Neil said. He looked at the drop of water on his thumb as though he had cured a disease.

"It always works," I said. "It's the most honest relationship in the house."

He laughed. Then he told me that Vanessa had stayed in Chicago, and that she had not asked him not to come. I said that was a start.

The note from Vanessa came in the second week of January, in a cream envelope with her initials on the flap. It was three lines long, in her tidy hand.

Constance, I have thought about the lobby and I have thought about the spreadsheet. I was afraid of being caught unprepared, and I let that fear decide things it should never have been allowed to decide. I am sorry, and I do not expect an answer.

I read it standing at the counter. Then I took the Christmas card out of the drawer under the toaster and mailed it to the two of them, weeks late, with nothing written inside but my name. It seemed to be the right size of answer.

In March, a crane lowered the new Boiler Two through the sidewalk hatch of the Ostrander, on a gray morning with ice still in the gutters, and about sixty neighbors stood on the pavement in their coats to watch it go down. It was green and gleaming, and it swung slowly in the air like a sleeping animal. Dmitri stood beside me with a clipboard, and that same week he was named chief engineer, with a raise he was too polite to mention. Esther chaired the board, and the minutes had become a pleasure to read.

They also gave me a title, and I made them promise not to print it. In the building, I am the boiler godmother. When somebody hears a sound they do not like, they call me, and I tell them what it is, usually in about four minutes, free of charge.

Back in Fort Wayne, on Saturday mornings, I teach a class at the trades program of the community college, called Boilers for Beginners. My first session had twelve women over forty and two teenagers, and a girl of seventeen with a green streak in her hair asked whether I ever got used to the noise. I told her no, and that I hoped she never would, because the noise is the boiler talking. She wrote that down. They all did.

I saw Vanessa in April. She had asked to meet, and I had said I would be at the Ostrander for the spring inspection anyway, so we met in the Gallery, in the middle of the afternoon, when the room was empty and the radiators were only barely warm. She sat in a folding chair with her coat on her lap, and she looked smaller than I remembered, perhaps only because she was no longer at the head table.

She told me about her mother, who had kept their apartment so cold one winter, to save on the bill, that the family wore their coats at the dinner table. She had never said it to anyone, not even Neil. "I told myself it was optics," she said. "It was never optics. I couldn't stand to be caught out."

I let her finish. I did not tell her it was all right, because it wasn't. But I said I would rather have one hard afternoon with her than a year of nice schedules, and something in her shoulders let go.

"I didn't think you'd come," she said. "That night. Nobody would have blamed you for staying home."

"I know," I said. "That's the part I'd like you to think about."

She nodded slowly, and she did not argue, which was the first time I had seen her do that and mean it.

When I left, I stood a minute in the basement by the new boiler, and I listened to the Ostrander the way I listen to every building. There was no knocking, and no gasp, and no silence. There was only an even hum under the floor, and a tick in an iron pipe now and then, like a clock in another room. That is how a building sounds when it is well. It is the sound of nothing happening, and it is the best sound I know.

So I will leave you with a question. If someone you love has told you not to come, but you can hear a sound they can't, do you go? I would like to know your answer, and I would like to know where you are listening from tonight, and whether it is cold there.
