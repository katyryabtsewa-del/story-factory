# What I Could Prove

When my granddaughter asked me whether I had done something bad, I told her the only true thing I could think of. I said, "No, sweetheart, I didn't. Nana loves you to the moon." Then Derek's voice came on the line, low and hurried, saying, "Okay, Piper, give me the phone, time for bed," and the call was over before I could ask him a single question.

I did not sleep that night. At three in the morning I was at my kitchen table with a pot of decaf and the green ledger open in front of me, turning pages. Seventy-one lines, each in my own handwriting, each with a check mark. It is a strange thing to look at six years of your own love written down as arithmetic.

I added the column twice, because I do not trust a number I have only added once. Seventy-one payments, the latest one on the first of October. It came to a hundred sixteen thousand, four hundred and forty dollars, and it was money I had never once been thanked for out loud. My savings account, which had started at two hundred ten thousand dollars, had come down to about sixty-one thousand. I had my pension and my Social Security, and I was not going hungry. But I had been spending my late husband's last gift to me on a house I had just been told I could not enter.

In the morning I drove to Gloria's.

Gloria Pell lives in a brick ranch house with a red Subaru in the driveway and a ceramic goose on the porch that she dresses for the seasons. That week the goose was a pumpkin. She retired from the Buckeye Valley Credit Union after thirty-three years as a loan officer, and in that time she had turned down more bad ideas than a church has hymns. She put me at her kitchen table, poured the coffee, and read the ledger without a word.

When she was done she took off her glasses. "Helen, can I ask you a question you're not going to like?"

"You usually do."

"What happens if you stop paying?"

I had asked myself that at three o'clock that morning and had not liked the answer. "Then the bank calls us both," I said. "I'm a co-borrower. A missed payment lands on my credit and on Derek's, and the house could end up in trouble. That's Piper's bedroom, Gloria."

"That's right," she said. "Which is why they counted on you." She slid her phone across the table. "And look at this. I wasn't going to show you."

It was Vanessa's page. A photograph of the front of the house, golden in the evening light, with a swing set in the side yard and a pumpkin on every step. Under it she had written, "We built this home with our own two hands. No handouts, no shortcuts, just love and hard work. Grateful for every brick." Eleven hundred people had liked it. A woman had commented, "You two are an inspiration!" with three little hearts.

I put the phone down very gently. Gloria is a good friend, and she did not say a word about the hands. She only reached across and pressed hers over mine,, and the goose watched us through the window in its little orange costume.

"What do you want to do?" she asked.

"I want to talk to my son," I said. "He's not a bad boy. He's a soft one. And soft is worse in a family, because you can't even get properly angry at it."

I did not call ahead. Derek works at Tri-County Flooring Supply in a low cinderblock building off the state route, with a showroom up front full of laminate and a warehouse behind it that smells of sawdust and cardboard. On Thursday, October eighth, at half past ten, I walked into the showroom in my good coat. The girl at the counter said, "Can I help you?" and I said, "I'm Derek Brandt's mother," and for once the word did exactly what it should.

He came out from the back with his sleeves rolled up and a pencil behind his ear, and when he saw me his whole face lost its color. "Mom. Mom, please, not here."

"Then where?"

He took me to a little office with a glass wall and a sample board of vinyl plank, and he closed the door and sat down like a man being sentenced. For a minute he said nothing. I let him. A school cafeteria teaches you that if you wait long enough, a nine-year-old will tell you everything.

"I should have told you a long time ago," Derek said at last. "I told Vanessa I do consulting on the side. A hundred ninety thousand dollars a year. Mom, I don't do any consulting. I make seventy-one thousand dollars, and that's all of it."

"Why on earth would you say that?"

"Because she was going to leave." He covered his mouth with his hand. "Not leave me. Leave all of it. When I got laid off, she said she didn't marry a man who couldn't provide, and I said I had a plan, and then the number just came out. And every year it got harder to take back." He looked at the sample board. "She thinks she's on the deed, too. She thinks she's on the loan. I told her it was all handled, and she believed me, and she's never once checked, because she doesn't read things. You read things. She doesn't."

"I know she isn't on the deed, Derek. I was there when we signed it." I kept my voice even. "What I didn't know was that she didn't know."

"She told her parents the down payment was their gift," he said. "She said it at a lunch once, and I didn't correct her, and now her father tells people at the club." He was crying a little by then, quietly, the way he did at seven when he broke something and tried to hide it behind the sofa. "I'm afraid, Mom. I'm so afraid of her finding out. I'll tell her. I will. After Piper's party. I just can't ruin the party."

I did not say what I thought, which was that the ruin had been under construction for years, one small lie at a time, like a house. I only said, "You told your daughter I did something bad."

"I didn't tell her that. Vanessa did." He wiped his face on his sleeve. "I'm sorry. I'll fix it."

I stood up and buttoned my coat. At the door, with his hand already on the knob, my son turned, and he could not quite look at me.

"Mom, one more thing," he said. "We need your signature on something. It's just paperwork."
