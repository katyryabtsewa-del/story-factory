# My Door Is Open

A month after that Thanksgiving, I set Walter's table for Christmas, and this time I only needed one leaf.

The Saturday after Thanksgiving, Olivia and I had gone through the house with a butter knife and peeled forty colored dots off my furniture. I left one, the blue dot under Walter's table, beside his name. I had plans for it.

There were six of us at Christmas, and Walter's chair: Tommy, Olivia, Rachel and her boy, Harold, and me. Kevin and Brittany stayed in Columbus, but Kevin let Olivia drive down, and I took that as a sign of something. Rachel's boy brought his trumpet and played "Silent Night" after the ham, every note a little to one side of where it belonged, and Harold applauded until his bow tie went crooked. It was green, for Christmas. Then Harold opened Brittany's champagne, which had been sitting in my refrigerator for a month, and said it would be a shame to waste a perfectly good payday. The grown-ups had a small glass, and the children had cider.

Rachel has called me every Sunday since Thanksgiving, and that recipe of ours is coming along.

In January, Harold got a letter from the law firm in Columbus, on that same heavy paper, saying the concerned family members no longer wished to pursue the matter. Kevin never did get a real lawyer. I expect he found out what one costs.

The rest I heard from Olivia, a little at a time, on Saturday mornings in my kitchen. In February Kevin sold the boat. In March a sign went up on the front lawn of the big house, and Brittany's realtor friend was smiling on it in his quarter-zip sweater. I hope he told the buyers it had great bones. Brittany went to stay with her sister for a while. Olivia said her mother had grown up in a rented trailer and had promised herself she would never be poor again. It did not excuse one sticker. It did explain a few of them. And on Tuesday nights, Olivia said, her father sat at the kitchen table with a calculator and a financial counselor on the phone, going through every bill he had hidden from us.

On a Sunday in April, Kevin came by himself.

Tommy and I were sitting down to pot roast when a small gray car I did not recognize pulled into the driveway. The big black one had gone back to the dealership. Kevin came up the porch steps holding a store-bought apple pie in a plastic dome, and I could tell from the doorway that the crust had been made by a machine in another state.

"I'm not here to ask for anything, Mom," Kevin said.

I held the door open, and he came in. Tommy got up without a word and pulled out the chair beside his own, and the three of us ate supper at Walter's table.

Over coffee, Kevin told me all of it, and he did not use the voice once.

"Eighty-five thousand dollars," Kevin said. "That's what I owed. The boat, the credit cards, and a bonus the dealership took back. There really was a car wash, Mom, but I was never going to put a dime into it. I needed that sixty thousand to keep the cards quiet."

He looked down at his hands on the oak.

"We told your doctor you were losing your mind so we could sell your house," Kevin said. "I said that out loud to my counselor on Tuesday. It was the first time I ever heard how it sounded."

"Have you said any of this to your brother?" I asked.

"He came by the shop yesterday," Tommy said. "I let him hold the flashlight."

After supper, I told Kevin there was something under the table I had been saving for him. I handed him his father's big flashlight. "Your brother tells me you're good with one of these," I said.

Kevin got down on his knees on my kitchen floor and shined the light up at the underside of the table. I heard him go quiet when he found it, the little blue dot beside his father's name. He peeled it off with his thumbnail, and then he stayed down there for a long time. Getting up off a floor is a project at forty-four too, it turns out, but that was not why he stayed.

On his way out, Kevin stopped with his hand on the screen door.

"Mom," Kevin said, "is it all right if I come back next Sunday?"

"My door is open, Kevin," I said. "My checkbook is not."

Kevin laughed then, a real laugh. "That's fair," Kevin said.

He has come every other Sunday since. He brings a store-bought pie every time, and I eat it. Some pies you eat for the pie, and some you eat for the person who brought it.

In May, Harlan Creek High gave out the first Blue Kettle Scholarship in the gym, which still smells like popcorn. It went to a senior who has cooked breakfast for her three little brothers since she was eleven and wants to open a bakery someday. I gave her my sour cherry recipe along with the check. That same week, Olivia got a letter of her own. She had been accepted to a six-week summer cooking program for high school students, starting in July, and Kevin was the one who drove her to the interview.

And at the start of June, just a few weeks ago, Olivia and I drove to the Grand Canyon.

Walter always wanted to see it. While we had the diner, we could never close it long enough, and after we sold it, there was his heart, and then there was no more time. So Olivia drove most of the two thousand miles, and I read the map out loud, because I do not take directions from a telephone. We had a sour cherry pie in a cooler on the back seat. On the fourth evening, we sat on the rim with two plastic forks and ate it while the sun went down over the canyon. I have always thought sunset was the prettiest part of the day. Nobody on that rim was in any hurry for it.

That night in the motel, I wrote just one line in one of Walter's spiral notebooks: Grand Canyon, four days, one pie, two forks.

When your family decides you are a confused old woman who leaves the stove on, they stop watching you. Being underestimated at sixty-nine turned out to be a kind of freedom. I could go anywhere and look at anything, and not one of them ever thought to wonder what I was writing down.

So now I will ask you, the way I would if you were sitting across Walter's table from me with a cup of coffee and a slice of pie. What would you have done about Kevin? Would you have let him back in that Sunday in April, store-bought pie and all, or would you have left him standing on the porch? I would truly like to know.

And tell me where you are listening from. In all those years behind that counter, I poured coffee for people from three counties and a good many states, and I always liked knowing where they had come from.
