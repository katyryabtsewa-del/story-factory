# Walk the Route First

On Thursday morning I put nineteen dried recipe cards on the passenger seat of Walt's old Ford, tucked his ledger under the seat, and set out to drive a road I knew by heart as if I had never seen it before.

I started at mile marker eleven, where Hilda Voss was waiting on her porch with the walker parked beside her chair, because nobody on Route Three has ever been surprised by my truck. I sat down on the step and told her about the dumpster, the dump, the tin, and the cards with the dots. I did not tell her about the money. Walt's letter said to walk the route first, and I took that to mean some listening before any talking.

Hilda took off her glasses and polished them on her sweater. "In two thousand nine," she said, "I had a roof that was a colander. I had a bucket in every room and a daughter in Arizona telling me to sell. And one Monday in July a load of shingles came, and a man I never saw. Three days. I stayed in the kitchen the whole time, because I was ashamed to have anyone see how bad it had been."

"You never knew who it was?"

"I heard humming." She put her glasses back on. "For three days somebody hummed on my roof, a hymn that had no words, always the same four bars, and I thought, well, whoever he is, he is happy. I turned my hearing aid off so I wouldn't spoil it." She studied me over the rims. "Winnie, did your husband hum?"

I could not answer her. I nodded.

"I was afraid you would say that," said Hilda, "because it means I have been rude to that man's widow about her cooking for twenty years." She reached out with a hand like a bundle of kindling and patted my knee. "Saturday. What happens on Saturday?"

I told her about the open house. She did not say a word about coming, and I did not ask, because I had decided I would not ask anybody for a single thing.

Dewey Marsh lived three miles on, in a brick ranch with the best-kept mailbox on the route. He is seventy-nine, a bachelor, a retired grain buyer, and he keeps a basset hound that spent nine years chasing my tires and never once caught them. When I pulled in and opened the truck door, the hound climbed straight in over my knees and sat down on the passenger seat on top of all nineteen cards.

"Well," said Dewey from the porch, "he finally caught it."

"He's sitting on my cards."

Dewey came down the steps one at a time and stood by the truck with his hands in his pockets. When I explained, he looked at the hound and said nothing. Then he said that in January of two thousand eleven his furnace had gone out at ten below, and the next morning there was a new one in the basement. The county office told him it was the heating assistance fund. He never believed them, because the heating assistance fund does not leave its tools.

"I always thought I was the only one," he said, finally. "A man gets to thinking the world did him one special favor, and he doesn't ask for another. I never knew there were nineteen."

"Neither did I," I said. "Not until Tuesday."

"Cabbage rolls," Dewey said. "Saturday. I'll bring the cabbage rolls." He looked at the hound, who had begun to snore. "If I can get that dog out of your truck."

It took both of us. The hound went home in Dewey's arms like a sack of feed, grumbling, and I drove on with one card chewed at the corner.

Thursday afternoon I did the Lund twins, who are sixty-four, farm the same quarter section, and finish each other's sentences. They had been told their well pump was under a manufacturer's warranty, and they had spent five years wondering what kind of warranty sends a man at night. Friday morning I sat a while in the bus shelter at mile marker nineteen, and looked at the church bell rope, which hung straight and new. Each person I told made the same face, the one a man makes when he has been handed a package he assumed belonged to somebody else.

What I noticed was that every one of them thought they were the only one. For nineteen years, nineteen households had carried a secret kindness around the county without knowing eighteen others were doing the same. And my husband had known them all, and had known me, and had known I would have told every single one of them thank you, and that they would have felt small.

I saved Ila Fenn for last. Her small rented farmhouse sits at mile marker twenty-two, with a gravel yard and a swing set, and a gray ramp at the side of the porch that nobody has had the heart to take down. I got there at four on Friday, an hour before she leaves to clean offices in town. Ila is thirty-three, with two children and one good car, and she met me at the door in her cleaning smock with her hand on the doorframe.

"Winnie? Is it the route? Did something happen?"

"Nothing happened," I said. "I came to see how the ramp was holding up."

She looked at me, and then at the side of the porch. "He used it every day for two winters," she said. "My dad. He could get out and see the road. The man came on a Sunday, said the county was getting rid of the lumber and he might as well use it. He didn't give a name. I wanted to thank him, but I could tell he'd rather fall through the floor." She stopped. "That was your husband. Wasn't it?"

I did not say yes. I took the fritters card out of my jacket, with its dot in the corner, and I put it in her hand.

She cried, quietly, standing in her doorway. Then she wiped her face with the back of her wrist and she asked what Saturday was. When I told her she said, "I work until two in the morning, you know." And then, "I'll make the fritters."

I drove home in the dusk with the windows down and counted boxes. I counted two hundred and thirty-eight before I turned onto Elm Row, which is the closest I had come in thirty years to finishing the route.

At nine that night, with the dishes in the rack and a light on in the workshop, the telephone rang. It was Hilda Voss, who does not use the telephone after seven.

"Winnie," she said. "I called around. It seems that half the route has been called by the other half." I heard her turn off the hearing aid and on again. "I am bringing the crisp. You can't make it, so I will."
