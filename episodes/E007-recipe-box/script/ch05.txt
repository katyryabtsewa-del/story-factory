# Card One Hundred Seventeen

The pale thing against the back of the tin turned out to be a sandwich bag, but before I could get at it I had to get two hundred and twelve cards out of the weather.

Del Haskell, who runs the Larkin County transfer station the way other men run a submarine, took one look at my lap and went into the scale house. He came out with clean brown paper and binder clips, wiped the picnic table under the little tin awning with his sleeve, and said, "Lay them out. One layer. Don't stack."

So I did. I lifted the cards out in handfuls and laid them down in rows like a woman dealing the world's biggest hand of solitaire. The ink had run on nearly all of them. The blue ballpoint had gone to clouds, the felt tips to bruises. One card in violet had bled out across its whole face like a pressed flower, and I held it a long minute while Del went and stood by his truck.

It was card one hundred seventeen, Hilda's rhubarb crisp, half-pulped and soft as a leaf. I turned it over carefully, and underneath it, where the dividers had held it flat for forty years, the cardboard lining at the back of the box had a slit cut in it, as straight as a ruler.

Out of it I drew the sandwich bag.

It was a plain zip bag, folded over twice, and inside it were three index cards and a folded sheet of lined paper, all of it dry. Walt knew about damp. I unfolded the paper with fingers that did not feel like mine, and there it was in carpenter's pencil, in the blocky, leaning capitals I would know on the moon.

I read it standing at the picnic table in the rain, and I will give it to you the way it was written.

"Bern. If you are reading this, the box got thrown out, or it got found, either way you're holding it, which means you went looking. Good. If it was Marnie, don't be mad at her. Folks who throw things out are usually afraid of something. Go and get the red toolbox before somebody sells it. The dots are porches. You know every one of them better than I do. I never could tell you, because you'd have told them thank you, and they'd have felt small. Walk the route first, Bern. Then decide."

And down at the bottom, squeezed in sideways along the edge, he had added, "One more thing. Card one hundred seventeen is the best crisp in the county and you still can't make it."

I laughed. People expect a woman in my position to cry, and I did that later. First I laughed, out loud, at a county dump at seven in the morning, with the rain dripping off my braid. Walt had spoken to me from the other side of a year and two months, and the last thing he told me was that I couldn't cook.

Then I cried, a good, long, loud cry. Del came over, set his thermos at my elbow, and went away again.

When I could see again, I read the three index cards. The first said, in his capitals, Key is taped inside the lid. Don't pry it, you'll bend the hinge. The second said, Hilda's crisp is butter, oats, brown sugar, rhubarb. You will burn it anyway. The third said, Greg will want to help. Give him a job, Bern, or he will find one.

Then I looked at the dots. In the top right corner of card one hundred seventeen, in the same pencil as the letter, there was a very small dot. I had seen such dots for years and taken them for crumbs of Walt's pencil, and on all those soaked cards, pencil was the only mark that had not run.

I went down the rows with my reading glasses on their beaded chain and counted. A dot on Hilda's card. A dot on the corn fritters, Ila Fenn, mile marker twenty-two. A dot on Dewey Marsh's cabbage rolls, and one on a card from the Lund twins' mother. I counted twice, because a carrier counts everything twice. There were nineteen cards with a dot, and I did not need the letter to tell me what they were. They were porches, and I had stood on every one.

I was holding the last of them when a pair of headlights came across the lot, slow, with their wipers going.

It was a gray sedan I knew, and the man who climbed out wore a yellow rain jacket with the fold lines still in it, the kind they sell at the register of a gas station. Greg did not wave. He walked across the wet gravel and stopped at the end of the picnic table and looked at the rows of ruined cards and then at me, in my dead husband's boots, with garbage in my braid.

My son did not say one word, and neither did I. He took off the jacket, which was the only dry thing for a mile, and held it over the cards so the rain would stop falling on them. He stood like that for ten minutes with his shirt going dark, and I have thought about it every day since.

"Marnie told me at midnight what was in that dumpster," he finally said. "I haven't slept. I stopped at the house on the way, and the kitchen light was on, and the receipt was on the counter. I'd come to tell you I talked to her."

"I figured," I said, and I handed him a stack of binder clips. Walt had said to give him a job.

We dried the cards. We pressed them between Del's brown paper, and Greg held the clips and I laid the sheets, and neither of us mentioned the letter, the listing, or the paper he had signed. It was the quietest hour I have ever spent with my son. When the tin had drained I ran my thumb under the lid, the way the first card said, and the rain had loosened the old tape. A brass key dropped into my palm, taped there with a strip of the masking tape Walt used for my mile marker tabs.

Greg looked at it. "It's a padlock key, Mom," he said. "A small one."

"I know," I said. I looked at the line near the middle of the letter. Go and get the red toolbox before somebody sells it.

I did not need the calendar in the scale house window. I had heard it with my own ears at my own kitchen table, the fourteenth, with a trailer. Today was Tuesday the thirteenth.

"Tomorrow," I said out loud, to a picnic table and a dump attendant. "At nine o'clock. A man is coming for my husband's toolbox."

Del looked at the key in my fist. "Then you'd better go home," he said, "and sit on it."
