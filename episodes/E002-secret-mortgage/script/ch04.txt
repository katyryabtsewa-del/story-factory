# Just Sign Here

A week after I stood in my son's office at Tri-County Flooring, he came to my duplex with a folder. It was Thursday, October fifteenth, a cold wet evening, and I had a pan of stuffed peppers in the oven, which is the kind of detail you remember when the rest of the night goes sideways.

Derek knocked at a quarter past six. He had on his good jacket, the gray one, and he was holding a blue folder against his chest the way a boy holds a report card he is not proud of. Behind him on Tilden Street a white SUV idled at the curb with its headlights on the rain.

"Vanessa's in the car," he said. "She thought it would be easier if she waited. She doesn't want to crowd you."

I looked at the SUV. The engine was running and the dome light was off, and a shape in the driver's seat was looking at her phone. "It's forty-eight degrees, Derek," I said. "Tell your wife to come in or go home. I won't have her sitting out there like a getaway driver."

He went back out into the rain. I turned off the oven, and a minute later I heard heels on my little porch, which has a loose board and has been the end of better shoes than hers.

Vanessa came in smiling. She wore a cream coat, her hair did that expensive thing where it looks as though no one has touched it, and she carried the smell of vanilla and money into my front room. She looked at my couch, my afghan, and the framed photograph of Ray in his work apron, and her smile did not change. "Helen. Thank you for seeing us. What a cozy little place."

"Sit down, Vanessa. Would you like tea?"

"We can't stay long." She sat on the very edge of the couch. Derek sat beside her, and I took Ray's old chair, the one with the worn arms.

"This is nothing," Vanessa said brightly. "It's just paperwork, because you're on the title as a courtesy, Helen, a technicality for the bank, and the bank needs your signature to unlock some funds for the house. Derek will show you where. A few initials, a few signatures, and we're out of your hair."

Derek opened the folder and laid it on my coffee table. I got up, found my reading glasses on the bookcase, put them on, and picked up page one.

"Helen, really," Vanessa said. "You don't need to read them all."

"I read everything," I said. "I read the invoices from the milk company for twenty-nine years. I have never signed a piece of paper I haven't read, and I'm too old to start for a cream coat."

Nobody spoke. I read. It took me eleven minutes, and the only sounds were the rain, the furnace, and the pages turning. It was an application for a home-equity line of credit. The amount was ninety-five thousand dollars. The stated purposes were a swimming pool, a kitchen addition with a wall of glass, and what the form called business inventory for Haven and Hue. Under the section for the borrower's income, in neat black type, I read a hundred ninety thousand dollars a year, from sales and consulting.

And on the line for ownership, where the form asked for the names of everyone holding title to the property, someone had typed Derek Brandt, Vanessa Whitfield Brandt, and Helen Brandt.

I took off my glasses and laid them in my lap. I looked at my son, and Derek looked at the rug.

"Vanessa," I said, "this application says Derek earns a hundred ninety thousand dollars."

"Of course it does. His consulting is very successful."

"And it says you own the house."

"Well, yes. We own the house." She laughed a little. "Helen, I know your generation likes to keep things simple, but Derek and I are married."

I did not say what I knew, which was that she held neither a deed nor a note, and that the man beside her had spent six years letting her believe otherwise. Twenty-nine years in a cafeteria taught me that you do not ring the lunch bell until the food is hot. I folded my hands.

"No," I said. "I won't sign this."

Vanessa's smile held for one second, then two. "I'm sorry?"

"I won't put my name on a loan for ninety-five thousand dollars secured by a house I have been paying for, on a form that has numbers on it I know are wrong. If you want to borrow against the house, you can ask the bank to explain to me why."

"Mom," Derek said, very low. "Please. Not now."

"I'm doing it now, Derek, because it's now that you put the paper in front of me."

Then Vanessa did something I had never seen her do. She stopped smiling. It was like watching a house with every light on go dark, one room at a time. Her face smoothed, then sharpened.

"Let me be very clear, Helen," she said. "You were never on the invitation list. Not for the wedding, not for the christening, not for any of it. We have tolerated you. Derek insisted. He said, she's lonely, she's harmless, she brings casseroles. But I have a business, and an image, and a daughter whose life is going to be photographed. I will not have it dragged down by a woman in a rented duplex with a Buick."

I only asked, as mildly as I could, "If Derek makes so much money, Vanessa, why not ask your father for the pool? Bruce seems so proud to have given you the house."

"My father did give us the house." She did not blink. "Daddy gave us the down payment as a wedding gift, and he is going to go on believing it, because it makes him happy, and I won't have that story changed over a signature. If you won't sign, you'll sign later. Everyone signs eventually."

Fifty-two thousand dollars, I thought. In a cashier's check, in my purse. For the first time that evening I felt completely calm, like a woman setting down a heavy bag at a bus stop. I picked up the pen Derek had left on the table, held it a moment, and laid it carefully on top of the folder.

"Thank you for coming," I said. "I'd like you both to go home now."

Vanessa stood. She put on her cream coat, button by button, and she looked down at me in Ray's chair. "And Helen. Don't come Saturday. I mean the twenty-fourth. I mean it kindly."

They went out into the rain. I heard the SUV door close, and then the engine, and then nothing, only the furnace. I sat in the chair a long time, with the stuffed peppers cooling in the kitchen, and I thought of Ray and the sentence he said whenever I wanted to leave a loose end. Paper doesn't forget, Helen. And I had six years of it.
