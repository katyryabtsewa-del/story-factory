# Thursdays

I did not turn around in that parking lot. It took me six weeks to do it, and I will tell you how it went, because it did not happen in the loud way people expect. It happened on ordinary mornings, with the kettle on.

The state surveyors stayed at Silverleaf Terrace for five days. They pulled every screening form for the last two years, and the director of nursing, to her credit, told them everything she knew. Odette called me the Monday after to read the findings back to me. In fourteen residents' files, the scores did not match the notes, the times, or the staff on duty. Fourteen people had been moved behind that keypad door on a number that was not theirs. In November the state placed a hold on all new admissions to Birch Court, and the owner's lender, I heard, was not pleased.

Silverleaf paid a fine. Gideon Pratt and the building's administrator were, as the letter to the families put it, no longer with the company. I have thought about Gideon since. I do not think he planned to hurt anyone. I think he looked at a number, and then at a person, and each time he chose the number it got easier. That is worth watching for in yourself, and I try.

My deposit came back by check, two thousand five hundred dollars, with a letter that said Silverleaf regretted any inconvenience. Cyril's niece, who had not been told about the fall until Odette told her, drove up from Pueblo and moved him to the Garden Wing that week. By Christmas she had taken him home with her, to a house with a ramp, a visiting nurse, and a dog, and he sent me a card that said, in his small square hand, Fourteen paces from the front door to the kitchen. All open.

Imelda works days now, with a second aide on nights, and her knees are the same, but she says she sleeps. She calls me Miss Peg, and I have given up asking her to stop.

Things were harder at Renata's end. The other facilities called her one by one, politely, and ended their contracts, and her husband sold two of the trucks. The banker, who had read about all of it in the paper, sat down with them in December anyway and stretched the balloon payment over three years. I never found out whether that was decency or arithmetic. In a small town they are very often the same.

On December sixth, a Sunday, my daughter knocked on my kitchen door. She had no folder and no purse, just a pair of car keys in her fist. It was the first time in my life I had seen Renata come into a room without something to hand me.

I made tea. We sat at the table with the cracked yellow top, with the bare aspens the color of pencils outside the window. She said nothing for a long time, and I did not fill the silence.

"I thought I was helping," she said at last.

"I know you did."

"I thought it was how things work. Everybody gets a fee." She turned the mug in her hands. "I used your card, Mom. I told myself I'd pay it back before you noticed."

I said, "Renata, I would like to ask you for something. It's an old habit of mine. Would you read it back to me? What you did, in order, and what it did to people. Not what you meant by it."

She did. She had no notes, and she did not look away once. "I used your card for the deposit," she said. "I called your doctor's office four times and told them you were forgetting things. You weren't."

"Five times," I said. "The nurse told me."

"Five. I asked a client, a realtor named Carla, to come to your house and tell me what it would bring."

"The woman with the light," I said. I had wondered about her for two months, and now I knew, and I let it sit where Renata had put it.

"I took seven thousand five hundred dollars from Gideon for putting you behind a locked door. I carried the power of attorney in, and I never once asked to see your score." She took a breath. "And a man with a broken wrist lay on a floor because there was one aide, and I helped fill that building."

When she finished, she was crying again, but not the way she had cried on my bed with the cake. "You could have warned me," she said. "Before that room."

"I could have," I said. "I decided not to, and that is mine to carry. This part is yours."

I did not hug her. I was not ready, and she did not ask me to be.

"I can't make it all right," I said. "I can offer you two conditions. First, you call every family you ever placed at Silverleaf, and you tell them what you now know, and you help them get their records. Second, you come with me on Thursdays and sit with the people on the other side of that brochure. You don't need to fix anything. You only need to sit."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she said, "What do I say to them?"

"Nothing, to start. You'll be surprised how much of it is listening."

In January I began the thirty-six hours of training that the state requires of a volunteer ombudsman. Odette taught half of it, and she handed out granola bars at every break. I wrote the time in the margin of every page.

Lu drove me to my first Thursday round in April, because she wanted to watch. I wore a lanyard with a badge that said Volunteer Ombudsman, and my own name underneath. It was a different building, on the other side of the valley, with a smell that was mostly bread. A call light came on over a door, and I went to it and knocked and asked what the lady needed. It was a glass of water. I brought it, and I looked at my watch, and it had been one minute and forty seconds, and I wrote that down, too.

Down the hall, Renata was sitting beside a woman in a wheelchair with a jigsaw puzzle between them, not talking, just turning pieces over. She looked up at me, and I raised my hand, and she raised hers.

That evening I sat at my kitchen table and watched the buds swell on the aspens. Hank used to say to write it down, Peg, you will forget the good parts too, and he was right, so I have.

So I would like to ask you something, and I would truly like to know. Would you have forgiven Renata, or only let her sit in the hallway? And tell me where you are listening from tonight, whether it is a kitchen, or a porch, or a long road. I always like to know where the voice lands.
