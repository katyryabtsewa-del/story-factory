# Certified Mail

On the same afternoon my doctor showed me what Brittany had told her about me, I sat down at Walter's oak table with a certified letter from a law firm in Columbus. I slit the envelope with a butter knife and read it.

It was two pages long, on paper heavy enough to stand up by itself. The firm was writing, it said, "on behalf of concerned family members," who were aware of recent incidents involving my safety. They wished to settle matters privately and with dignity, and so the firm enclosed a durable power of attorney naming my son, Kevin Whitaker, as my agent. If I preferred not to sign it, the letter said, the family "may pursue court-supervised options."

In case you have been lucky enough never to see one, a durable power of attorney is a paper that lets somebody else sign your name for you, on your checks and on the deed to your house. The durable part means it keeps working after you are no longer able to object. And court-supervised options meant a judge, and a hearing, and a stranger with a clipboard asking me what year it was.

The power of attorney was stapled behind the letter, six pages of it. Somebody had stuck little red plastic flags beside every line where I was supposed to sign. There were five of them. My family has never been shy with stickers.

I read the whole thing once, and then I got up and wiped down the counter by the sink. It was already clean. I wiped it anyway, from the coffee maker to the bread box, and then I sat back down and read the letter a second time, slower. It said the same thing the second time. That is what you pay lawyers for.

The letter did not say which family members were concerned. It did not need to. There was only one name typed on the line for my agent. The word incompetent was in there too, tucked into the middle of a long sentence like a pit in a cherry, where you would not find it until you bit down.

I folded the letter along its creases and filed it in my recipe box under C, for Columbus, right behind chicken and dumplings.

Kevin called the next evening at seven.

"Hi, Mom," Kevin said. He was using the voice, soft and warm, like a man talking a cat down out of a tree. "How did the doctor go yesterday?"

I had not told Kevin I had a doctor's appointment. It was written on the calendar on my refrigerator, in the square for Thursday. Brittany had spent three Saturdays in my kitchen.

"My blood pressure is better than hers," I said. "She tells me that every year."

"That's great, Mom. That's really great. So, I guess you got a letter."

"I did," I said. "My neighbor signed for it."

"I know how it looks," Kevin said. "But it's for your protection, Mom. That's all it is."

"Protection from what, honey?" I asked.

"From anything," Kevin said. "Scammers. Falls. You're all alone in that big house."

"I'm alone in it because your father died," I said, "not because I lost track of him."

Kevin laughed, though it was not a joke. "Nobody wants to go to court, Mom. You sign, it's done, and you never have to think about money again."

"I like thinking about money," I said. "I was good at it. Does Tommy know about this?"

There was a pause on the line. "Tommy doesn't need to be bothered with it," Kevin said. "You know how he is."

"I do know how he is," I said.

He told me he loved me, and I told him goodnight. Then I wrote down every word I could remember in my green notebook. It took two pages. Kevin says a great deal when he wants something.

Rachel called on Saturday morning. I could hear her son in the background practicing the trumpet, badly, the way every ten-year-old does.

"Kevin says you got a letter," Rachel said. "It's just paperwork, Mom. That's what Kevin says."

"Is that what you say?" I asked her.

For a long moment there was nothing on the line but the trumpet.

"Mom," Rachel said, "can I ask you something?"

"You can ask me anything, honey," I said.

I heard her breathe in. Then I heard her stop. "Never mind," Rachel said. "It's nothing. I'm just tired."

Rachel has been telling me things are nothing since she was four years old, and it has never once meant nothing. I waited, the way I used to wait outside the bathroom door when she was little and crying in there with the water running, but she did not go on.

"And how was the wedding?" I asked.

"The wedding," Rachel said. "Oh. It was nice. Mom, I keep forgetting to mail the ring. I'll bring it at Thanksgiving, I promise."

I wrote that down too.

On Sunday Tommy came for supper, the way he does every Sunday. He came in at four with a bag of rock salt for the porch steps, for when the ice comes, and he went through the house and changed the battery in every smoke detector. Then he tested each one. They all shrieked the way they were supposed to. Not one of them had ever had a reason to.

I made pot roast with carrots and onions, and an apple pie with the cheddar crust, and we ate at Walter's table with the radio on low. Tommy ate the way his father did, without much talking and with great attention.

Over pie, I nearly told him.

"Tommy," I said.

He looked up at me with his father's eyes. "What is it, Mom?"

"Nothing, honey," I said. "Have another piece."

I know. I had written my own daughter down in my notebook for saying that very thing. Tommy is slow to anger, like his father was, but when it comes, it comes all at once, and I did not want him on the highway to Columbus in the dark with it. Kevin and Rachel had made this mess. I did not want Tommy carrying any part of it until I knew exactly what it was.

He left a little before eight, after checking the lock on the back door twice.

That night I decided I would call Harold Greene in the morning. I had put it off since Thursday, because once I took this to a lawyer, it would stop being a family matter, and I had wanted to give my family every chance to be a family. They had used their chances to ask about my blood pressure and promise me a ring.

At nine o'clock, headlights swept across my kitchen ceiling. Nobody comes up my driveway at that hour on a Sunday. I went to the window with the dish towel in my hand and watched a small car pull in behind mine and stop crooked, with the engine still running.

It was Olivia, who was supposed to be in Columbus doing her homework. She came up the porch steps in pajama pants and her father's old high school sweatshirt, hugging the old family iPad to her chest with both arms. Her eyes were red, and I could tell she had been crying for most of the drive.

"Grandma," Olivia said, "you need to see this."
