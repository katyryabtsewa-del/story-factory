# Twenty-Two Pounds

By ten thirty-five on the Friday after Thanksgiving, my lake house had already been sold, the turkey had already been eaten, and the only person who knew either of those things was the woman on the dock in the navy fleece. That woman was me. I was sitting on the bench at the head of my own dock, facing the house and the drive, with a mug of cocoa gone lukewarm, when I heard a car on the gravel.

The cove was flat and gray, with ice along the edges as thin as a dinner plate. Craig's silver crossover came around the bend and stopped by the garage, and my son sat behind the wheel for a long moment, looking at the house the way a man looks at a bill he expects to pay.

I didn't get up. I wanted to see how long he'd sit there, and I will admit I was a little curious how a man's face looks when he is about to be surprised and does not yet know it.

He had a plan for that house. He had a plan for me, too, and it involved a place called Pinecrest Commons, where someone could check on me a couple of times a day. He had not asked me about either one.

That's where I want to start. But you can't understand a thing like that without knowing the woman on the dock, so let me back up to Wednesday morning, when the bird was still in its grocery bag.

My name is Dorothy Ellery, and everybody calls me Dot, except the State of Maine, which calls me Dorothy on my driver's license and has never once been wrong about my height. I am sixty-eight years old. I was a school nurse for thirty-three years at Harrow Consolidated School in East Harrow, a town of about nineteen hundred people on the north shore of Lake Wassumkeag. I took the pulse of more children than I can count. I can tell a faked stomachache from forty paces, and I still carry a tackle box of bandages in the back of my green Subaru. The Subaru has two hundred thousand miles on it and a dent from a deer that I have decided not to discuss.

My husband, Frank, built our lake house himself in nineteen eighty-nine out of cedar he pulled from an old barn. He was a boat builder by trade and a caretaker of the lake by temperament, and he could tell you how deep the cove was by the color of the water. When he died six years ago, of a stroke in the parking lot of the hardware store, he left me three notebooks full of dock measurements, a lopsided red canoe, and a deed with my name on it and nobody else's. He had a lawyer draw it up that way. "So nobody can ever sell your roof out from under you," he told me. I thought that was the most romantic thing a man had ever said about property. Most days I still do.

He also taught me to sail, which I did badly, and to back a boat trailer, which I did worse, and he never once said so out loud.

For thirty-eight Thanksgivings, the whole family came up to the lake. Craig came first, then Craig's wife, then the grandchildren, who used to need pulling out of the water by their hoods, and then, a long while after, the divorce. We ate in the kitchen, because the dining room table had been Frank's workbench for a decade and never quite got cleared.

Every year I bought the turkey in Harrow on the Wednesday before, because the store at the lake does not stock a bird that size, and every year I drove it home in a cooler on the passenger seat, because I did not trust a grocery store to keep anything cold for a forty-minute drive. Nobody in the family ever asked me to change the menu, except Tyler, who at fourteen wanted tacos and was told no in the voice I had learned in the school office. This year's bird weighed twenty-two pounds. I squeezed it, I looked at the skin, and I told the man at the counter that it would do nicely. I had been planning that bird since the summer.

The house itself is a cedar and green-trim place on the north end of the cove, with a screened porch facing the water, three bedrooms, a dock that Frank rebuilt every spring, and a mudroom with forty years of boots lined up along the wall like a choir. The wood stove in the front room is his, too. He installed it that first winter, and he swore the copper gasket would outlast us both. I have spent a lot of cold nights since then being grateful he was right about that.

Craig called while I was loading the cooler into the back.

"Mom, hi. Quick thing. Small change of plans."

I know a small change in a man's voice. I heard it for thirty-three years from children who swore the stomachache was nothing, and it was never nothing. I closed the hatch and leaned against the car.

The cold came up through the soles of my boots. Across the road, the sign in front of the Harrow Congregational church said, in plastic letters, Give Thanks, which I have always found a little bossy for a Wednesday. Once, in my second year at the school, a seventh grade boy came into my office with a wrist he swore was broken in three places. It was a sprain. I called his mother, who listened to me describe the wrist and then said, "Dot, he does that." I thought about that boy a good deal that morning. Some people have been doing a thing so long that everybody around them has stopped checking.

He told me that he, Dana, Tyler and Brooke had booked a place called Pine Ledge Lodge for the holiday, over in the next town. It had a heated indoor pool, a private chef, and rooms at two hundred dollars a night. "The kids really want the pool," he said. "Tyler's been on me for a month. And Dana has some connection with the place, so it's kind of a good deal."

"And the lake house?"

"Well, that's the thing. You get it all to yourself, Mom. Nobody to cook for, nobody banging around at six in the morning. Come up Friday, we'll all have lunch, and we can talk about some ideas I've got for the place."

"What kind of ideas?"

"Nothing you need to worry about. Honestly, it's no big deal." In my experience, that is the first thing people say about a big deal. I could hear wind on his end and a woman's voice saying his name. "Okay, Dana's waving at me. Love you. You'll love the quiet, Mom."
