# Pruitt's Table Saturdays

The text said, "Mom, are you home Saturday? I would like to come and wash dishes. Please don't say no."

It is November now, the Sunday before Thanksgiving, and I am telling you all of this from my yellow kitchen, with the Wedgewood warming up and a cup of coffee going cold at my elbow. That text came on the Monday after the wedding. I stood in the college parking lot and read it three times, and then I wrote back four words. Bring an apron. Come early.

He came at seven. Gregory showed up on Spaight Street in jeans and sneakers, carrying a brand new apron still creased from the package, and I put him at the sink with a stack of sheet pans and a bottle of soap. He did not say a word for three hours. Neither did I. We worked side by side in the quiet, and every so often I handed him another pan, and he took it as though it were something fragile.

Since then it has become a rhythm. In October, Marco and I started the classes, which we call Pruitt's Table Saturdays, and we hold them in the Corso kitchen for anyone over fifty-five who wants to learn to cook the way our grandmothers did. We have twenty-two students. A retired bus driver is learning pie crust, and a widow in the front row keeps asking for the gravy. And every other Saturday, at eight in the morning, my son comes in his apron and takes the dish shift at my table, with the sleeves pushed up to his elbows. The retired bus driver calls him chief. Gregory did not know what to say about that the first time, and now he answers to it.

Last Saturday I cut him a slice of kringle and set it on a plate at the counter, no ceremony, the way I did when he was nine. He picked up his fork and took one bite, and he stood there for a while looking at the butter shining on the pastry. Then he put the fork down and cried a little, quietly, into the dish towel. I did not say anything. I just stood next to him and smoothed the counter with my palm, and after a while he laughed and wiped his face and finished the slice.

Courtney came to the house three weeks after the wedding. She stood on my porch in a camel coat holding a pie from the grocery store, still in its plastic dome, and a folded note in her other hand. I looked at the pie and then at her, and she said, "I know. I know it's store-bought. I tried to make one and it was a crime scene."

"Come in," I said. "We'll see what we can do with the crust."

The note was handwritten, on thick cream paper. It said that she had used the word cute as a weapon, that she had been afraid of being thought small and had made another woman feel it instead, and that she had been taught by her own father to be proud of work and had somehow forgotten how. It ended, I would like to learn the gravy, if the invitation still stands. I put the note in the fireproof box with the receipts, between Sam's signature and the lunch at Verdant. I will tell you that forgiveness is not a switch. I did not say I forgive you. I said the invitation stood, and I handed her a peeler.

The Pruitt's Table Scholarship started in October as well, at Madison College, with the forty thousand dollars that was meant for a house. It pays for the first year of the culinary program for young people who want to cook and cannot afford to. We have two students already. One is a girl who works the night shift at a gas station and spends her days off in the library reading about sauces. When she wrote to thank me, she spelled my name wrong, and I framed the letter anyway. Gregory and Courtney found out about it from Priya. Courtney put down her coffee cup and said, "Oh," and then nothing for a long minute, and I think she understood it a good deal better than I had hoped.

On the first Friday of every month, we hold a fish fry at Corso, one night only. Marco's cooks hang a hand-lettered sign in the window, beer-battered cod, potato pancakes, and cheese curd gravy fries, and they play Frank Sinatra over the speakers because Marco insists. The first night, the line went out the door and around the corner, and somebody came up to me at the counter and said, "You must be the lady from the wedding," and then, "Chief, I used to eat at your place on Willow Street." I told him I had been called worse. Sam would have loved it.

My house will be full on Thursday. I am hosting Thanksgiving for fourteen. Edith is coming, of course, with lemon bars and her opinions. Marco and Priya are bringing a pan of something I did not ask for and will not turn down. Richard Ashford has already been to the house, with a case of mustard in a flat wooden crate and a folding table in the back of his truck, because he said a table with a leaf in it was a lie, and he has set it up in my dining room, where it wobbles. Courtney is coming Wednesday afternoon to peel ten pounds of potatoes. I have told her to keep her nails short. Gregory will cut the pies.

I think about that tent a good deal, and about what it means to be ashamed of your mother. I do not believe my son was ever ashamed of me. I believe he was afraid of the room, which is a different thing, and a much more common one. Most of the people who have hurt me were not cruel. They were frightened and in the wrong company, and nobody had ever shown them what happens when you do not flinch. I kept my voice down that spring, I kept my hands busy, and I kept my receipts. And in the end the truth got up in front of a hundred and eighty people and told itself.

Sam used to say, let the food do the talking. In my experience it has a great deal to say, if you will only sit still and let it.

So I would like to ask you something, whoever you are, wherever you are listening tonight. Tell me where you are, and what is on your stove. And tell me this, in your own words. If it had been you at table fourteen, would you have stayed silent the way I did, or would you have stood up and said it yourself?
