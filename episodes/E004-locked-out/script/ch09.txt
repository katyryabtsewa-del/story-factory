# The Key to the Front Door

That Thursday ended at my kitchen table with a pen and a legal pad, but it took until June to learn whether I had been wise or only tired, and I would like to tell you how it came out.

The paperwork went first, because paperwork is quicker than people. In the last week of February, Cecilia Rowe telephoned me at home and said in her ruler-straight voice that the review was closed. Larkmoor's lawyer had written a letter withdrawing every claim against the house on Birchwood Lane, and the lien they never filed, and never could have, was stamped void. The money was another matter. Larkmoor could not touch the house, but Tessa still owed it what she had actually received, one hundred eighty thousand dollars less the thirty-one thousand Ray had paid, as an ordinary debt with no house behind it.

Tessa had worked out a repayment plan with the advisor at the credit union, years long, and Larkmoor, which had no better choice, wrote off the fees and the steep interest. As for Mister Voss, she had heard he was exploring opportunities in another state. I said I hoped it was somewhere warm. She said, very quietly, that she had not laughed at work in a year.

I was at home by then, in every sense. For three weeks after the meeting I slept at Winnie's and walked across the lane each morning as a visitor, because a house that has been locked against you takes a while to forgive you. On the fifteenth of February I carried my steno case back across the lane myself, and Winnie followed with eleven garbage bags and a running commentary.

Tessa's statement had gone in the week before. Cecilia said it was the plainest confession she had ever read, with every date and every dollar, and not one excuse.

Gus ran the shop. He wore Ray's apron for a month before he let anyone see him do it. The bell over the door still rang flat, and the cat still slept on the safe. In April he asked me, with his ears the color of ripe tomatoes, whether it would be all right if he hung Ray's picture behind the counter. I said Ray would have found it unbearable, and that it should go up at once.

Tessa kept her word, which surprised no one but Tessa. In April she gave up the lease on the second building, and the landlord kept every dollar she had poured into his walls, and she kept the first Foxglove exactly as it had been before she got ambitious. On the day she handed back the keys she called me from the parking lot and said nothing for nearly a minute, and then she said, "It's smaller now," and I said, "Most good things are." She had also gone to the church basement, and told the funeral committee in a clear voice that I had never worked on any dying man. Afterward, the very old woman in the corner mailed me a card that said only, I thought as much.

The Sunday suppers were another matter.

The first one was a disaster. Tessa arrived ten minutes early with a grocery store apple pie still in its plastic dome, and we discussed the weather for fifty minutes, as if one of us were to be tested on it. The third was the Sunday she stayed to dry the dishes without being asked, and in the middle of the plates she told me about her mother, Joan, who hummed in the kitchen, burned the toast, and could not carry a tune in a bucket. I said I now understood where Ray had gotten it. Tessa looked at me, and for the first time in nineteen years she smiled with her whole face.

That was the night I took out the handkerchief.

I laid the brass ring on the table, with the house key on it, bright as a new penny, and the paper tag that said T. She did not touch it for a long moment.

"He cut that the week my mother died," she said. "He told me I would always have a key to the house. And I said something awful, I don't even remember what, and he put it in a drawer, and I never asked." She picked it up as if it might be hot. "Twenty-two years. It has never been used."

"It does not work anymore, I am afraid," I said. "But I thought you should hold it."

She did not cry, which I took as progress. She put it in her pocket, the way you put away something you intend to return to.

I will tell you what I took from that spring, and then I will stop. Ray loved two stubborn women and managed it by keeping his mouth shut, which is not a method I would recommend. And I had spent nineteen years so busy being right about Tessa that I never once let her get to the end of a sentence.

In the second week of June, on a bright Saturday, I was on my knees in the garden behind the house, among Ray's old tomato cages, when I heard a truck in the drive. It was Ray's truck, which Tessa had returned to the shop in February. She climbed out wearing work gloves and carrying a paper sack from the hardware store and a small steel case.

"I'm putting in a new lock," she said. "Ray's cylinder is tired, Gus says, and I want one I put in myself. With the right screws, this time. Gus has already inspected them."

It took her an hour. I sat on the porch steps with a glass of lemonade and watched my stepdaughter, with her father's long fingers, fit a deadbolt into my front door as if she had been doing it all her life, which in a way she had. When the bolt was in, she opened the steel case. Inside was a brass key, cut and filed, with its edges still bright, and a little stamping tool.

"I cut it on Dad's machine this morning," she said, a little shyly. "Gus held the lamp."

She set the key on the step, tapped the die twice with a tiny hammer, and held it out to me on her flat palm. On the head of the key were two small letters, stamped side by side. An L, and next to it, a T.

I put it in the lock, and it turned, with a click as round and clean as any Ray ever made. I sat for a while with the door open and the sun coming across the old floorboards, and neither of us said anything at all.

Now I have a question for you, and I would like an honest answer. When your stepdaughter, or your son, or anyone you have given your best years to, has locked you out of your own life on the very day you buried your husband, what do you do? Ray asked me to give her one chance. I gave it, on paper, with a signature, and I have not yet decided whether I was generous or only worn out. Winnie says she would have called the sheriff, the newspaper, and a priest, in that order. So tell me, would you have given her the chance?

And tell me where you are listening from. I like to picture a kitchen, or a porch, or a long drive. If it is snowing where you are, please put on a hat.
