# Wanda Pickett Has a Laminated Card

On Monday morning, two days after I heard my husband describe me to a stranger as a woman who would sign anything, I drove Gladys forty minutes to Mobile with a sticky note in my purse and a folder of copies on the seat beside me.

The State Licensing Board for Pool and Spa Contractors keeps its regional office on the second floor of a tan state building with a lobby that smells like floor wax and old carpet. The elevator took its time. I rode it up with a man carrying a ficus, and at the end of a long hallway I found a pale green counter with a bell on it and, behind the counter, a woman in reading glasses and a peach-colored cardigan.

Her nameplate said Wanda Pickett. She was fifty-five years old, with a stack of laminated cards on a metal ring and a glass dish of butterscotch candies at her elbow, and she looked up at me over her glasses in the exact way my high school librarian once did when I returned a book late.

"Morning, sugar," she said. "Take a butterscotch. You look like you drove here on a mission."

I took one. I had planned a speech in the car, but what came out was, "Missus Pickett, I'd like to ask some questions about the rules, in a general way, and I would appreciate it if you would not ask me why."

"Honey, I'm a state employee," said Wanda. "I don't ask anybody why. I only tell them what the card says." She flipped through the ring, and pulled out one the color of a lemon, and set it on the counter between us. "Go ahead."

I asked about a company that has a licensed qualifying agent on file. She said that was the law. Every pool contractor company had to have one, a living person who had passed the state exam and who answered to the board for the company's work. Not the owner, necessarily. Not the person on the truck door. The person on the certificate.

"And if that person resigns?" I said.

Wanda gave me a small look, and then she looked at the card. "In writing. Signed, on this form here, and it takes effect when it's signed and filed. The company gets a grace period of sixty days to finish whatever jobs it's already got under contract. Those permits stay good, the crews can keep working, the customers don't get left in the lurch. What it cannot do is sign a new contract or pull a new permit, not one, until it names a new qualifier."

"And the new qualifier has to be a person who passed the exam."

"Passed it, not studied for it, not gonna take it. The state gives that exam a few times a year, and the next sitting is Tuesday, November eighteenth, in Montgomery." She tapped the laminated card. "And before you ask, one person can be the qualifier for one company at a time. Not two. That's the rule."

"What if she resigns from one company," I said, carefully, "and wants to start her own?"

"Then she's free as a bird. She passed the test, it's her name on the certificate, she walks across the hall and files a new application. Nobody on this earth can stop her but the state, and the state only wants to know that she's the one who passed." Wanda took off her glasses. "Is there a non-compete? That's not our business, but I'm telling you it's somebody's."

"There is not."

"Well, then," said Wanda, and she unwrapped a butterscotch for herself.

I asked her what I most needed to ask. "What about the customers? Say a company has jobs half done, a pool dug, a patio poured. Does the family get stranded?"

"Not if it's done right. That's why we have the sixty days. The company gets two months to finish its work and find itself a qualifier. If it's been smart, it's already got one lined up. If it hasn't," and she gave a little shrug, "that's between it and its Maker."

I thanked her. I had not said a name, and neither had she, but she was looking at me with a great deal of sympathy.

"Sugar, I've been at this counter nineteen years," Wanda said. "I have seen grown men cry right where you're standing. Big men, in good boots. And I'll tell you something. Never one time has it been the person who read the manual."

I laughed. It was the first time in three weeks I had laughed from the stomach, and the man with the ficus, who was waiting behind me, laughed too, though I do not think he knew why.

I sat for an hour afterward in the front seat of Gladys in the parking deck, with the windows down and a legal pad on the steering wheel, and I made my plan, the way I have made every plan in my life, in a list.

First, the jobs. We had fourteen open jobs, pools and patios in every stage between a hole in the ground and a ribbon-cutting. I knew every homeowner's name and what each of them was afraid of. I would not strand a single one. As the company's qualifier, I was entitled to do my job until the minute I quit it, so over the next week I would make sure every one of those fourteen jobs had its permits pulled, its inspections booked, and its supplier bills paid. The company would have its sixty days. I would see to it that the people on the other end of those jobs never felt so much as a draft.

Second, Ray. He had a right to hear it from me before he heard it from the room, and I would find the moment.

Third, Priya Nair. I would call her that afternoon and ask her to draft my letter of resignation to go with the board form, effective at the moment I signed.

And fourth, the two gentlemen from Houston. I had never met them, and I owed them nothing, but I did not mean to let two strangers wire one point four million dollars across the country on a signature they had been told was a formality. That was not payback. That was ordinary decency, and it would happen to be inconvenient for my husband.

Before I left, I went back up in the elevator, because there was one more thing I wanted. Wanda was eating a second butterscotch and doing a crossword. I asked for the form. She slid it across the counter, a plain white page with a state seal and a line at the bottom that said qualifying agent, and she held on to the edge of it a half second longer than she needed to.

"Resignation takes effect the minute it's signed and filed, sugar," she said. "Not the day before, and not the day after. Be sure of your date."
