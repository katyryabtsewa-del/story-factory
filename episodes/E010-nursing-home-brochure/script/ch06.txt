# Odette

On Thursday evening, with a blue folder in my drawer and a number in my notebook, I sat in the vinyl chair with my own telephone and called Lu.

"Who do we know?" I said. "Someone who can do something. Not a lawyer, not yet."

"You'll have to be more specific, hon. Most of the people we know have been dead since the nineties." A cat jumped off a counter, and then she said, "Odette Watanabe, from the casserole committee at church. She was a high school counselor for thirty years, and now she volunteers for the state ombudsman program, the one for people in nursing homes. She hands you a granola bar before she says hello."

"Call her," I said. "Saturday morning, if she'll come."

On Friday I played a confused old woman, and I enjoyed it more than I should have. At breakfast I told Gideon I could not find my glasses, while they sat on top of my head. "They're right there, Miss Peggy," he said, and I saw his face go slack with relief. After that he talked more freely around me. He told the dietary manager, in front of me, that Birch Court had to be filled by the end of December, because the owner's lender had a little list. I wrote down every word in the bathroom.

That night Imelda pressed her telephone into my hand. On its screen was a page of Cyril's field book. "He wants you to have all of it," she whispered. "Nine years of measurements, and nobody ever asked." Lu sent the photographs on to Odette.

On Saturday morning I asked Gideon whether a guest might sign herself out to the library. He was delighted, and he said the word independent the way other people say ice cream. He walked me to the keypad door himself, with his shoulder between me and his hand, and I listened, and the four tones were the ones I already had.

The community room at the Alder Bench Library smelled of coffee that had been on the burner since the Carter administration. In the corner sat a woman in round glasses with a canvas tote bag on the chair beside her. Odette Watanabe stood up and shook my hand with both of hers, and before I had even said my name, she held out a granola bar.

"Lu says you've had a week. Eat something first."

I ate it. Then I laid the green notebook on the table and went through it page by page, the way I have given thousands of addresses at three in the morning. Odette wrote it all down with a felt-tip pen, and twice she said back to me what she had heard. It was the first time all week that I felt a little less alone.

"I'm not a lawyer, and I'm not a police officer," she said. "I speak for the residents. I can take a complaint to the state health department. They can inspect without telling anyone first, and if they find enough, they can stop a facility from taking new residents."

"Enough of what?" I asked.

"Paper. Dates. A person who was there." She looked at the picture of Cyril's field book. "Peg, I have a stack of complaints that say, in effect, he said, she said. You have times, and a ninety-one-year-old surveyor. With your permission, I will file on Monday morning."

"You have it."

"I can't promise you a date," she said. "They come without calling, and that is the whole point. A fall with an injury counts as an immediate safety concern, so it could be days. It could also be after Thanksgiving."

"Then I will run Thursday as if nobody is coming," I said. "The conference is at ten, and the notebook has to stand on its own."

"Tell me the time anyway," Odette said. "I will be in the lobby with my tote bag. And Peg, the aide who is afraid, don't push her. Just let her know I'm here."

On Sunday at seven, before the day shift had finished its coffee, I let myself out through the keypad door with the four numbers I had learned by ear. In the lot sat a small gray hatchback with its engine running, and Imelda inside it in a parka. I tapped on the glass, and she lowered it.

"I won't ask you anything," I said. "I'll only tell you there's a woman named Odette, and she carries granola bars."

Imelda laughed, one dry breath, and then she stopped. "Eleven years," she said. "I have changed those people. I will say it to anybody with a badge. Tell her yes."

At nine I told Gideon I would stay through his family care conference and pay for the extra four nights. His whole face opened up like a garage door.

That night Renata came back, alone, with a paper plate under foil. It was a slice of my birthday cake, which she had frozen, as she would. She sat on the edge of my bed with the cake on her knees, and she began to cry.

My daughter does not cry. She did not cry at her father's funeral. She cried now in short, furious breaths, and told me about her husband's trucking company and a loan of one hundred ninety thousand dollars with a balloon payment due on the last day of December. The banker would not even sit down with them unless they paid seventy-five hundred dollars in good faith by the thirtieth of October. "Silverleaf pays me when I place somebody," she said. "It's a referral fee, Mom, seven thousand five hundred. Everyone does it. I'm not stealing from you. I'm just taking it off your plate."

I could have written her a check that night. I had two hundred ten thousand dollars saved, and I want you to know that I thought of it. But she had not asked me for money. She had asked me to sign a folder, and I had in my drawer a sheet of paper that said I could not draw a clock.

"I know," I said, gently, and I held her hand until she stopped.

I did not warn her, and I want to be plain that this was a decision and not a kindness. If I told Renata what was coming, she would tell Gideon, and by Monday ten other people in that building would have their forms quietly rewritten. So my daughter would walk into that room blind and take it in front of her husband, and I would be the one who put her there.

When she had gone, I turned to the last page of the notebook, where I keep what has to be done, and wrote it as plainly as a call sheet. Thursday, ten o'clock. Bring the notebook. Renata does not know. The state may come, and it may not.

It was the first call I had ever taken where I could not tell the caller that help was on its way. I decided to keep talking anyway.
