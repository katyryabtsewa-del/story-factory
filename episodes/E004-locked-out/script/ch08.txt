# One Chance

I stood in my own hallway for twenty-two minutes by the clock over the coat rack, outside my own kitchen door, while my stepdaughter read her father's letter, or read it ten times, and I did not once knock.

The radiator ticked. A snowplow went by on Birchwood Lane and shook the glass in the front door. I had waited outside a great many jury rooms, but never on a verdict of my own.

At last the door opened. Tessa came out with the letter pressed flat against her coat, and her face looked scrubbed raw. For a moment neither of us said a word.

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out in pieces. "The whole time. He never said one word to me about it after that day. He paid for it, Loretta, and he sat at my table on Sundays and told me to eat."

"I know," I said. "I read the notebook."

"He wrote that he wanted me to be the one to tell you."

"He wrote that too."

She put her free hand over her mouth. I did not hug her, because we were not that kind of family. I did the one useful thing I could think of and put the kettle on.

We sat at the same table where she had been taken apart in front of a stranger an hour earlier, with the peppermint wrapper still on it. Tessa wrapped both hands around her cup. I have found over the years that tea does half the work of mediation.

"I am going to tell you what happens next," I said. "I have written it down, because that is what I do. You may say yes, or you may say no, and either answer will go in the record."

I took out the legal pad, on which I had written the terms the night before in Winnie's spare room, and read them aloud, one by one, as plainly as a verdict.

First, the bank on Water Street would proceed with its fraud review against Larkmoor Bridge Funding. Larkmoor's claim on this house was void, because the house was mine and I had signed nothing, and how they lent without checking who signed was now their problem. Second, Tessa would write a full, true statement of what she had done, with dates, signed before Cecilia Rowe at the bank, leaving out nothing that shamed her. Third, in return, I would not ask anyone to press charges against her, for as long as she kept every line on the page.

"There was no buyer," Tessa said suddenly. "Dale told me Larkmoor would take the house and sell it by the end of February. I couldn't say that to you. A buyer sounded better."

"It does," I said. "I had worked that out."

"Fourth," I said. "The shop goes to you, as your father wished. It is in his will, which I filed with the register of wills on Monday and have copied for you. Gus has the first right to buy in or manage, and I would like him to manage, because he loves it, and you do not. The shop's earnings and savings go to pay what Ray promised on the debt. Fifth, you will give up the lease on the second building, close the spa wing, and keep Foxglove only as it was before you got ambitious. You will see a debt advisor at the credit union, who will help you write a plan to repay Larkmoor what you actually received, less your father's thirty-one thousand, as an ordinary debt with no house behind it. I have made the appointment. It is Monday at nine."

"You made the appointment," Tessa said.

"I made the appointment. I am a very efficient woman. Sixth, you will go to the church basement and tell seven ladies, in a clear voice, that I did not work on a dying man. I would like to be present for that, but I will not be, because I was not invited."

Tessa let out a short, damp sound, close to a laugh.

"And seventh," I said. I looked down at the pad. I had written this one last, and crossed it out, and written it again. "We will have supper at this table every second Sunday for one year. I will set the table. You will bring the pie. We will not discuss the past unless it comes up on its own."

She stared at me. "Why? I lied to you. I tried to take your house. I put your things on the porch in the snow, and I let a church full of people think you were a vulture." Her chin shook. "You could have called the sheriff and watched me lose everything."

"I know you would have," I said. "That is rather the point."

"Is this for Dad?" she asked.

"Partly," I said. "He asked me to give you one chance he gave you too late. I am not going to pretend that does not matter to me. But mostly, Tessa, I am tired. I am tired of being the only person in this family who remembers how to be decent, and I would like some company."

She said nothing. Then, in a very small voice, "I called you Dad's wife because if I called you anything else, it meant she was really gone."

"I wondered," I said. "I never found the nerve to ask. You were always on your way out the door."

She laughed, a wet and shaky laugh, and I let her have it.

It was not forgiveness, not yet. It was something smaller and sturdier, like a plank laid across a creek, and I did not know whether it would hold both of us. I slid the legal pad across with a pen. She read all seven terms with her lips moving, as her father had taught her to check a key twice, and then she signed, and I signed under her.

Then I telephoned the shop and the bank, as planned, and twenty minutes later Gus came in with his cap in his hands, and Cecilia Rowe behind him in her gray coat. They read all seven terms standing at my table and each signed on the line marked for a witness. Cecilia said she signed as a private person and not as the bank, which is exactly what she would say. Tessa did not look at Gus. "I'm sorry about the card machine," she said to the table, and Gus said, "Yes, ma'am," and went red to the ears.

When they had gone, Tessa reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out something small and silver, the key to the cheap lock that Gus had carried off in a paper bag. She laid it on the table and looked at it with disgust.

"I won't be needing this," she said.

"No," I agreed. "It was a very poor lock."

She did not smile. She was looking at my hand, which had gone, without my deciding it, to the cardigan pocket where the brass key lay.

"Loretta," she said. "Can I see it? The one he left you."

I took it out and set it on the table between the teacups. She did not pick it up. She only leaned closer, and read the five words in her father's capitals, and her mouth moved with them.

I had not told her about the other key. It was in my handbag, wrapped in a clean handkerchief, on a brass ring with a paper tag that carried a single letter, and the letter was hers.
