# What Ray Knew

I sat in a little room in the back of Merrow Valley Bank and read my husband's notebook without skipping a line, and it took me forty minutes to learn that I had been married to a better man than I knew and a worse liar.

Ray wrote the way he cut keys, with nothing left over. The second entry said, November third, later. Larkmoor Bridge Funding, notice of default. One hundred eighty thousand borrowed in April against the house on Birchwood. My name on it. I did not borrow it.

November fourth. Drove to Foxglove. Tessa in the back kennel. I put the paper on the counter and she sat down on a dog crate. She cried the way she did at nine when she put the ball through the window. I held her. I did not know what else to do.

I had to put the notebook down at that. In nineteen years, I had never once seen Ray put his arms around his daughter. In our house, they shook hands.

Tessa had told him everything in a flood. The very bank where I was sitting had refused her a loan to expand, because the business was already stretched, and a friend had mentioned Larkmoor, which did not ask many questions. In April she sat in their office and signed her father's name, meaning to pay it back by Christmas. Ninety-five thousand had gone into the second building and the spa wing, which she rented, so she had poured it into a landlord's walls. Forty-eight thousand paid off an older private loan that was drowning her, and thirty-seven thousand kept her staff paid and her landlord quiet. By November the money was gone, and the default letter was on its way to a shop where her father opened the mail.

Looked at the signature. Wrong R. I make mine with a tail that goes back under. Whoever signed this lifted the pen in the middle. Blue ink. I have never signed anything in blue in my life, and Tessa knows it.

November sixth. Called Larkmoor. Man named Voss, soft voice. He said it was a timing issue, that they funded on Tessa's word and a notary's stamp, and that the title work would be done later. I asked if anyone had checked my signature. He said they lend against the house and who signs is between me and my family. Then he said that if payments stopped, they would foreclose and have the house sold by the end of February.

A dead man's note about a telephone call is not evidence, and I knew it. I wanted that sentence said again, out loud, where it counted.

On November eleventh he sat her down at the shop and had her write a page headed Acknowledgement, admitting that she had signed his name and promising to make it right, and he signed under her. I had that page in a plastic sleeve in front of me. Her handwriting was small and slanted and tight, not the hand of a monster but of a girl asking for more time.

Then Ray did the arithmetic I would have done, and something I would not have. He wrote, Lo's name has been on the deed nineteen years. Tessa never knew. The paper isn't good against the house. But I could call the sheriff and she would lose the business and her name in this town. Or I can pay it down quiet and give her time. I am paying it down.

After that, the entries were dates and numbers, three thousand here, four thousand there, all from the shop savings. Over seven weeks, from the first week of November until the week they took him to the hospital, he paid thirty-one thousand dollars to a lender with no right to a dime of it, to keep his daughter out of a courtroom I knew too well. All that time he came home smelling of brass and cold and told me the shop was slow.

On December eighteenth he wrote, Doctor says my heart is tired. I will tell Lo at New Year's, when she is rested. The last payment was dated December twenty-third. On the twenty-fourth I called the ambulance myself, from our kitchen, in my robe. Then there was a blank page, and then the last line, shakier than the rest.

I should have told Lo the first night. I wanted Tessa to be the one to say it.

I closed the notebook and broke the seal of the cream envelope that said For Loretta, because I had run out of reasons to wait.

It was one page in the same square capitals, and I will give you only part of it, because the rest was ours.

Lo. I am a coward about this and a poor hand at excuses, so I will skip them. You would have taken out your red pen and fixed the whole thing in an afternoon, and I could not stand being corrected by you in front of my own daughter. That is the whole of my reason, and it is not a good one. I am sorry. Give her one chance I gave her too late. Then do what you think is right, and I will be proud of it.

That was all. He signed it with a single R, with the tail that goes back under.

Winnie was out of the station wagon before I reached the curb, and she took one look at my face and pushed me into the passenger seat. She did not say a word, which in twenty-six years I had seen her do twice. She wrapped my shaking fingers around a cup of tea, and I told her all of it, in order.

When I finished, I could see the whole picture. Tessa had changed the locks not out of grief but to get into that house before I did and find his papers. As for the buyer with a closing at the end of February, there was no buyer. That was the date Voss had given Ray for the foreclosure, and Tessa had dressed it up as a sale, because a sale sounds better than a threat. No title company would have closed on a house whose second owner had never signed anything. She had been so busy locking me out that she never asked what her father had locked up.

She did not know about box four hundred seventeen. If she had, it would have been empty.

"Well," said Winnie at last, in a small whisper, "now what? Do we call the sheriff?"

"No," I said. "Everyone in this story has been in a hurry. I would like to ask a question first."

I went back inside, alone. Cecilia was at her desk. I told her only what I needed to, that I held the papers of a forged loan on my own house, that my stepdaughter had signed it, and that this bank had turned her down the spring before. Cecilia did not move for a moment.

"I knew her father," she said. "I did not know she had been turned away at my own counter." Then she folded her hands. "I cannot discuss anyone's account with you, Missus Kessler. But I can tell you where a fraud review begins, which is with the wire records. We have some, and I would put the file together quietly, if you asked."

She paused. "The bank can open a review, but you are the owner. How this ends with the person who signed is your decision, not mine."
