# The Drawer

At ten past four on Tuesday morning, with Boiler One humming and Esther Bloom's folder under my arm, I asked Dmitri Lazar to open his desk drawer.

The boiler plant has a little office tucked in a corner, the size of a closet, with a metal desk, a cot, and a coffee maker that Isaiah had been feeding since midnight. I sat on the cot with my reading glasses on and the folder on my knees. Dmitri stood in front of the desk with his hands hanging, and I could see the argument going on inside him. At last he pulled the bottom drawer open and lifted out a thick envelope.

"I kept a copy," he said. "Nobody told me to. The management company said it was being handled, and I took that to mean I should stop asking. But it seemed wrong for it to be only in one place."

I took the report from him. The cover said Ketterman Engineering Associates, and below that, Condition and Capacity Assessment, Ostrander Condominium, and a date in August. It was twelve pages long and stapled in the corner, and somebody had put a yellow tab on page three.

I read it all the way through, slowly, once for the meaning and once for the tone. The tone was what struck me. It was a polite, careful document, written by people who did not want to frighten anyone and were frightened anyway. Page three said that Boiler Two had reached the end of its useful life. It also said, in a sentence I read twice, that under cold-weather conditions the single remaining boiler would carry the full load of the building with no reserve, and that the loss of that boiler would leave the residents without heat. Page nine urged the association to restore the quarterly service contract at once. The new boiler was priced at three hundred and forty thousand dollars.

Then I opened Esther's folder. The minutes of the November meeting were inside, with her handwriting in the margin in green ink. I read the motion. Moved by the treasurer, Missus Vanessa Rudd, to defer the capital plan to the second quarter, pending a refinancing review. And beneath it, in Esther's neat hand, a note that said, V. called the report within acceptable risk. It is not. I voted no. Four to one.

I put both papers down on my knee.

What I understood, on that cot, was that nobody had made a mistake. I had told myself for a month that Vanessa was merely proud, and that I could forgive pride. But proud people don't write a motion with the right words in it. This was a decision, made on a spreadsheet, with the numbers in neat columns, nineteen thousand dollars a year saved on the contract and three hundred and forty thousand not spent, and a spring listing sitting in the corner of the page where nobody had to look at it. Someone had weighed a cold night against a clean number, and the number won.

"Aren't you going to take pictures?" Dmitri asked quietly. "I mean, if you wanted to."

"No," I said. "It isn't mine to photograph, and I'm not here to collect anything. I'm here because a pipe was knocking."

I did not call Vanessa. I thought about it, and my thumb actually found her number, and I put the phone away. Anything I said to her at that hour would have been about me.

"Dmitri," I said, "do you want your name on a thing like this?"

He swallowed. "I'm afraid of losing the job, ma'am."

"I know you are. That isn't what I asked."

"Yes," he said. "I do."

"Then here's how it's done properly. Tonight, while you still remember, you write down what you saw, with the times, in plain words. The cutoff locked out at twelve minutes after nine. You reset it twice. The doorman came down at nine forty. And you tell the whole board, not one person in a quiet hallway, so nobody can say later that it was a misunderstanding." I pushed a fresh sheet of paper across the desk. "Write it like a log, not like a complaint."

He picked up a pencil and began.

Isaiah had come to the office door with two paper cups, and he stood there a while before he spoke. When he finally spoke, he said it softly. "Missus Rudd, I've sat at that desk for twenty-two years. And the thing I know about this building is that everybody upstairs believes the heat is something that just happens."

"Most people do," I said.

"Somebody ought to tell them it's a person," he said, and he handed me a cup.

I drank the coffee, and I thought about Ferris. His last winter, there was a storm in the northern part of the county, a freezing rain that took down lines for sixty miles, and he worked through it for three days without sleep. At the end, a woman on the co-op's list of people who needed power for medical equipment did not make it through the second night. She was on the list. Everyone knew she was on the list, and the list had been filed in a drawer in an office. Ferris came home on the third evening and sat in the truck in our driveway for an hour with the engine off. I watched him through the kitchen window, and I didn't go out until he opened the door himself. Then he said the only thing he said about it, ever. Somebody knew, Connie, and it got filed.

I looked at the report in my lap, with its yellow tab, and I thought, that is a drawer.

I made my decision there, calmly, which is the only way I know how to make one. First the building, because a cold building takes no side. Then the truth, because it would keep until the building was warm.

"Come on," I told Dmitri. "The first blowdown stirred up everything that was lying on the bottom. We need to flush it again before the sun comes up."

We went out to Boiler One together, and he said the valve's name, and he opened it. This time the roar was shorter and the water cleaner,. We relit, and I leaned against the wall and watched the glass hold steady, and then I heard it.

It started small, a tick in the iron, and then another, and then a long, slow hiss that moved away from me up the wall. It was the sound I'd been listening for all night. Steam was climbing the north riser.
