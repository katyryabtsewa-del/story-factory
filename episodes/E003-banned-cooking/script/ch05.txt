# Table Fourteen

On the morning of the wedding I woke before the alarm, with Gregory's telephone call from that Monday still in my ears.

He had asked whether I had a ride, and whether my dress fit. Then he asked, in a voice stretched thin, whether I had "talked to anyone about the catering." I told him I was looking forward to a lovely day. He said, "Okay. Okay, good," and let out a breath I could hear across the city. I had told him nothing untrue. I had only told him less than I knew, which I had learned from him.

The wedding was on Saturday, the nineteenth of September. I put on my navy dress, the one with the three-quarter sleeves that Sam always said made me look like a senator's wife, and I fastened his wristwatch over my cuff. In my purse was the card with the pressed violet. It held a single index card now, and I did not yet know whether I would hand it to anyone. I drove out to Harbor Point Estate with a box of tissues and no food of any kind.

It is a beautiful place. A long green lawn ran down to Lake Mendota, the water the color of a new nickel, and on the lawn stood rows of white chairs with sprigs of sage tied to each one. Beyond them was the tent, enormous and white, with lanterns strung inside. A boy in a vest handed me a program, and I looked for the word kringle in it, and of course it was not there.

The ceremony was at four. They seated me in the front row, which I think was Richard's doing. Gregory came down the lawn in a gray suit, and I could see from fifty feet away that his hands were shaking. He kept opening and closing them, the way he did as a little boy about to recite something at school. When he reached the front, he looked at me for exactly one second, and I gave him the smallest nod I had.

Courtney came down the aisle in a gown that must have cost more than my first car, and she was very lovely. I did not begrudge it. Gregory's voice cracked on the word promise, and I cried a little into a tissue, because whatever else was true, he was my boy, standing in the sun, getting married.

Then came the cocktail hour, with servers in black carrying tiny things on spoons. I stood holding a glass of sparkling water, and three of Courtney's friends drifted over, wearing linen and pointing their phones at everything they ate.

"You're Gregory's mom," said one of them, a tall girl with a gold necklace shaped like a fork. "So lovely. And what do you do?"

"I worked in food service," I said.

"Oh, like a cafeteria?"

"Something like that," I said. "For a good many years."

She patted my arm and drifted away.

It was then that Richard found me. He is a large man, built like a refrigerator in a gray suit, and he walked across the lawn with a paper plate in each hand and no patience for the spoons. He set one down beside me, six little crab cakes on it, and sat. The bench groaned.

"I do not do fuss," he said. "Eat, Nora. There are seconds."

We ate crab cakes in the sun and said very little. Then he said, looking at the lake, "Gregory told me once that his mother makes the best pot roast in the state of Wisconsin. He said it at Easter, in front of Courtney, and then he looked as if he had said a swear word."

"He was being kind," I said.

"No. He was being honest, which is rarer." Richard wiped his fingers on his napkin. "I started as a stock boy, Nora. I know what it is to have people ashamed of where you came from." He looked at me with his small, shrewd eyes. "Do you know anything about this caterer?"

"I've heard they're very good," I said.

"Hm," said Richard Ashford.

It was true, and it was the only thing I said to that man all day that I did not enjoy.

At six o'clock the guests went into the tent, and I found table fourteen, exactly where the map had put it. The kitchen was in the big house behind the tent, joined to it by a covered walkway of white canvas. Where the walkway met the tent, a pair of swinging doors had been fitted into the wall, and every time a server pushed through, I got a draft of warm air that smelled of browned onions and sherry. My companions were two polite bankers and Richard's cousin, a quiet woman in lavender who ate her bread roll in three exact bites. She said, "Oh, you poor thing, you're by the doors," and I said I rather liked the doors.

The beet and burrata salad came first, and I would not have changed one leaf of it. Then the doors swung open, and the second course came out on a tray balanced high on a server's hand, and the smell reached me before the plate did. I knew it with my whole body. It was the horseradish cream and the browned onion and the sweet, dark pan gravy that my husband's mother had written out on an index card in nineteen fifty-one. The plate was set in front of me, and I looked down at my own pot roast, wearing a little sprig of thyme and a new name.

The banker on my left took one bite and stopped chewing. "This is the best thing I have ever eaten at a wedding," he said.

"Is it?" I said.

"It tastes like Sunday," said the cousin in lavender, almost to herself.

From the head table I heard Courtney's voice, high and bright, carrying across the tent. "This is what I mean!" she cried to her friends. "This is what authentic actually tastes like!" Her friends photographed the short rib from above, as though it might get away.

Beside her, Gregory had lifted his fork. He smelled the gravy first. I saw him do it. Then he stopped with the fork in the air, and his eyes went to the swinging doors, and then straight across the tent to table fourteen, to me. All the color left his face. He set the fork down untouched and reached for his water, and Courtney went on talking, and he did not hear one word of it.

I cut a piece of my own, and it was tender, and the gravy was exactly right. I ate it all. Over by the doors, one of the panels opened a few inches and a big man in whites looked out, found me in the crowd, and winked. Then the door swung shut again.

At the head table, the best man told a long story about a canoe and sat down to soft applause.

Then my son stood up, a glass in one hand, and reached out for the microphone.
