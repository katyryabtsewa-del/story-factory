# What He Said on the Terrace

My son had followed his bride out to the terrace while the tent was still on its feet, and I did what a mother does. I waited, I let the brown butter apple tart come to my table, and I ate every bite of it before I went to find them.

Priya bent down to my ear at the end of the course. "They're on the lake side," she said. "Mister Ashford went out and came back, and he said the choice is yours." She cleared her throat. "And Missus Pruitt, I looked it up. Section nine says family of the couple may not enter the kitchen. Strictly speaking, you are an owner. Marco says the door is open whenever you want it."

I thanked her, picked up my purse, and went out the side of the tent into the cool September dark.

Courtney was sitting on the stone railing in her gown with her satin shoes off and her mascara run down both cheeks, looking about fifteen years old. Gregory stood with his back to the lake and his tie pulled loose. When he saw me, he looked like a man watching a train come.

"Mom," he said. "I'm sorry. The toast, the lunch, the text. All of it."

"I know you are," I said. "That's not the same as being finished."

He nodded slowly, as if he had expected it. Then he sat down on the stone steps in his gray suit, put his elbows on his knees, and told me.

"I was eleven," he said. "The kids had started calling me Fish Fry Greg, and you told me that smell paid for my braces."

"It was the wrong thing to say," I said.

"No, Mom, it was right. That's the thing. It was true, and I was still so ashamed that I wanted to be somebody else, and then I was ashamed of being ashamed." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I stopped bringing friends home. When I got the job at the bank, I let people think you were retired from something fancy. Then I met Courtney, and her friends had opinions, and every time she said the word elevated, I heard, not us." He looked up, and his eyes were red. "I should have said at that lunch that my mother is the best cook in Wisconsin and she is making the cake. I was a coward. I'm still a coward. I just got caught."

It was hard to hear, and harder to say nothing. I wanted to put my hand on his head the way I did when he was small. But I thought of the promise I had made Sam, and I understood that protecting a son's pride is not the same as protecting him from what it costs.

"Courtney," I said.

She wiped her cheek with her wrist. "I didn't want to be the girl whose mother-in-law brought a casserole to her wedding," she said, very small. "My dad stocks shelves, Nora. Everyone in high school called us the hardware people, like it was a joke. I swore I would never be homespun. I used the word cute like a knife, and I knew exactly what I was doing. I wrote section nine myself." She took a shaky breath. "I'm not asking you to say it's all right."

"It isn't," I said, as gently as I could. "But thank you for saying it plainly. That is a start."

Then I opened my purse and took out the card with the pressed violet, and both of them looked at it as if it might be hot.

"I wrote this in January," I said. "I meant to give it to you in the receiving line."

Courtney opened the envelope. There was no check inside. There was a single white index card, and on it, in my handwriting, was the recipe for the pan gravy, down to the last teaspoon of sherry. Beneath it I had written one line. When you want to learn the real thing, come to Spaight Street on a Saturday and bring an apron.

"The money," Gregory said, hoarsely. "Mom, I didn't ask for it."

"I know you didn't, and that is the only reason I am not angrier," I said. "The money is staying with me for now. I think it has somewhere better to go. Now go back inside, both of you. You have a hundred and eighty guests and a father who has been standing at table one with his hands in his pockets for twenty minutes."

They did not hold hands, but they went in together, and that was something.

By the time I came back into the tent, the music had started and the floor was full, and people kept stopping me. A woman in green asked for the short rib recipe. A man with a white mustache told me his mother had run a diner in Racine and he had not tasted pie like that since nineteen seventy-one. The tall girl with the fork necklace came up with pink cheeks and said, "I am so sorry about the cafeteria thing," and I told her it was an honest mistake. I wrote my address on the backs of programs.

Richard Ashford asked me to dance, and we went around the floor like a refrigerator and a bird. "Nora," he said, in the middle of the second song, "in thirty-one hardware stores, we sell every kind of nail ever invented and not one decent jar of mustard. I intend to correct that. I am bringing a case to your next dinner."

"I haven't invited you," I said.

"You will," he said.

At midnight, Priya put her hand under my elbow and walked me to the swinging doors I had been staring at for six hours. We went through together. The kitchen was a tiled blaze of steel and steam, with forty people in white leaning on counters, and as I came in, every one of them put down what he was holding and began, without being told, to applaud. Marco stood at the end of the line with a tray of sour cream raisin pie bites. He bowed and handed me one, and I stood in my navy dress with my shoes in my hand and ate it, at last, inside a kitchen.

"Fire me?" he said.

"Give yourself a raise," I said.

On Monday morning I drove to Madison College, to a small office with a good view of a parking lot, and I signed my name seven times while a patient woman from the foundation put a gold sticker beside each signature. When I walked back to my car, my telephone buzzed in my purse. It was a text from my son, and I read it standing in the parking lot, just as I had read the first one in March.
