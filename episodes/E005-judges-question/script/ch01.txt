# Nine O'Clock, Courtroom Two

The lawyer for my son read my life aloud in a voice as flat as a kitchen table, and the clock above the courtroom door, which runs four minutes slow, said it was nine o'clock when he began. By the wristwatch I wear, which was my late husband's and is too big for me even on the tightest hole of the band, it was four minutes past. I looked at the watch and then at the clock and then at the watch again, because looking at the time is easier than looking at your only child while he tries to take your farm.

This was Courtroom Two of the Tamarack County Courthouse, on a Tuesday morning at the end of March. There were oak benches worn pale along the edges, a flag on a stand, tall windows letting in a thin gray light, and a heater beneath them that ticked every few seconds like a patient person tapping a fingernail. I had counted the steps from the door to the front bench on my way in, and there were eleven. I had counted the people in the gallery as I walked past them, nodding to each one as I once nodded to parents at the bus stop, and there were fourteen.

Pearl Dunbar was one of the fourteen, in her church dress and tennis shoes, with a purse on her lap the size of a feed sack. Three farmers I had known all my life sat in a row with their caps held on their knees, and I understood that they had come to find out whether I would still be standing at lunchtime. A soft-faced man in a pressed tie sat alone in the very last row with his hands folded, like a man waiting for a table at a restaurant. His name was Tucker Rand.

The paper in the lawyer's hands was called a Petition for Appointment of Guardian and Conservator, and my son Brent had filed it five weeks earlier. The lawyer wore a navy suit and spoke so quickly that he sounded like one of those podcasts people listen to while they fold laundry. He read that Vera Halvorsen, aged sixty-nine, was no longer able to manage her property or her person. He read that she had left a pot on the stove until it burned dry. He read that she had missed a property tax installment on twenty-four acres of pear trees, and that she had been seen wandering at the back of her land at dusk. After each item he paused, and the pause was the worst part, because it was meant to give the judge time to picture an old woman in a bathrobe.

I was not in a bathrobe. I had dressed in my good navy slacks and my canvas barn jacket, with my silver braid pinned up and my round tortoiseshell glasses polished on the hem of my blouse, because the jacket is the only coat I own that has never let me down. The last page of the petition, the lawyer said, asked the court to appoint my son as conservator of my estate, with the power to sell real property, and to place me in what he called supported living. I knew what that meant. It meant a room with a number on the door.

Brent sat at the other table, across a four-foot gap of carpet, in a charcoal blazer over a quarter-zip pullover, with his sandy hair combed so carefully across the thin place that I could tell he had used water. He is forty-four years old and he has a quick smile that he uses like a tool, the way another man might use a pocketknife. When the lawyer got to the stove, my son reached across the gap, laid his hand over mine where it rested on the table, and patted it twice.

"Mom," he said, in a voice pitched for the gallery, "nobody's blaming you."

The bailiff, a large and kind man who had nodded back when I said good morning at the door, studied the ceiling. My young lawyer, the one the court had given me, sat on my other side in a suit that had been slept in at some point, and he studied his notepad. I studied Brent's hand. It was warm and dry, and I had held that hand at every crossing in Tamarack Flats until he was nine years old.

I did not pull mine away. I drove a school bus for thirty-seven years, and I learned on the first morning that the way to keep forty children calm is to be the calmest thing on the road. I counted heads twice, once getting on and once getting off, and in all those years I never came up one short. I once drove forty-one third graders through a hailstorm with a substitute who did not know where the heater was. I retired at sixty-three, that spring after my husband Lloyd died of a stroke at seventy, and I have run Halvorsen Pear Farm ever since. It is twenty-four acres of Bartlett, Bosc and Comice trees, a white farmhouse with a deep porch, and a roadside stand with a hand-painted sign. The farm is in my own name, plainly, in the county records, and everybody in that courtroom knew it.

The habit of counting never left me. I count stairs, parked cars, people in a room, stakes in a field. Lloyd used to say I could not walk into a church without knowing how many hymnals were missing. I mention it now because before noon it would matter more than anything else I have ever done. I count because numbers do not change their story when somebody in a good suit asks them to.

I am telling you this from my porch in September, with the first Bartletts coming in, and I promise to tell you all of it, including the one question the judge asked me and what I answered. But I want you to sit in that room with me first, at nine o'clock by my watch, with the heater ticking and my son's hand on mine, because the answer only means something if you know how sure everybody was.

The lawyer turned a page. "Finally, Your Honor," he said, "the petitioner draws the court's attention to the most troubling pattern of all. Six calls to the Tamarack County sheriff, made by Missus Halvorsen over the past weeks, each reporting prowlers on her property. Six calls about prowlers who did not exist."

Brent patted my hand again. Judge Marsh was looking at the petition over the reading glasses she wears on a cord, and she did not look up. My young lawyer leaned toward me and asked in a whisper whether I was all right. I told him I was fine, and then, because it was true and I had been holding it for five weeks, I said quietly, "There were six calls. There were also prowlers. I counted them."
