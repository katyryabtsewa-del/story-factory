# Brooke at Midnight

My granddaughter was crying on the other end of the phone, the way a fifteen-year-old cries when she has decided not to, which is badly and out loud, and it was midnight on Thanksgiving, and I had agreed to sell my house two hours earlier.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry, Grandma. I didn't know about the Pinecrest part until today."

"Breathe first, honey. Then tell me."

She breathed. I could hear a space heater rattling beside her bed. She was at Pine Ledge Lodge, a few doors down from her father.

"There was a block on the family calendar last week that said Lakeside Cedar Retreat, which sounds like a candle. So I asked Dad, and he said it was a rental thing for January and not to make it weird. I said cool. That's what I said, Grandma. Cool." She took a breath that caught halfway. "Then today in the lobby he left his phone on the table when he went to talk to Dana. It was open to an email. It said, a room for my mother. It said Pinecrest Commons, and Portland, and January. I read it twice. I took a screenshot of that, too."

"All right."

"And Tyler knew. He said you'd be fine, that you'd be sitting by the stove with your crossword."

"I don't do crosswords."

"I know. I told him that." She gave a wet little laugh, and then the crying came back, quieter.

I believed her. I have sat across from a great many children who knew something was wrong and kept their mouths shut, and most of them weren't bad. They were scared that telling the truth would cost them the people they loved.

"Brooke, listen to me. Between saying a true thing and keeping the peace, the peace always costs more than people think. You called me. That was the right choice, and you made it at fifteen."

She was quiet. Then she said, "What is Dad going to do tomorrow?"

"You tell me."

"He wants to leave early. Dana's driving him crazy about the lunch reservation. And I was thinking." Her voice dropped. "I could slow us down. Take the long way, say I feel carsick, maybe pretend we had a flat. Then you'd have more time to get your stuff."

"Don't you dare," I said.

"Grandma."

"Brooke Ellery, you do not slow that car down for me, and you do not invent a flat tire. Your father can drive as fast as he likes. Sit in the back seat with your headphones on. And I'm going to ask you for one hard thing. Don't tell your father or Dana or Tyler what I'm doing. I'm not asking you to lie. If he asks you straight out, you tell him the truth. But you don't volunteer it."

"What if I can't?"

"Then you can't, and I will love you anyway." I meant it, and I also knew I was handing a fifteen-year-old a secret the size of a house. "When you get here, walk up to that front door and look at everything. The dock. The stove. The boots. Look at it the way it was."

She didn't say anything for a while. Then, very softly, "Okay."

"Get some sleep, sweetheart. And Brooke? Thank you for calling."

"Love you, Grandma."

"I love you more than the lake."

She hung up, and I sat there with the phone in my hand. Then I stood up and went out to the mudroom, because if I was going to be a woman who sold a house before breakfast, I was going to pack it properly.

I did not take the dining room table, which had been Frank's workbench, because Nan said it was part of a house that was selling with its kitchen, and because it would not fit through a farmhouse door on Mill Street anyway. I did not take the wood stove. I had stood in front of it half the night, and I knew that it belonged in that room, with a family coming who would need it, and not in a truck on a snowy morning with its pipes in blankets. A stove sold with a house should stay put.

What I took was a short list. I packed my clothes into six boxes and wrote the room on each in marker. I wrapped my sister-in-law's quilt in a clean sheet and laid it on the bed like a patient. I made a pile for the truck: my bed, the dresser, Frank's chair, the cedar chest, the good blue plates with the chipped rim. The mudroom was last. There were twelve pairs of my boots, six of Frank's, and a row of small rubber boots with faded ducks on the toes, which belonged to children who grew up and never came back for them. I kept the work boots that still smell like the boat, and my own best pair, and the ducks. I put them in six feed sacks, with masking tape and a name on each, and left the rest by the door for Jenna's children, who would grow into them.

By two in the morning I had the sacks lined up by the back door like a crowd waiting for a ferry. Then I went down to the boathouse and dragged the red canoe out onto the sawhorses. It is lopsided, as Frank always said, because he built it from a kit he never quite finished, and it has a crack in the bow that he patched with fiberglass and blue tape in nineteen ninety-three. The tape is still there, gone brittle as paper. I ran my hand along the patch in the flashlight beam. I told Frank I'd been right about the roof. I don't think he minded that I said it to a boat.

The notebooks I put in a shoebox, wrapped in a dish towel. There are three of them, full of dock depths, tide charts, and little sketches of the cove with arrows pointing at rocks. On the cover of the last one, in his square capital letters, he wrote: For Dot, in case. I have never known in case of what. I didn't want to know that night.

At about four, I made coffee and sat down with my boots still on. The windows went from black to gray, and the snow came down soft and steady over the cove.

At ten minutes to six, headlights came up the gravel through the falling snow, and behind them, the blade of a plow truck threw a long bright arc of light across the drive, and across the cove, and across the back of my hand. At the same moment my phone buzzed on the table.

It was a text from Craig, sent at five fifty in the morning, a time at which my son has not been awake voluntarily since college. It said, Can't sleep. Thinking we should come over early. I'll text you when we leave.
