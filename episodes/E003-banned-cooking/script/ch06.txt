# The Professionals

My son stood at the head table in the lantern light with a microphone in his hand, and I folded my napkin once and laid it in my lap, the way you do when you know a long course is coming.

Gregory began well. He thanked his new father-in-law, and Richard raised a hand from table one as if he were stopping traffic. He said that every day he woke up amazed Courtney had picked him. That part was sweet.

He had not touched his short rib since that first bite. He had watched the kitchen doors for an hour like a man watching a pot he was sure would boil over. Now he took a long swallow of champagne, and his shoulders changed. When Gregory is frightened and people are laughing, he reaches for whatever gets the biggest laugh, and it is never kind.

"I have to say something about the food," he said. "It has been unbelievable. Truly professional, every plate." He glanced at the doors. "My mom is here tonight, somewhere over there by the kitchen. She's probably checking on the gravy."

A few of Courtney's friends giggled.

"So I just want to say," Gregory went on, lifting his glass, "to my mother, Nora, who for once let the professionals do the cooking."

The laughter came all at once, Courtney's friends mostly, and men at the long tables who laughed because the others did. It rolled across the tent, bright and loud, and it did not come from table fourteen. At table fourteen there was complete quiet. The cousin in lavender put down her wine.

People always ask what I felt. It was not rage. It was the feeling of a stair missing in the dark. And I understood the joke. It was a man throwing something overboard so that nobody would look in the water. If the food belonged to the professionals, it could never be mine.

I laid my hand flat on the tablecloth, and for once I did not smooth it. I had nothing to say yet.

"And speaking of professionals," Gregory said, talking faster and louder now, "let's have a huge round of applause for Chef Marco Reyes and his team, who are back there making magic. Chef, come out here! Come take a bow!"

I think he meant to settle it. A bow, a handshake, applause, and the doors swinging shut on the whole matter. He had decided it was safer to put his own hand on the lid.

Courtney clapped and cried, "Bring him out!" My son invited him. I did not arrange it.

The swinging doors opened and Marco walked out in his white jacket, wiping his hands on a towel, with Priya a step behind him. Marco came up to the head table and shook Gregory's hand. "Marco," Gregory said, barely above a whisper. "Greg," said Marco. Then he asked, quite gently, "May I?" and Gregory, white around the mouth, handed over the microphone, because there was nothing else a man could do in front of a hundred and eighty people.

Marco's voice fills a kitchen, and it filled that tent. "I have never been prouder of a dinner than I am of this one, and I need to tell you why." He turned toward table fourteen.

"When I was nineteen years old," he said, "I walked into a restaurant on Willow Street called Pruitt's Table with everything I owned in a duffel bag and asked for a job washing dishes. A woman named Nora Pruitt showed me how to take apart a chicken. She taught me everything I know, and she did not charge me one dollar for it. I have known the groom since he was nine years old and did his long division in the back booth."

The tent had gone very quiet.

"The short rib you ate tonight is her pot roast," Marco said. "Horseradish cream and all, from an index card in her mother-in-law's handwriting. The brown butter apple tart, coming out in a few minutes, is her kringle. The little sour cream raisin pies at the late-night station are hers. I renamed them, because I was told the guests would be very food-literate." He paused. "I think you are."

At the head table, Gregory had gone the color of the tablecloth. Courtney was not looking at Marco. She was looking at Gregory, and I watched her read it in his face, that he had known this man all along.

"Months ago," Marco said, "I gave Chef Pruitt my word that I would say nothing tonight. She left me one door. If anyone made a fool of her in front of her guests, I could do as I saw fit. She did not ask me to use it, and she can fire me on Monday." He turned to the head table. "Gregory, you said she let the professionals do the cooking. The professional is sitting at table fourteen, and I would like everyone in this tent to know it. Nine years ago, Missus Pruitt sold her restaurant and put every dollar she had left into mine. She holds forty percent of the company that fed you tonight." He held out his hand toward me, palm up. "Ladies and gentlemen, my teacher and my partner. Chef Nora Pruitt."

I was not angry with him. I sat there with my face as hot as a griddle and Sam's watch ticking on my wrist, and all I could think was that a boy I had taught to break down a chicken had stood up for me in front of a tent full of strangers.

Richard Ashford stood first. He pushed back his chair at table one so hard that it fell over, and he began to clap, slowly, one clap at a time, looking straight across the tent at me. Then the banker at my table stood, and the cousin in lavender stood beside him and said, "I told you it tasted like Sunday." Then the whole tent was on its feet.

When it quieted, Richard called across the tent, "Nora, would you say a few words?"

I stood. My knees were not what they were, but they held. I smoothed the tablecloth and waved off Priya's cordless microphone, because the tent was so quiet I could hear the lanterns hiss.

"Thank you, Marco, and thank you all for eating it," I said. "The recipes were Sam's mother's, and all I ever did was keep them warm. Gregory and Courtney, I wish you a long marriage, and I hope there is always something on the stove."

I sat down. It was not much, but it was dignified, and it was mine.

Up at the head table, a chair scraped back. Courtney stood with her napkin crumpled in her fist, and she did not look at me, or at her father. She looked once at my son. Then she walked out through the side flap with her gown gathered in both hands. Gregory stared after her for one frozen second, set down his glass, and ran after her toward the terrace.
