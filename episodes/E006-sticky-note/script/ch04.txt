# Forgetful

On Wednesday morning, with Marisol's receipt in my cardigan pocket and the closing two days away, I did not call my daughter. I put on my coat and went next door to see Fern Albright.

Fern has lived beside me for thirty years. She is seventy-three, she wears a man's barn coat the color of a wet paper sack, and she drives a Ford truck that she calls Gladys. She has never softened a sentence to spare a feeling. When I sat down at her kitchen table and told her the whole of it, from the sticky note to the witness line, she listened without moving, her coffee going cold at her elbow. When I was done, she said, "That boy has never once looked me in the eye. I told Earl that, the day Nicole brought him to the Fourth of July."

"Earl said he was just shy."

"Earl was a lineman. He thought everybody was a wire." She stood up. "I'm already in my coat. Where are we going?"

"To the sheriff's office. Somebody there should hear this who isn't related to me."

"I know just the man," Fern said. "Ray Hatch married my cousin Dottie. He does the money crimes, and he does not miss a thing."

We rode to the sheriff's office in Gladys with the heater roaring and the ice cracking under the tires. It was a low brick building around the corner from the square, with a flag snapping in the wind. Detective Raymond Hatch met us in a small room with a table, two chairs and a window onto the parking lot. He was fifty-four years old and very tall, and he folded himself into his chair in sections, like a carpenter's rule. On the table in front of him lay a steel thermos and the morning crossword, half finished, in pen.

"Missus Ledbetter," he said, in a voice as slow and warm as sorghum. "I met your husband once, on the bank of Parksville Lake. He gave me a worm and told me I was holding my rod like a hymnbook. I've thought about that for fifteen years."

"That sounds like Earl," I said.

I told it again, in order, the way I would explain a pattern to a new girl. I laid Marisol's receipt on the table and the photocopy next to it. Detective Hatch put on half glasses and looked from one signature to the other for a good while. Then he did something I liked, which was to ask me to write my name on a clean sheet. I wrote it the way I always do, and the tick sat under the r like a stitch.

"Well, now," he said softly. "Ma'am, I believe you. I want you to hear me say that first."

"I hear it."

"But believing isn't proving." He took off the half glasses and folded them. "A signature missing a flourish is a signature a lawyer can argue about for two years. A young lady who typed what she was told to type, who is afraid for her job, is a witness a good defense attorney will pick at like a scab. And your son-in-law has done nothing yet but ask you to come to a meeting. What I need is for them to say it. Out loud, with you in the room, on a day when it matters. Mister Teague will say he remembers you. Mister Brandt will tell it to the lender. I need to hear both of them describe a day that never happened."

"And they will," I said, "if I sign."

"They'll do better than that." His eyes had the faintest glint. "They'll do it if you don't. Tell me, how does he treat you now?"

"Like I'm a cup he is carrying across a room."

"Then let him carry you." Detective Hatch tapped the table once with a long finger. "For two days, Missus Ledbetter, you are a forgetful old woman. You lose things. You repeat yourself. You do not sign one sheet of paper that isn't the one on Friday, and you ask every question a confused person would ask. People who think you can't remember will tell you everything, and they'll tell it twice, so you won't forget."

Fern made a sound like a kettle. "Josie's been practicing that act for forty years on customers who wanted a discount."

"Then she's got a head start," said Detective Hatch.

I went home feeling oddly light, the way I do when a gown that has been wrong for weeks finally has its seams marked. Fern said I should not call my daughter. I said I knew. That was the first time that day my chest hurt.

At four o'clock I took my glasses off their cord, wrapped them in a dish towel, and tucked them into the refrigerator behind the pickles. Curtis came at five, with a spiral ham in a foil pan and a brochure in his jacket pocket. He found me standing at the open refrigerator with a puzzled look on my face, which was not entirely an act.

"Josie, hon, what are you hunting for?"

"I can't recall," I said. "Something. It was on my mind."

He came to look over my shoulder, and there between the pickles and the butter sat a dish towel with my glasses folded inside it. Curtis looked at them. Then he looked at me, very kindly, and took out his phone and typed something with his thumb.

He set the ham in the oven, and he took out the brochure like a man laying down a good card. It was glossy and blue, and the title was Brain Health at Any Age. "I took the liberty of making you a memory screening appointment at the clinic in Cleveland. It's nothing, hon, it's only a few questions, and it would give us all peace of mind. Let's get ahead of it."

"That's thoughtful," I said. "Is it today?"

"Next Tuesday." He smiled. "And while I'm thinking of it, the ice is something awful, and I'd feel better if I held on to your car keys. Nicole or I can drive you anywhere you like."

I looked at the key ring in my hand for a long moment. Then I handed it over, and I said, "Thank you, Curtis. That's very kind. I'd better not be trusted with my glasses, either."

"They're right here, Josie. They were in the refrigerator."

"Were they?" I said. "Well, bless you."

He left at six. I put the glasses on, and I called Fern, and we laughed in my kitchen until we had to sit down.

At nine o'clock that night, my phone buzzed on the table. It was a text from Detective Hatch, with a photograph attached. It was a close picture of a single line on the last page of the power of attorney. I put on my glasses and read it. Under the word witness, in blue ink, in the round, careful handwriting I had watched grow up from crayon, were the words Nicole Brandt, December second.
