# Welcome Home

On the Sunday I checked in at Silverleaf Terrace, I knew where they meant to put me before anyone said a word, and I signed in as though I had seen nothing at all. It was Sunday, October eleventh, and I was there to find out what my daughter had paid two thousand five hundred dollars for. The lobby smelled of boiled carrots and hand sanitizer, which I would learn was the smell of every hour of the day. There was a gas fireplace with a fake log, a fish tank with one very old fish, and a sign that said Please Sign Our Guests In. Under Reason for Visit, I wrote, in my best dispatcher printing, respite stay, and under Time In, I wrote nine fifty-five.

"Miss Peggy!"

Gideon Pratt came around the corner with both hands out. He was forty-nine, I knew from his website, and he had a soft fleece vest with the Silverleaf logo over his heart, a lanyard, a whitened smile, and the kind of voice that sells you a carpet by describing how it will feel under your feet. He took my hand in both of his and kept it.

"We are just so glad you're home," he said.

"I've been here eleven seconds," I said.

He laughed as though I had been charming, and that was how I learned that he laughed at everything, and that none of it meant he was amused.

He gave me the tour. He called the dining room "the bistro," the activity room "the studio," and the beauty shop "the salon," and in every room he used the word home. The carpet had a pattern designed, I think, to hide anything that might be spilled on it. The Garden Wing was bright and a little warm, with wide doors and little shadow boxes of memories hung beside each room.

"And now," Gideon said, "just a little chat in my office. It's nothing at all. Every guest has one."

His office was glass on two sides, with a view of the front lawn and the fountain. On the desk were a bowl of mints and a coffee mug that said Welcome Home. He slid a clipboard from a drawer and said, "I'm certified to do this myself, so we can skip the red tape. It's a little brain game, Miss Peggy. Ten minutes. No wrong answers."

I said, "I have a habit. Would you mind if I wrote down the time?"

"Of course, of course." He smiled at my notebook. "That's adorable."

I wrote, in the margin, ten twelve. The clock on the wall, a square white one with a second hand, agreed with my wristwatch.

He read me five words and asked me to say them back: lantern, spoon, river, saddle, ash. I said them back. He gave me a sheet of paper and asked me to draw a clock face with the hands at ten past eleven. I drew a circle, put in the twelve numbers, and made the hour hand a little shorter than the minute hand, because the hour hand is shorter.

He asked me to name as many animals as I could in one minute. I got twenty-two, including a pika, a marmot, and an elk that had once walked through my petunias. He asked me to subtract seven from a hundred, over and over, and I went down to the sixties before he nodded. He asked me where I was, and I said Silverleaf Terrace in Alder Bench, Colorado. Then he asked me the date.

I said, "Sunday, October tenth."

He wrote something. It was the eleventh. I had been looking at the heron through the glass wall, wondering how long it had been since anybody had turned that fountain on, and I was off by a single day. I corrected myself before he could say anything, and he said, "No, no, that's fine," in the tone of a man who has just been handed a gift.

Here is what I noticed. When he started to score, he turned the clipboard over. He did it casually, as if the back were a nicer surface to write on, and he lowered his arm across it and covered it with his sleeve. In thirty-six years at a console I learned that when somebody's hand moves over a thing, you write that down. I did, in my own shorthand, while he was straightening his vest.

I looked at the wall clock. It was ten thirty-one.

"Well!" Gideon said, and flipped the form over again and slid it into a folder. "That was lovely. You did great, Miss Peggy. Really great."

"What was my score?" I asked.

"Oh, we'll go over that with the team. Let's not make this about paperwork." He stood. "Now, here's a small wrinkle. We're a little tight on beds in the Garden Wing this week. So for your stay, I'm going to put you in Birch Court. It's our neighborhood for folks who like a little extra care. It's just as comfortable. You'll hardly notice."

I looked at him for a count of five. I had read my name upside down in the lobby, and I let him say it anyway, because I wanted to hear how he said it. Then I said, "Let me read that back. I booked the Garden Wing, and you have decided to put me behind the locked door."

"It's not locked," Gideon said. "It's secured."

Birch Court was at the end of a second-floor hallway, and to get in, you stood in front of a gray door with a keypad on the wall beside it while he punched in four numbers with his body between me and his hand. He did not think about the sound. Each number gave its own small tone, and the lock buzzed. Behind the door, the hallway was long and quiet, with a handrail along both walls. On a corkboard by the nurses' station hung a row of laminated cards on yarn, and each card said Today Is, and then a blank, and in the blank, somebody had written a day of the week in dry-erase marker. They said Thursday. It was Sunday.

My room was small and clean, with a hospital bed and a vinyl chair, and its one window looked out on the parking lot. Hank's tan truck sat in the second row, looking like a dog left outside a store.

At noon I ate lunch in the Birch Court dining room, and at five I went down for the first dinner, and sat at a table for four. Across from me a very thin old man in a plaid blanket, with a hearing aid the color of a pink eraser, was cutting his meatloaf into exact squares. He looked at me over the top of his glasses, then pulled a surveyor's pencil out of a bag at his elbow, and slid it across the tablecloth to me.

"Forty-one paces to that door, ma'am," he said. "And in nine years, nobody has opened it for me from the inside."
