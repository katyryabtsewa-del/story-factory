# Bring a Pen

At nine o'clock on Tuesday morning, the thirtieth of September, I walked into the company conference room on four hours of sleep, with my purse on my arm and a pen in my hand, exactly as instructed.

The conference room is the nicest room in the building, which is not a high bar. It has a long laminated table, a window that looks out on the dolphin's tail, and a framed photograph of Boyd shaking hands with a state senator. Two gentlemen from Houston sat on one side of the table in dark suits, with their phones face down, looking as though they wanted this over before lunch. Tripp sat beside them, pale and straight. Boyd was at the head of the table in a crisp white golf shirt, with his sunglasses pushed up on his hair, as relaxed as if he were waiting on a haircut.

Otis Beaumont stood in the doorway behind me with his cap in his hand. He had not been invited, and nobody had told him to go.

"Mimi, honey," Boyd said, "there she is. Gentlemen, this is Mimi, who keeps this place running. She's been with me since the beginning."

I said good morning. The older of the two Houston gentlemen nodded politely. I sat down in the chair across from Boyd and set my purse in my lap.

Boyd had a manila folder in front of him. He drew out a single page, folded so that only the bottom showed, and slid it across the table to me with two fingers. There was a line, and under the line, printed small, the words qualifying agent.

"Just the annual renewal," he said, with the big smile. "Same as always. Sign right there, and we'll all get on with our day. Just sign, Mimi, like always."

It was nine-oh-one by the clock over the door. Be sure of your date, Wanda had told me, and I had been sure. Any day earlier, and Boyd would have had time to lie to Houston or move the closing. Any day later, the buyers would have learned the truth after their money had gone. Today, with them in the room, the ink and the truth would arrive together.

"I'd be delighted to sign it, Boyd," I said, in my best church-supper voice. "But I wonder if the gentlemen from Houston would like to know what it is I'm signing. It seems only polite, since it's their money."

"Oh, it's nothing," said Boyd. "A technicality."

"It's the annual renewal of this company's contractor license," I said, turning to the older man. "It has to be signed by the company's qualifying agent. That's the person who passed the state exam and who answers to the state for the company's work. And I thought you'd want to know, sir, that the person on file is me."

Nobody spoke, and out the window a gull landed on the dolphin's tail.

"The certificate has hung in the back office since the spring of two thousand thirteen," I went on, "in my name, Marilyn G. Lanier. Mister Haverty has hung his blazer right underneath it for four weeks."

Tripp had gone the color of skim milk. "I did ask," he said, quietly, to nobody. "He said the license was in order, and the number on my checklist said active. I took his word."

"I know you did, dear."

The older Houston gentleman turned his head slowly. "Mister Lanier, our lender's commitment requires a named qualifier in good standing on the day we close. Is Missus Lanier your qualifier or isn't she?"

"It's a technicality, gentlemen," Boyd said again, and a little of the shine was gone from his voice. "She's family. She does what I tell her. Mimi, sign the paper."

"I will gladly sign a paper," I said. "I just want to sign the right one."

I opened my purse. From it I took the state form Wanda Pickett had given me, with the seal in the corner, and beside it the letter Corinne Doucet had typed the Wednesday before, and I laid them side by side on the laminated table. I had practiced this the way a woman practices a pie crust, and my hands did not shake.

"This is my written resignation as qualifying agent," I said. "It's effective when I sign it. I'm sorry for the short notice, gentlemen, but I didn't think it would be right to let you wire a great deal of money without knowing."

I wrote the time at the top, for the board, the way I write the time on everything. It was nine-oh-four. Then I signed my name and the date.

Then I photographed the page and sent it to a small office above a bakery. The answer came in under a minute, a single word. It said, Filed.

"Boyd," said the younger Houston gentleman, in a low, even voice, "who's the qualifier now?"

Boyd opened his mouth, and no sound came out. I watched him look at the folder, at Tripp, and last of all at me, for the first time in some years.

"Mimi," he said, "you're doing this to hurt me. After everything I gave you. I'll have my attorney on you before lunch."

"Your attorney is welcome to call mine, Boyd. Nobody can make a woman keep a job, not even a husband." I said it gently, because I meant it gently. "I'm leaving you exactly what you always said I was. A clerk. And clerks can't pull permits." I turned back to the table. "Every open job has its permits pulled and its bills paid, and Otis's crews will finish every one. You have sixty days. After that you will want a qualifier."

The two gentlemen from Houston rose together. The older one buttoned his jacket. "Mister Lanier," he said, "we'll be pausing this transaction pending verification of the license. Our attorneys will be in touch." He turned to me and nodded. "Ma'am. Thank you for your candor."

They walked out, and Tripp stood last, looking at me with a face I almost felt sorry for. "I won't take a man's word again," he said. "Not about a thing that matters."

"Then you have learned it cheaply, dear," I said.

He nodded, and left. And then it was only Boyd, and me, and Otis in the doorway, and a plate of untouched pastries.

Otis went down the hall and came back in two minutes carrying a cardboard box from the copy room. Inside were my World's Okay Bookkeeper mug, the box fan with its cord wrapped neatly around it, and, on top, the framed certificate, which he had taken down off the wall without my asking.

"Yes ma'am, Miss Mimi," he said. "That's yours."

I carried the box out to the parking lot and set it on the hood of Gladys. Boyd came out after us, his golf shoes quick on the pavement, and stopped ten feet away with his sunglasses slid down onto his nose.

"Fine," he said. His voice was ragged. "Fine. I'll just take the test."

"All right, Boyd," I said. "It's Tuesday, November eighteenth. In Montgomery. Bring a pen."
