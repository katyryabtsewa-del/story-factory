# Corso

When my son said the word Corso over my pot roast, I set my fork down on the edge of my plate very gently, the way you put down something that might go off.

"That's wonderful, honey," I said. "I hear they're very good."

"They're the best in the city, Mom," Gregory said. "The head chef is some kind of genius. Courtney says he's been nominated for some big national award." He waved his hand. "You wouldn't know him."

I picked up my fork again and took a bite of pot roast. I want you to know that I tasted it, and that it was good, and that I said nothing more for the rest of the evening except to ask if anyone wanted more horseradish cream.

After they left, I washed the dishes by hand, dried each one, and put it away. Then I sat down at the yellow kitchen table and laughed, quietly, for about a minute, because I could not think of anything else to do with it.

Let me tell you about Marco Reyes.

He walked into Pruitt's Table on a Tuesday in the fall of nineteen ninety-six, nineteen years old, carrying a green duffel bag that looked as if it belonged to somebody else. He asked if we needed a dishwasher. Sam looked him over from the grill and said, "Can you stand up for eight hours, chief?" Marco said he could stand up for twelve. Sam hired him that afternoon.

He washed dishes for six months, and I noticed that he watched my hands more than he watched the sink. One slow Wednesday I caught him studying a whole chicken on my cutting board as if it were a puzzle, so I handed him my boning knife and said, "Come here. I'll show you where it comes apart." He broke down that bird badly, and then forty more that week, and by the end of the month he did it the way I do. I taught him pot roast and gravy and how to bring a pie crust together without ever warming it with your hands. Within five years he ran the line, and for fifteen years after that he ran my whole kitchen, and he called me Chef, which nobody else ever had, and sometimes Missus P. when he wanted a favor.

Marco is the closest thing to a second son I ever had, which is a hard thing to say about a man when the real one has just told you to say you worked in food service.

When Sam got sick, we sold the building. You can love a place with your whole heart and still not own the stove. After the debts and the taxes, the sale left me with one hundred eighty thousand dollars. A smaller woman would have bought an annuity. But Marco had a dream of his own, a restaurant with copper pans and brick walls on Williamson Street, and he had been saving for it since he was twenty-five. He came to my kitchen one night the month Sam was in the hospital, and he did not ask me for money. He told me he was going to open a place someday and he wanted me to eat in it.

I put the whole one hundred eighty thousand into it. He insisted on every piece of paper. We went to a lawyer, we signed in blue ink, and when it was done I owned forty percent of Corso Hospitality, a limited liability company, which means the restaurant and the catering company both. Sam signed as a witness at our kitchen table with a blanket over his knees, and he joked that he had finally gotten a restaurant he did not have to clean. I still have that page in the fireproof box.

In nine years I have never been to a Corso event as an owner. I get a check every year, last year it was ninety-four thousand dollars, and I go to the owners' meeting every January and eat in the back booth, where nobody bothers me. Gregory knew Marco had opened a place, and he believed I had lent him a small sum that was long since paid off. I had never corrected him, because he had never asked. When you spend years learning that your son is embarrassed by the work you did, you stop volunteering the work you do.

The Monday after the pot roast, I drove to Williamson Street at ten in the morning. It was not the day of the meeting and I had no appointment. The restaurant was closed, so I went around the side to the Corso Events office, where I had been exactly twice.

A young woman looked up from a tablet. She had a clipboard under her arm, black hair pulled back, and a lanyard that said Priya Nair, Events Manager. "Can I help you?" she said, and then her eyes went past my shoulder to the photograph on the wall behind me. It was a framed picture of a young man with a mustache and a woman in an apron, standing in front of Pruitt's Table with their arms around each other. She looked back at me, and at the photograph, and at me again.

"Oh my goodness," she said. "You're her. You're the Chef."

"I'm Nora," I said. "Is Marco in?"

Priya had been at Corso for two years and had never met me. Marco had been away in Spain most of April, and Priya had signed the Ashford contract on her own. She had seen a Missus Nora Pruitt listed as mother of the groom and had wondered, but she had not wanted to bother anybody on a guess.

Marco came out of the walk-in cooler in his whites, wiping his hands, and he saw me and opened both arms. "Chef!" he said. "Is it January already? Did I lose a whole season?"

"No," I said. "Sit down, Marco. I need to ask you about a wedding."

We took the back booth in the empty restaurant. I told him about the lunch and the text. Marco listened with his big hands folded on the table, and the longer I talked, the redder his neck got. When I finished, Priya slid into the booth beside him and set a folder in front of me without being asked.

"I think you should see section nine," she said.

I read it twice. It said, No outside food or beverage. Family members of the couple may not enter service or kitchen areas. At the bottom, in the margin, someone had typed, Added at client's request, C. Ashford.

"Courtney did that herself," Priya said quietly. "She was very specific. She said, and I wrote it down, that she did not want any relatives wandering into the kitchen trying to help."

Marco pushed his chair back so hard that it scraped. "Chef," he said, "say the word, and I walk out of that contract today."

I smoothed the tablecloth with the flat of my hand. It was an old habit, and it told them both to listen.

"Marco," I said, "don't change one thing."
