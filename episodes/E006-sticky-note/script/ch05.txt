# The Witness Line

The photograph on my phone showed one line of my daughter's handwriting, and I looked at it until the screen went dark and then I touched it awake and looked again.

Nicole Brandt, December second. The N had a little hook at the bottom, as it had since the second grade, when she decided that a hook looked grown up. I had seen that hook on birthday cards and permission slips. I could not make myself believe that she knew what she had signed.

I sat up most of that night in the sunroom with a half-finished choir robe in my lap, and I did not sew a stitch. I thought about Nicole at six, missing her front teeth, standing on a kitchen chair to write her name on a poster board in purple marker, so big it ran off the edge. I thought about her at nineteen, leaving for Knoxville with a trunk of clothes I had let out and taken in again. I thought about the January three years ago, at the cemetery in the freezing wind, when she held my elbow for an hour and never once let me feel that she was the one who needed holding. She was not a wicked person. She was a person who fixes things, and if you hand a fixer a broken thing and tell her it is her mother, she will pick up the tools without asking whose hands made the break.

Thursday morning at ten o'clock she came to my door with a pot of chicken soup wrapped in a bath towel.

It was the same soup she had brought on the nineteenth of November, the evening after my eye surgery, when I lay on the sofa with a plastic shield on my face and she fed me broth with a spoon. She set the pot on the stove, hung up her coat, and asked how I had slept.

I put the kettle on. I smoothed the tablecloth. "Nicole, hon," I said, in the vague, careful voice Detective Hatch had told me to use, "I keep having the feeling that I signed something in November. Did I? I can't recall it, and it troubles me."

She went still. She had been stirring the soup, and the spoon stopped in the pot.

"Mama," she said, the way she used to when she was small and had broken something. She turned around with her hand over her mouth, and her eyes filled up, and the whole of it came out of her at once.

"You did sign it. Curtis said you went in with Mister Teague on a Tuesday, and you did it all properly, and you were fine, Mama, you were so fine. And then you forgot. He said you felt silly about it, and that if I made a fuss you'd be mortified, and the kindest thing was to just sign as the witness, so the paper would be tidy, and not say a word to you. So I didn't. I signed it at our kitchen table. I didn't read much of it. He said it was just a line." She was crying openly now. "And then the glasses in the refrigerator, and the church, and I thought, he's right, and I didn't want to see it. I'm so scared you're slipping, Mama, and I was only trying to do it right."

I had never wanted to say a thing so badly in my life. It sat on my tongue as plain as a thimble. I did not say it. I got up, put my arms around her, and held on while she shook, and I said, "Hush, hush, hon. It's all right. I am right here," and every word of it was true and none of it was the truth.

What I saw, holding her, was the shape of the thing. Curtis had not needed my daughter to be wicked. He had only needed her to be afraid. Of all the stories that could explain a paper with my name on it, the one she could least bear was the true one, that her husband had lied to her. The one she could bear was that her mother was losing her mind, because a sorrow can be nursed. He had given her something to nurse, and she had nursed it for a month.

She ate half a bowl of soup, and I ate a whole one, and at the door she kissed me and said, "Curtis will come by for you at nine-fifteen on Friday. I have to open the office first, but I'll be there."

When the car had gone, I called Detective Hatch from the sunroom with a shaking hand. "I can't keep this from her," I said. "She is in pieces."

He let me finish. Then he said, unhurried, "Missus Ledbetter, I know. And I would give a great deal to say go ahead. But think it through. If she goes home tonight and says one word to her husband, he cancels the closing. You have no loan, no recording, no confession. He has a clean paper and a little time. And he tries again in six months, with something quieter, and by then your daughter has been told for half a year that you're failing. Tomorrow at ten she will learn it once, with the proof in her own hands. It is the kindest way I know, and also the hardest."

"It is the hardest thing I have ever done," I said.

"Yes, ma'am."

Curtis came at six that evening, with my mail in a rubber band and a pocketful of cheer. I had said I was going to lie down, and I lay on the daybed in the sunroom with an afghan over my knees and my eyes closed. The door to the kitchen stood open, because it always does. I heard him fill the pill organizer. Then his telephone rang, and he said, "Hold on, I've got my hands full," and set it on the counter, on speaker.

A man's voice came out of it, flat and tired, full of gravel. "Brandt. Tri-County. I want to hear it's real."

"Friday at ten," Curtis said, quite pleasantly. "It's all set. Closing's in the notary's office, the old girl signs, and the money's in the account by Monday."

"Two hundred twelve thousand dollars. The first of February. I don't take excuses."

"You won't need one. The loan covers most of it, and the balance I'll find. Once she's settled, she'll sign something else, and we'll call it estate planning. She's foggy, my friend. She put her glasses in the refrigerator. After Friday it's all in order."

The man grunted. The call ended. Curtis whistled again, and he put the last pill in the last little door, and he called, "Josie, hon? I'm heading out!"

I made a small sleepy sound. The front door closed. I lay perfectly still until his engine had faded down Hollis Street, and then I got up, walked quietly to my sewing table, and threaded a needle, because that is what my hands do when the rest of me is very angry.
