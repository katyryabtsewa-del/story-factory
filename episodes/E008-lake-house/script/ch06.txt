# Brooke at Midnight

When we left off, my granddaughter Brooke had called me at midnight on Thanksgiving, and she was crying the way a fifteen-year-old cries when she has decided not to, which is badly and out loud.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry, Grandma. I didn't know about the Pinecrest part."

"Breathe first, honey. Then tell me."

She breathed. I could hear a space heater rattling beside her bed and a phone case set down on a hard surface. She was at Pine Ledge Lodge, a few doors down from her father.

"The family calendar," she said. "Dad put everything on it, even the dentist. Last week there was this block that said Lakeside, and I thought it was a joke, because it said Lakeside Cedar Retreat, which sounds like a candle. So I asked him. He said it was a rental thing for January and not to make it weird." She took a breath that caught halfway. "I said cool. I said, cool, Dad. That's what I said."

"All right."

"And Tyler knew. Tyler knew the whole time. He said you'd be fine, that you'd be sitting by the stove with your crossword."

"I don't do crosswords."

"I know. I told him that." She gave a wet little laugh, and then the crying came back, quieter. "I didn't know they were going to put you in a room with a parking lot. I swear to God, Grandma."

I believed her. I have sat across from a great many children who knew something was wrong and kept their mouths shut, and most of them weren't bad. They were scared that telling the truth would cost them the people they loved.

"Brooke," I said, "listen to me for a minute. I'm not going to tell you it's not hard. You're going to have to choose a lot of times in your life between saying a true thing and keeping the peace. The peace always costs more than people think it will. You didn't keep it tonight. You called me. That was the right choice, and it was a hard one, and you made it at fifteen."

She was quiet. Then she said, in a smaller voice, "I took a screenshot."

"Of course you did."

"Of the listing and the calendar. I didn't know what else to do."

"That's exactly right. Send it to yourself, too, so it's somewhere besides your phone." I shifted the legal pad on the table. "Now. What is your father going to do tomorrow?"

"Drive the car. He's going to be on the phone the whole way. Dana wants to stop for a latte in Bangor." She sniffed. "Actually, I was going to ask you. I was going to try to slow us down. Like, take the long way, or pretend we had a flat. Maybe you'd have more time to get your stuff."

"Don't you dare," I said.

"Grandma."

"Brooke Ellery, you do not slow that car down for me. You do not invent a flat tire. Your father can drive as fast as he likes, and you are going to sit in the back seat with your headphones on, and when you get here you are going to walk up to that front door and look at everything. The dock. The stove. The boots. Look at it the way it was."

"What am I supposed to do after that?"

"Whatever you think is right," I said. "That's the only thing I have ever asked of anybody in my life, and I'm asking you."

She didn't say anything for a while. Then, very softly, she said, "Okay."

"Get some sleep, sweetheart. And Brooke? Thank you for calling."

"Love you, Grandma."

"I love you more than the lake."

She laughed, a real laugh this time, and hung up.

I sat there for a minute with the phone in my hand. Then I stood up and went out to the mudroom, because if I was going to be a woman who sold a house before breakfast, I was going to pack it properly.

The mudroom is the oldest room in the house. There are eleven pairs of my boots, six pairs of Frank's, including the work boots that still smell like the boat, and a row of small rubber boots with faded ducks on the toes, which belonged to children who grew up and never came back for them. There is one pair of ice skates nobody has worn since Craig broke his wrist on the cove in nineteen ninety-four.

I put all of it in feed sacks, by size, with masking tape and a name in marker on each one. By two in the morning I had nineteen sacks lined up by the back door like a crowd waiting for a ferry.

Then I went down to the boathouse and dragged the red canoe out onto the sawhorses. It is lopsided, as Frank always said it was, because he built it from a kit he didn't finish, and it has a crack in the bow that he patched with fiberglass and blue tape in nineteen ninety-three. The tape is still there, gone brittle as paper. I turned the canoe over and ran my hand along the patch in the flashlight beam. I told Frank I'd been right about the roof. I don't think he minded that I said it to a boat.

The notebooks I put in a shoebox, wrapped in a dish towel, on the top shelf of the closet in my room. There are three of them, and they're full of dock depths, tide charts, and little sketches of the cove with arrows pointing at rocks. On the cover of the last one, in his square capital letters, he wrote: For Dot, in case. I have never known in case of what. I didn't want to know that night.

The stove was the one thing I had already decided to keep. Nan said the stovepipe would need two men to come down, and that a stove that heavy ought to leave the house the way it came in, with respect. I wrote on the kitchen calendar to ask Ray's boy to bring blankets.

At about four, I made coffee and sat down with my boots still on. The windows went from black to gray, and the snow came down soft and steady over the cove.

At ten minutes to six, through the kitchen window, I saw headlights come up the gravel through the falling snow. They swung around the big cedar by the garage, and behind them, the blade of a plow truck threw a long bright arc of light across the drive, and across the cove, and across the back of my hand.

I stood up and reached for my coat.
