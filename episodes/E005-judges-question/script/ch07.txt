# The Question

Judge Marsh had said she had one question, and the heater under the windows chose that moment to stop ticking, so that I heard the whole room breathe in.

"Missus Halvorsen, you telephoned the sheriff six times," she said. "Tell me exactly what you saw."

Brent's lawyer was on his feet before she had finished. He said that the respondent had not yet been called, that she was represented by counsel, and that the court would be departing from ordinary procedure. The judge did not raise her voice. She said, "Counsel, I may question anyone in this room, and I have chosen to. Sit down." He sat down.

My young lawyer did not move. He folded his hands on his folder, and I stood up, because I was taught to stand when spoken to by someone who deserved it.

"Your Honor, I will give it to you as I used to give it to the transportation office," I said. "Six evenings. The first was a Monday, and I called at six forty-two. The second was a Wednesday, at six fifty-one. The third was a Friday, at six forty. The next Monday it was seven oh five, then Wednesday at six forty-eight, and the last was a Friday, at six fifty-five."

"Go on," the judge said.

"On the first three evenings there were two men, and on the last three there were three. They carried a tripod with a small telescope, a mallet, and orange stakes with pink ribbon. They drove one white pickup truck with a ladder rack. I wrote down the plate every time, on the back of an egg carton lid. It began with the letters K, T, R, and it ended in seven four one."

I reached into the pocket of my barn jacket and took out a rubber band holding six flat pieces of cardboard, and I held them up. The bailiff stepped forward, and the judge nodded, and he carried them to the bench.

"You did not ask me this, Your Honor," I said, "but I will tell you anyway. There are forty-one stakes. I counted them twice, going up and coming down. Nobody I know put them there. And on the driver's door of that truck somebody had peeled off a sign, but a sign leaves its shadow, and in the last light I could read three letters. They were B, A, S."

Judge Marsh looked at the egg carton lids as though they were the most reasonable documents she had been handed all year. Then she said, "Is the records clerk of the sheriff's office present?"

My young lawyer rose and said that she was in the hall, and the judge said to bring her in. The woman in the green sweater came down the aisle with her folder in both hands, took the oath in a small clear voice, and sat. She opened the folder and read the log aloud, one entry at a time, each one beginning with the dispatcher's words, report of suspicious persons, rural property, caller known to dispatch. Six forty-two. Six fifty-one. Six forty. Seven oh five. Six forty-eight. Six fifty-five. Every one was the minute I had named, and every one carried the plate in the dispatcher's own typing.

"There is also a registration return entered on the first call," the clerk said. "The owner is a business, Basalt Line Surveying, with an address in Medford. It was marked low priority, since no vehicle was present when the deputy arrived."

"B, A, S," the judge said, to no one in particular.

"Yes, ma'am," I said.

"Thank you, Missus Halvorsen. Please sit. Thank you, Madam Clerk." The judge turned her head. "Mister Halvorsen, you are still under oath. Would you return to the stand."

Brent got up slowly. His lawyer half rose, and the judge said, "He may confer with you after." My son walked past my table without looking down and sat in the witness chair, and for the first time all morning his hands did not know where to go.

"Mister Halvorsen," Judge Marsh said, "did you, or anyone acting for you, give those men permission to enter your mother's land?"

It was the plainest sentence I heard all day, and I knew within a second that it was the whole case. If he said no, then strangers had walked my rows, and my calls had been the truth. If he said yes, then he had given away land that was not his, and my calls had been the truth. I watched him realize that there was no door in the room that did not lead to me.

"Your Honor," Brent said, "I think it is more complicated than that. There are, ah, commercial relationships. It was really a timing matter."

"That is not an answer to my question."

"Mister Halvorsen," the judge said, and she said it gently, which was worse, "I will ask it once more. Did you or anyone acting for you give those men permission to enter your mother's land?"

He looked at his lawyer, who looked at his shoes. He looked at the last row, and Tucker Rand became interested in his own knees. Then my son looked at me. I did not look away, and I was not trying to hurt him. I only wanted him to see that I was still there.

"Yes," Brent said.

"Yes, you gave permission."

"Yes, Your Honor. Under an agreement. With a developer."

"Did you tell your mother about this agreement?"

"No."

"Did your mother give you authority to speak for her land?"

"No," he said, very quietly, and he put his hand over his mouth.

Judge Marsh set her glasses on her nose and said, in the same flat dry voice she had used all morning, "Then your mother has been telling the truth about her own back acres since late February."

Pearl, I learned later, had unwrapped a peppermint and forgotten to put it in her mouth.

"The court finds that there is no evidence, much less clear and convincing evidence, that Vera Halvorsen is unable to manage her affairs," the judge said. "The petition is denied. The clerk will forward a copy of this transcript to the county's civil authorities, as appropriate. Missus Halvorsen, you are free to go home."

The gavel came down. It made a small flat sound, like a pear dropped on a floorboard. In the back row, Tucker Rand was already on his feet, buttoning his jacket and going quietly toward the door, like a man leaving a restaurant when the check is coming.

Brent stepped down from the witness stand and stood in the aisle with his arms hanging, looking at me. I looked at the door. I had begun, without meaning to, to count the steps to it, eleven, the way I always did. My young lawyer put a hand out toward my elbow, and I told him thank you, but no. I put both palms on the table, and I stood up on my own.
