# The Mailbox in Camas Junction

Danny had stepped down, the judge had left the door open to a temporary order, and Tucker Rand was smiling in the last row. I sat very still and thought about a Thursday five days before, when I drove into town to find out why I had never seen a property tax notice.

I had been reading the petition at my kitchen table that morning, and the third item stopped me. It said I had missed an installment, and that the notices had gone unanswered. I have paid my taxes in person, with a check from my green ledger, every year since Lloyd and I took over the farm, and I had no memory of any notice. Then I began to think about what else I had not seen lately, and I realized that I had not received my electric bill in seven weeks, or the seed catalogs I order every winter, or the card my cousin sends me every February with a pressed flower inside.

I put on my barn jacket and drove my old truck to the Tamarack Flats post office, which is a low brick building with a flag and a bench, and a postmaster who used to ride my bus. She sat in the third seat on the left for six years, with a lunch box shaped like a horse. She is forty-five now, with reading glasses on her head, and when I came through the door she looked up from the counter and her face changed.

"Missus Halvorsen," she said. "I was going to come see you. I have been trying to work out how to do it all week."

"Then I saved you a trip," I said. "I think my mail has gone missing."

She took off her glasses, looked at the two people in line behind me, and said, "Can you come around the back?"

We stood in the sorting room, among the gray bins and the smell of paper and rubber bands, and she opened a drawer and took out a small card. It was a change of address form, the kind anyone can fill out in two minutes, and it said that Vera Halvorsen of the old farmhouse at the end of Orchard Hollow Road had moved her mail, effective immediately, to a postal box in Camas Junction. It had come in an envelope, she said, not at the counter, which was why nobody had asked for identification. It was dated seven weeks before the hearing, two weeks before the first white truck had come up the county road.

"I should have called you," she said. "It did not feel right. But the signature matched the card on file, close enough, and I am not supposed to second-guess a customer's own form."

I looked at the signature. It read Vera Halvorsen in blue ink, in a hand that was almost mine. But the sevens in the date were crossed in the middle with a short bar, the way they teach it in some schools, and I have never in my life crossed a seven. I know only one person who does, and he is my son, because I sat at the kitchen table with him at eight years old and tried to cure him of it and failed.

"Thank you," I said. "Could you put my mail back the way it was?"

"I already did," she said, and she was nearly crying. "Yesterday. I was not going to sit there and let it go on."

I should tell you that I did not cry either. I drove to the end of the post office lot, put the truck in park, and sat there with both hands on the wheel for what my husband's watch told me was forty minutes. I thought about the sevens. I knew my son was ashamed, because I had seen it at Christmas, in the way he would not look at the pear pie. But a man can be ashamed and still go on doing it. I also asked myself why, in five weeks, I had never telephoned anyone about those three letters on the truck door. I had three letters and no name, and a woman who goes asking strangers who is staking her land is a woman whose stakes get pulled up in the night. I had left the question with the sheriff, which is what the sheriff is for.

Then I drove home by the river road, on one of those clear, cold March afternoons when the whole valley looks as though somebody has washed it, and I walked the rows. The buds on the Bosc were still tight and brown, a week or so from breaking, and in the north corner the orange stakes stood in the grass with their pink ribbons lifting in the wind. I stood a while with my hand on a tree that Lloyd's father had planted, and I told it that I would not be going anywhere.

The courtroom clock said ten thirty-six, and my watch said ten forty, and Judge Marsh looked at both of them in turn, as if she had a private opinion about the clock, and called a recess of fifteen minutes. "The court will take up the request for a temporary order when we return," she said.

Brent stood up with his lawyer, and they went out through the side door together, speaking low. Tucker Rand got up from the last row and followed them with his hands in his pockets. I waited until the gallery had moved, and then I took the long way out, past the water fountain at the end of the hall, because I needed a drink and because my knees had gone stiff.

The old courthouse is all tile and marble, and it carries sound the way a drum does. I was still around the corner when I heard my son's voice, low and tight, saying, "Once she's under, the timing works. The petition goes through, I sign, and you have your authority by the end of the month."

"That is what we were told," said a soft voice I knew from the back row. "I will be honest, Brent. We are a little concerned about how the sheriff's reports look. Our people already got what they needed from the ridge."

"The judge isn't going to look at the reports. They're just a deputy saying nobody was there."

"Of course," said Tucker Rand. "It is only a timing matter."

I stood with my hand flat on the cool marble, and I did not move, and I was not angry. What I felt was the thing I used to feel at the top of a mountain grade in an ice storm, with forty children behind me, when I knew that the next thing I did would be the only thing that mattered. I counted. Six evenings. Forty-one stakes. Two men, then three. One plate, ending seven four one. And I decided, very calmly, that if anybody ever asked me, I would give them every number I had.

I turned the corner and walked down the hall without my drink. Pearl was sitting on a bench with her feed-sack purse in her lap, unwrapping a peppermint, and she looked up and studied my face.

"Vera Halvorsen," she said, in her loudest whisper, "you look like you are about to drive a bus."
