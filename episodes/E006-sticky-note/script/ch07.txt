# Look Under the R

At ten o'clock on Friday morning I climbed the stairs of Teague Title and Notary for the second time that week, with a recording running in my right pocket and a clear envelope in my purse.

A telephone shaped like a black starfish sat in the middle of the conference table, and out of it came the bright voice of a woman from Harlan Ridge Lending. Gordon Teague sat at the head in a red bow tie, clicking his pen cap faster than he had on Tuesday. Curtis sat across from me in his camel coat. Nicole sat at my right, and she took my hand under the table and squeezed it.

The stack lay in front of me again, and this time nobody called it Medicare. I tilted my head at it, as a confused woman would.

"Curtis, dear," I said, "remind me. What are we here for?"

"Josie, hon." He said it with great patience. "We're closing a loan on the house. You gave me the power of attorney in November, and today you only confirm it."

"A loan?" Nicole said. "Curtis, you told me it was paperwork. You said it was Mom's affairs."

"It's the same thing, honey. A formality." He did not look at her. He looked at me.

"The power of attorney," I said. "Remind me, when did I sign that?"

He glanced at the telephone. "The eighteenth of November. A Tuesday. You wore your blue church dress, and you signed twice, because the first pen smeared. You made a joke about the pen."

In my left pocket, Earl's button had grown warm in my fist. "Did I?" I said. "Gordon, tell me what you remember."

The pen cap clicked. "Two-fifteen," Gordon said. "A Tuesday afternoon. You were very much yourself, Josie."

"I can't recall a thing. Was it raining that day, Gordon?"

"It was dry," he said, too quickly. "Dry as a bone."

"Mister Teague, it rained until five," said a quiet voice from the doorway. Marisol stood there with her spiral notebook. "I mopped the front mat twice."

Nobody moved. From the speaker the bright voice said, "For the record, we need the notary to confirm the identity of the borrower, and that she signed the earlier power of attorney in person, and that she is signing today of her own free will."

"Of course," Curtis said, and he laid a hand on the stack.

"One moment," I said. I took off my glasses and folded them. "I would like to answer that one myself."

I slid the discharge packet from Riverbend Eye Surgery Center out of the clear envelope and turned it to face my daughter, and I laid Fern's print beside it, with the wet parking lot shining behind me.

"On the eighteenth of November," I said, "I was in Cleveland, Tennessee, having my left eye operated on. At two-fifteen I was in a recovery chair with a plastic shield taped to my face, drinking apple juice from a paper cup. At twelve minutes past three, Fern Albright took that picture in the parking lot. She sent it with one word. Pirate."

Nicole picked up the print with both hands. Her eyes went from the picture to the date on the discharge sheet, and I watched it arrive in her face.

Gordon had stopped clicking. His forehead shone. "Well," he said. "Now. It may have been the Wednesday. I get my days tangled."

"Wednesday," Curtis said, quick as a man catching a dropped plate. "Wednesday afternoon. That's right. Josie, you got your days mixed up."

Nobody had planned for that, not Detective Hatch and not I. I had only what I knew, and I laid it out slowly.

"On Wednesday the nineteenth," I said, "I lay on my sofa in a dark room with a patch over my eye, and the surgeon had told me not to go anywhere. I did not put on my blue church dress, and I did not sign anything with a smeared pen. Nicole, where was I that evening?"

"On your sofa," my daughter said, in a very small voice. "With the shield on. I brought you chicken soup, and I fed you with a spoon. Gordon, I was there."

"I have not been confused about anything today," I said. "Marisol, did anyone named Ledbetter come into this office on Tuesday the eighteenth, or on Wednesday the nineteenth?"

"No, ma'am. Neither day." She held up her notebook. "And I kept a receipt."

I asked Gordon to pass me the power of attorney from his file, and he did, with fingers that would not hold still. I drew a blank page from the stack and signed it slowly, so that nobody could miss a stroke. Josephine A. Ledbetter. And under the last letter, my little flick.

I slid it across to Nicole, beside the forged page. "Look under the r," I said. "For forty years I have signed with a knot. Earl said a seamstress ought to knot her thread. Now look at the paper that says I signed in November."

Nicole looked. Gordon looked. The knot was on the page I had just written, and on the receipt Marisol set beside it. On the forged page there was only the flat end of the r.

"This is a great deal of fuss over a flourish," Curtis said, and his voice rose a little. "Nicole, she's confused. You saw the glasses in the refrigerator."

"She just read me a hospital paper out loud, Curtis," Nicole said. "Word for word. Times and everything."

From the starfish telephone the bright voice said, a great deal less brightly, "Mister Brandt, in light of what I have heard, Harlan Ridge Lending is declining to proceed. The application is withdrawn, and no lien will be recorded against this property. Please do not contact this office again."

Gordon Teague picked his pen cap up off the table, held it against his chest, and began to cry quietly.

Then Curtis's telephone buzzed on the table. The screen lit face up, and I could read it from where I sat. Marlow Bridge Capital. He turned it over, fast, but Nicole had seen it too. She leaned toward him and said, so softly that I nearly missed it, "You let me sign it."

The slatted door at the back of the room opened, and Detective Hatch came out with his thermos in his hand.

"Nicole, honey," Curtis said, turning to her with a face I had never seen on him. "Let's not do this here."

My daughter stood up. She did not say anything at all. She walked to the window and stood with her back to us, looking out at the gray square.

"Mister Brandt," said Detective Hatch, mildly, "I'd like you to come with me for a conversation."

Behind me I heard the scrape of Gordon Teague's chair as he got to his feet, before anyone had asked him to, and in the quiet that followed I understood that I had not raised my voice once.
