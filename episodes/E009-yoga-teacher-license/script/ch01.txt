# The Waiting Room

My husband told me he was leaving me on a Thursday morning in August, in a plastic chair at Greer's Tire and Brake, with a ballgame playing on the waiting-room television and a paper cup of free coffee going cold in my hand. His truck needed two front tires. I had come along because my Buick, whom I call Gladys, was getting her oil changed in the next bay, and because Boyd Lanier liked an audience, even when the audience was his wife.

"Mimi," he said, "I need to tell you something, and I want you to hear it as a gift."

I said I always enjoyed a gift, and I waited. On the television a man in a red cap hit a ball clear over a fence, and the three other people in the room, a teenager and two retired gentlemen, cheered as if it had been their own idea.

"I'm not growing here," Boyd said. "Skye says I have so much unlived life, and she's right. I'm moving out Saturday. I wanted to tell you somewhere public so we could both stay civilized."

Skye was his yoga teacher. Boyd was sixty-eight years old, and he had been going to her classes on Tuesdays and Thursdays since March, in stretchy pants he had bought on the company card, which I had noticed, because I notice everything that goes on the company card. I had supposed it was a bad knee. I had been very generous about the knee.

I did not scream. I would love to tell you that was strength, but mostly it was that the coffee was hot enough to be useful and the teenager was watching us over the top of his phone. Forty-one years of marriage had also taught me something about Boyd. He could not be argued with, in public or in private, but he could be outwaited.

"Well, Boyd," I said, "bless your heart. Will you be wanting the good towels?"

He laughed, that big white-toothed laugh that has sold more swimming pools than any brochure ever printed, because he thought I was being brave and sweet. That was the first mistake he made that day. He made a great many more, and I kept a receipt for nearly all of them.

So that you can settle in and not wonder where this is going, I will tell you now. By Thanksgiving, my husband would lose his buyer, his crew, and his right to sign a single new contract, and I want to be fair to him: he had it coming for forty-one years.

I was not thinking that in the waiting room, of course. In the waiting room I was thinking that the man at the counter had just been told his brake job would run three hundred dollars more than the estimate, and that I had never seen anyone look so much like me.

"One more thing," Boyd said, and he stretched his legs out and crossed his ankles, the way he does when he is about to be reasonable. "Tripp is coming in Tuesday to look at the books. Just so you're not blindsided."

"Tripp," I said.

"Tripp Haverty. The growth consultant. I told you about him, honey."

He had not told me about him. He had told me about a Houston fellow who might help with marketing, in the same voice he used for the weather. But I said, "Of course," and I drank the cold coffee all the way down to the grounds, and when the man behind the counter called out that the pickup was ready, Boyd stood up, stretched like a cat in a sunbeam, and went to pay with the company card.

I rode home in Gladys by myself, and I want to tell you who was driving her.

My name is Marilyn Lanier, although I was a Gaudet before that, born in Bayou La Batre, Alabama, where the shrimp boats outnumber the stoplights. Everybody has called me Mimi since I was in diapers. I am sixty-five years old, five feet and one inch tall on a good day, and I wear my reading glasses on a beaded chain my daughter made in the fourth grade. In nineteen eighty-eight, Boyd and I started Lanier Blue Water Pools out of a rented shed. He had the big laugh and the sunglasses on top of his head, and I had a ledger and a card table.

Thirty-seven years later, the company lived in a low cinderblock building on Highway Ninety-Eight in Dauphin Landing, with a blue awning and a fiberglass dolphin on the roof that had been grinning at the traffic since the Clinton administration. We had three trucks, eleven employees, and a little over three million dollars a year in business. Boyd's face was on the door of his white pickup. My face was in the back office, behind a desk with a box fan on it, and I was perfectly happy there. I paid the bills, ran the schedule, answered the complaint line, and knew which customer had a husband in the hospital and which one had a dog that bit the pool man. When something went wrong, nobody asked for Boyd. They asked for Mimi.

Boyd paid me thirty-eight thousand dollars a year, which he called family compensation, as though that explained something. Whenever I brought him a form, a permit, an insurance notice, anything with small print on it, he would put one hand on my shoulder and say, "Mimi, honey, it's just paperwork." He said it so often that the crew gave me a mug for my sixtieth birthday that says World's Okay Bookkeeper, and I drink out of it every morning.

When I got back to the office that afternoon, the building was quiet. Otis Beaumont's crew was out on a job in Fairhope, the girls at the front desk had gone to lunch, and the box fan was turning its slow head from one side of the room to the other. I set my purse on the desk and stood there with my hands folded, the way you stand in a house after the company has left.

On the wall behind my chair, where it had hung for twelve years, was a framed certificate from the state. Boyd had walked past it, by my own rough count, twenty thousand times, and I do not believe he had ever once turned his head. I had dusted it every Friday. I had known whose name was on it since the day I hung it, but I had never looked at it the way you look at a thing that might be about to matter.

I took off my glasses, wiped them on my blouse, put them back on, and read it the way you read a will, looking for who gets what, and what happens on the morning that name walks out of the door.
