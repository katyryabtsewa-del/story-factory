# Six Evenings

My son had just told the judge that I believed men were hiding in my trees, and I will say plainly that I did not believe it. I knew it, and I had known it since a Monday evening in late February, five weeks before that morning.

I was walking the north rows at dusk, as I always did, with my jacket buttoned to the chin and the light going orange behind the ridge. I heard an engine on the old county road that runs along the top of my land, and then a truck door, and then voices. I went up between the Bosc trees as quietly as I could, and from two rows back I saw a white pickup truck with a ladder rack parked at my north gate. Two men stood in my trees. One was setting up a tripod, the kind with a little telescope on it, and the other was walking a line through the grass with an orange stake in one hand and a mallet in the other.

I watched him drive the stake into the ground and tie a bit of pink ribbon on the top. It was the second stake I had seen. I looked behind him and there was a first, and a third, and by the time the light began to go I had counted eleven.

I stepped out into the row and called, "Can I help you gentlemen?"

They both turned. The one with the tripod raised a hand, in the way a man waves at a neighbor on a tractor, and neither of them answered. Then they walked to the truck, put the tripod in the back, and drove away down the county road with their lights off until the curve. I stood in the dusk and thought what I used to think when a stranger stood too long at the bus stop. I do not know you, and you have not told me your business, and I am going to remember you.

I had a pencil stub in my jacket pocket, because I have carried one since the bus days. I had a lid from an egg carton, too, because I had been collecting Pearl's eggs that afternoon. On the back of the lid, in the last light, I wrote down what I had seen. The truck was white. It had a ladder rack. On the driver's door there was the ghost of a sign that somebody had peeled off, a pale outline with a few letters still faintly readable, and I wrote those down as well. Then I wrote the plate, which began with the letters K, T, R and ended in seven four one. I do not guess. I count, and I write it down while it is fresh.

I went into the house and called the sheriff's office at six forty-two. The dispatcher knew my voice, which is not an honor everyone would want. I told her what I had seen and gave her the plate, and she said she would send a deputy.

The deputy was Danny Ocampo. He came up my drive at about seven fifteen with the lights off, which was thoughtful, and took off his hat on my porch. He is thirty-six now. I remember him at six with a mitten on each hand, the boy who never lost a mitten in nine years and always said thank you when he got off the bus. He walked the rows with me by flashlight. The truck was gone. The stakes were not, though he only looked at the ground where I pointed and wrote in his notebook, and said, "I'll put it down, Miss Vera."

After that it became a pattern, and the numbers are the point. The second evening was a Wednesday, and I called at six fifty-one. The third was Friday, at six forty. The following Monday it was seven oh five, then Wednesday at six forty-eight, and last of all a Friday at six fifty-five. Six evenings, Monday, Wednesday and Friday, two weeks running. Each time the same white truck. The first three evenings there were two men, and on the last three there were three. Each time I wrote it down on a new egg carton lid, and each time I called, and each time, by the time a deputy turned up my drive, the truck was gone and the men with it, as if they had been timing it. Danny came out for five of the six. The sixth time another deputy answered, and he was late, and he did not look at the stakes at all.

By the end I could not walk the north rows without counting. There were thirty-three stakes by the second Wednesday and forty-one by the last Friday, and I knew because I went out the next morning and counted them twice, once going up and once coming down, the way I did with the children. Nobody had told me. Nobody had asked. Lloyd's trees stood in the middle of it like cattle that had been branded without a word to the rancher.

The clerk called the next name, and I was back in the courtroom with the heater ticking.

"The petitioner calls Deputy Ocampo," Brent's lawyer said.

Danny came down the aisle in his tan uniform with his hat in his hand. He is a big, steady young man with a gentle face, and when he passed my table he did not look at me, which I understood, and then he did, which I appreciated. He sat down in the witness chair like a boy who has been sent to the principal's office by mistake.

"Deputy," the lawyer said, "you responded to calls from Missus Halvorsen?"

"Yes, sir. Five of them." He cleared his throat and looked at me and said, "Miss Vera. I mean, Missus Halvorsen. I am sorry, ma'am."

"That is quite all right, Deputy," the judge said, without lifting her eyes from her notes. "Go on."

"And at each of those five calls," the lawyer said, "you found no prowlers on the property."

"No, sir. None present when I arrived."

"No suspicious persons. No trespassers. No one at all."

"Not when I arrived, sir. No."

"Thank you." The lawyer sat down with the air of a man who had put a very large book on a very small shelf.

My young lawyer stood up, so slowly that his chair squeaked. He asked only two questions. First he asked whether, in five visits, Deputy Ocampo had ever found anything Missus Halvorsen told him to be false. Danny said, "No, sir. Not once." Then he asked whether there had been anything on the property that the deputy had noted.

"Yes, sir," Danny said. "On my fourth visit I noticed orange survey stakes with ribbon along the north boundary. I put it in my notes. Orange stakes with ribbon, north boundary, no known contractor."

"No known contractor," my young lawyer repeated, and sat down.

Danny stepped down. In the back row, Tucker Rand, who had been sitting with his hands folded for an hour, leaned forward and put them on his knees.
