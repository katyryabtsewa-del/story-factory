# Daybreak

At five minutes past six on Tuesday morning, steam reached the radiators on the sixth floor of the Ostrander, and I heard about it from a man shouting down a stairwell.

"It's ticking!" he called. His voice came down five flights, thin and cracked and astonished. "Six-A is ticking! Mine's warm, it's warm, I've got my hand on it!"

Isaiah's radio crackled in his pocket, and then another voice joined in, and another. In the lobby, where about forty people sat wrapped in coats and sleeping bags and bedspreads, there was a small, tired cheer, the sort you hear at the end of a long church service, when the sermon was fine but everybody is ready for coffee. A woman in a ski jacket began to cry quietly into her husband's shoulder. Somebody said, "Thank God," and somebody else said, "Thank the lady with the wrench," and I said, loudly enough for the whole room, "Thank the pump seal."

That got a laugh. I did not want to be thanked. I wanted every riser in that building heating before I let myself feel anything at all.

Dmitri had lent me a pair of canvas coveralls with his name stitched over the pocket, three sizes too long, and I had rolled the legs at the ankles. I was greasy to the elbows. I had been on my feet most of five hours, and my knees had started to file complaints. But I made a slow circuit of the building anyway, floor by floor, with Isaiah at my side, laying my palm flat on the radiators in the north wing, one after another, the way a mother tests a forehead. They were warming. By the time I got to six, they were warm enough to ache.

Esther Bloom had not stopped for a moment. Sometime around five, she had written a notice on a legal pad in her teacher's hand, and Isaiah had run it through the copier in the management closet, and by six she was riding the elevator from floor to floor with a stack of paper in her lap, a stack the young man from five-C carried for her. At every door she bent, slowly, with her cane hooked on her arm, and slid a sheet underneath. I took one from the carpet outside six-D. It said, in black ink, Open Meeting of All Residents, Gallery, Tuesday at three o'clock in the afternoon. Subject, the boiler, the engineering report, and what we do next. Please attend. Esther Bloom.

I sat down at last on the bottom stair of the lobby, with a thermos that somebody had pressed into my hands, and while the building warmed above me I thought about gray boxes.

Optional. That was the word that had stayed with me. When a hospital loses a boiler at three in the morning, they call the chief engineer, and nobody asks whether she is available, because it is understood that she is needed. Not once in thirty-four years had anyone put me in a gray box. And when I'd driven out of Fort Wayne the night before, I had passed the exit for Saint Brendan's, and I had looked up at the plant chimney, glowing against the sky with its little red light, and a thought had come to me that I did not like. A building full of strangers had a use for me, and my own son's home had a line on a spreadsheet. I'd kept driving. Now, sitting on that stair, I was not sure whether the thought had been fair to Neil. I only knew that I'd had it.

At twenty past seven, a black sedan with a car-service sticker in the window stopped under the arch, and my daughter-in-law got out of the back.

She was wearing a camel coat. Her hair was perfect, and her face was not. She was holding her phone in front of her like something that had bitten her, and I learned later that she had slept at a hotel near the restaurant because of the ice, with her phone still silenced from the dinner. Forty missed calls, all from the Ostrander, all between a quarter past nine and five, and she had plainly been reading them all the way north. She came in through the revolving door, and she stopped.

She saw the lobby. She saw the fireplace, and the neighbors asleep in chairs, and the line of people who had just come down for a look at the woman in the borrowed coveralls, three or four of whom were shaking my hand. A man in a bathrobe said, "God bless you, ma'am." It took her a moment to see who they meant.

"Constance," Vanessa said. "What are you doing here?"

"Keeping your building warm," I said.

"I didn't ask you to."

"I know you didn't." I said it as gently as I could, because it was true, and because I'd decided long ago that I would never be the kind of woman who uses the truth as a stick.

She opened her mouth, and for once she did not have a sentence ready, and I saw something move behind her face that was neither anger nor shame yet, but was on the way to being one of them. Before either of us could speak, the front door swung open again, and in came a tall man in a long charcoal overcoat, dragging a small black suitcase, with silver hair and a face that looked as though it had been awake for twenty-six hours. That was Graham Tolliver, the managing partner of Vanessa's firm, who lived in the penthouse. He had left the dinner early for a late flight to Boston, and the same storm that trapped my son had held his plane on the ground until it was cancelled. He had turned on his phone at six and come straight from the airport.

He did not say good morning. He looked at the lobby, and then at Dmitri, who had come up from the basement to stand rigid by the elevator.

"Mister Lazar," he said. "What happened here, and how long has anyone known it could happen?"

Dmitri told him, in about ninety seconds, with the times. Tolliver listened without moving, and then he turned to Isaiah and asked the same question, and got an answer that was shorter and slower and exactly as clear. Then he turned to the small, straight woman on the cane, who had come over without being called.

"Since August, Graham," said Esther. "The whole board had the report, and you did not."

Nobody said anything. I looked at the floor. It is not a skill I'm proud of, but I know how to look at a floor.

Tolliver stood with his hand on the handle of his suitcase for a long time. Then he turned, and he spoke quietly, with no heat in it at all, to the woman in the camel coat.

"Vanessa," he said. "Four-B. Ten minutes. Bring the report."
