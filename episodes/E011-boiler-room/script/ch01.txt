# Overflow

The phone rang at nine forty-four on Monday night, and I knew who it was before I picked it up, because only one man in Chicago had ever promised to use my number, and he had promised to use it only if something was truly wrong.

I was standing at my kitchen counter on Hessen Street in Fort Wayne, Indiana, in my stocking feet, with the space heater ticking beside the table. I don't trust that space heater. After thirty-four years in the boiler plant of a hospital, I know what a cheap heating element does to a rug, so I keep it three feet from anything that burns and I shut it off every time I leave the room. The wind was leaning on the back door, and the radio had promised minus nine degrees before morning, with gusts, the kind of number that makes a woman with my background stop and listen to her house.

"Missus Rudd," a slow, deep voice said. "It's Isaiah, at the Ostrander. I'm sorry to call so late. It's making that sound again."

Before I tell you what I said to him, I'll tell you how this was going to end, because I've never cared for a story that hides the ball. By morning, ninety-six households would know exactly who had been keeping their building warm and who had been keeping it cheap. I want to say now that I never meant it to go that way. I meant to go up there, do my job, and drive home again, and I would have been content with that.

Three weeks earlier, on the Friday after Thanksgiving, an email had arrived from my daughter-in-law, Vanessa. It went to the whole family, and the subject line read Christmas Run of Show. I'll admit I had to ask the girl next door what a run of show was. She told me it was what a theater uses to schedule a performance, minute by minute, so that nobody backstage is surprised.

There was a link, and the link opened a spreadsheet with little colored boxes. Christmas Eve was a cocktail evening in the Rudd apartment, which was four-B, for Vanessa's boss and eight clients, described as curated, adults only, and work-adjacent. Christmas Day belonged to Vanessa's mother in Naperville. And at the very bottom, in a gray box, was my name. It said Constance, Overflow, December twenty-seventh, eleven o'clock to twelve thirty, optional, venue to be determined.

In my trade, overflow is what happens when a tank is full and the water has to go somewhere, so it runs down a pipe in the floor and nobody thinks about it again. I read that gray box standing up, in this same kitchen, and I laughed once, out loud, because the alternative was less dignified. I have been a daughter, a wife, a mother, and the first woman ever hired into the boiler plant at Saint Brendan's Hospital. I had never before been optional.

My son Neil answered that email with one line to everyone. It said, Looks great, V. He is forty-one years old, he is a kind man, and he has never in his life crossed a room to disagree with his wife. I wrote back, Thank you for the schedule. Then I put the Christmas card I had bought for the two of them into the drawer under the toaster, and there it stayed.

I should tell you who was standing in that kitchen. My name is Constance Rudd, but everyone calls me Connie, and I was sixty-nine years old. I retired four years ago as chief operating engineer of the plant at Saint Brendan's, which means that for a long time the heat, the hot water, and the steam that sterilized the surgical instruments were my business, down in a basement where people only visit when something is wrong. My husband Ferris was a lineman for the electric co-op, and he died six years ago in March, on a Tuesday, of a stroke, with his boots by the door. I have a canvas coat with the hospital's name still sewn on the pocket, reading glasses on a cord, and a pipe wrench in the trunk of my car, out of habit and not because I expect to need it.

Isaiah, who is a patient man, was still on the line. "I'm standing at the boiler room door," he said. "I came down because the young man asked me to. Dmitri's been resetting it, and he doesn't like it, and I don't like it."

"All right, Isaiah," I said. "Three questions. First, is the burner trying to light, or is it locked out?"

"There's a red light on the panel, ma'am. Locked out. It went at twelve minutes after nine, and he's reset it twice."

"Second, what does the water glass show?"

I heard him talk to someone, then come back. "Dmitri says it's clean but it's jumping. Low and then not low, low and then not."

"Third. How cold is it in the north wing?"

"The folks up there started calling the desk at nine-forty. The radiators on five and six are already stone cold. I've called four-B, Missus Rudd. Dmitri tried the younger Missus Rudd at a quarter past nine, and I've tried six times since, and it goes straight to the message. She's at the company dinner downtown. I tried Mister Tolliver's penthouse too, and nobody answers."

I knew where Neil was, because he had texted me at suppertime. His flight back from Minneapolis had been cancelled, and he was one of several thousand people sleeping in a terminal at O'Hare, with a telephone that was nearly dead. He could not have helped, and I knew it, and it did not make me feel any better.

"Tell Dmitri not to reset it a third time," I said. "Tell him to look at the float chamber on the low-water cutoff first, and to look at it with his own eyes."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And Isaiah, you remember what I told you in November. When it knocks, and then gasps, and then goes quiet?"

"That's the sound," he said. "That's exactly the sound."

"Then it's the cutoff, and it may be lying, and it may be telling the truth. I'm coming."

"Missus Rudd, I didn't call to ask you to drive. I only called to tell somebody who'd know."

"I know that," I said. "That's why you called me."

After he hung up I took an envelope out of the recycling, the electric bill, and wrote on the back of it the number of miles from my front door to the Ostrander, which was one hundred and sixty. I did that because I like to see a job in numbers before I begin it. Then I put on my coat, pulled the plug on the space heater, and took the car keys down from the hook. The wrench was already in the trunk.

I stood a moment in the doorway of my dark, tidy, quiet kitchen, with the cold coming in around my ankles.

"Well," I said out loud to nobody. "I wasn't invited to Christmas."
