# Thursdays

I did not turn around in that parking lot. It took me six weeks to do it, and I will tell you how it went, because it did not happen in the loud way people expect. It happened on ordinary mornings, with the kettle on.

The state surveyors stayed at Silverleaf Terrace for five days. They pulled every screening form for the last two years, and they sat with the director of nursing, who, to her credit, told them everything she knew and some things I think she had been afraid to know. Fern called me on the Monday after to read it back to me. In fourteen residents' files, the screening scores did not match the notes, the times, or the staff on duty. Fourteen people had been moved behind that keypad door on a number that was not theirs. In November the state placed a hold on all new admissions to Birch Court. The locked wing could not take a single new resident until somebody proved it could be run honestly, and the owner's lender, I heard, was not pleased.

Silverleaf paid a fine, a large one, though I was told it was smaller than the one they had earned. Gideon Pratt and the building's administrator were, as the letter to the families put it, no longer with the company. I have thought about Gideon since. I do not think he went to bed planning to hurt anyone. I think he looked at a number, and then at a person, and each time he chose the number it got easier. That is worth watching for in yourself, and I try.

My deposit came back. It came by check, two thousand five hundred dollars, with a letter that said Silverleaf regretted any inconvenience. I deposited it in my own bank with the new card in my own wallet. Cyril's niece, who had not been told about the fall until Fern told her, drove up from Pueblo and moved him to the Garden Wing that week. By Christmas he was home in his own house with a lift chair, a visiting nurse, and his niece's dog, and he sent me a card that said, in his small square hand, One hundred and four paces from the front door to the kitchen. All open.

Imelda works days now, with a second aide on nights, and her knees are the same, but she says she sleeps. She calls me Miss Peg, and I have given up asking her to stop.

Things were harder at Dana's end. When the news came out, the other facilities that had paid Hearthstone Senior Advisors called her one by one, politely, and ended their contracts. Dana's husband sold two of the trucks. The banker who had told them he would not meet unless they paid seventy-five hundred dollars had, it turned out, read about all of it in the paper, and he sat down with them in December anyway and stretched the balloon payment out over three years. I never found out whether that was decency or arithmetic. I have noticed that in a small town they are very often the same.

On December sixth, a Sunday, my daughter knocked on my kitchen door. She had no folder and no purse, just a pair of car keys in her fist. It was the first time in my life I had seen Dana come into a room without something to hand me.

I made tea. We sat at the table with the cracked yellow top, and the aspens outside the window were bare and the color of pencils. She said nothing for a long time, and I did not fill the silence.

"I thought I was helping," she said at last.

"I know you did."

"I thought it was how things work. Everybody gets a fee. Everybody says it's for the family." She turned the mug in her hands. "I used your card, Mom. I told myself I'd pay it back before you noticed."

I said, "Dana, I would like to ask you for something. It's an old habit of mine. Would you read it back to me? What you did, in order, and what it did to people. Not what you meant by it."

She did. It took twenty minutes, and she did not look away once, and when she finished, she was crying again, but not the way she had cried on my bed with the cake. I did not hug her. I was not ready, and she did not ask me to be.

"I'm going to tell you what I can do," I said. "I can't make it all right. I can offer you one condition and one Thursday. First, you call every family you ever placed at Silverleaf, every one, and you tell them what you now know, and you help them get their records. Second, you come with me on Thursdays and sit with the people on the other side of that brochure. You don't need to fix anything. You only need to sit."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she said, "What do I say to them?"

"Nothing, to start. You'll be surprised how much of it is listening."

In January I began the thirty-six hours of training that the state requires of a volunteer ombudsman. Fern taught half of it, and she handed out granola bars at every break, and I learned about resident rights and complaint forms and how to ask a person what they want rather than what you think they need. I wrote the time in the margin of every page.

Lu drove me to my first Thursday round in April in her own car, because she said she wanted to watch. I wore a lanyard with a badge that said Volunteer Ombudsman, and my own name underneath. It was a different building, on the other side of the valley, with a different smell, mostly bread. I walked down a hallway, and a call light came on over a door, a small white bulb, and I went to it and knocked and asked what the lady needed. It was a glass of water. I brought it. I looked at my watch, and it had been one minute and forty seconds, and I wrote that down, too.

Down the hall, Dana was sitting beside a woman in a wheelchair with a jigsaw puzzle between them, not talking, just turning pieces over. She looked up at me, and I raised my hand, and she raised hers.

That evening I sat at my kitchen table with my tea and watched the buds swell on the aspens. Hank used to say to write it down, Peg, you will forget the good parts too, and he was right, so I have.

So I would like to ask you something, and I would truly like to know. Would you have forgiven Dana, or only let her sit in the hallway? And tell me where you are listening from tonight, whether it is a kitchen, or a porch, or a long road. I always like to know where the voice lands.
