# The Arrangement

To explain why I stood behind a trellis and said nothing, I have to take you back six years, to a Tuesday in April and my own kitchen table.

Trevor had come over after work, still in his tie, and he sat down without taking off his coat, which in my house is how you know a person is in trouble. He slid an envelope across the table with two fingers. It was a letter from a collections agency. A friend of his had opened a restaurant, and Trevor had co-signed the loan out of loyalty, and the restaurant had lasted fourteen months. The friend had moved to Arizona. The loan had not.

"It's fine, Mom," Trevor said. "It's going to be fine. It's just that my credit score fell off a cliff, and now the bank won't touch us."

Us meant Trevor and Cassidy and Jonah, who was two years old at the time and asleep in a car seat in my driveway. Cassidy had found a house, he told me. Forty-one Tamarack Court. She had walked through it three times and cried in the kitchen. It was the house she wanted for the rest of her life, and with Trevor's credit in pieces, it was gone.

I looked at the letter for a while. A bookkeeper does not answer a question like that until she has done the figures, so I did them in my head while my son watched me. I had Gerald's life insurance, one hundred fifty-two thousand dollars, sitting in an account and doing nothing, because I had not yet figured out what a widow does with the price of her husband.

What I decided, over the next two days, was this. I would buy the house myself. I would put down ninety thousand dollars of Gerald's money and take out a mortgage for the other two hundred twenty-two thousand, thirty years, with Harbor Light Credit Union, in my name and only my name. Trevor and Cassidy would live there and pay me rent. Nobody would have to be embarrassed in front of a bank.

Trevor cried a little at my table, and then he asked me for a favor. "Mom, please don't tell Cass how we set it up," he said. "She's embarrassed enough already. Just for a while."

I told him a year or two, and not a day longer. I wish I could say that I made him sign something. I did not. He was my son, he was crying at my table, and Gerald had been gone just over a year.

What Trevor told Cassidy, as far as I ever learned, was that his mother was "on the paper for my credit" and had helped with the down payment. It was true as far as it went. Cassidy never asked how far that was, and in six years she never once asked to see a mortgage statement, a tax bill, or an insurance renewal.

Here is how the numbers worked. The mortgage payment, with taxes and insurance, came to two thousand four hundred dollars a month. Trevor and Cassidy sent me eight hundred dollars on the first of every month, by bank transfer, labeled "groceries," which was Trevor's idea, so that Cassidy would never ask. I covered the other sixteen hundred. I also paid twenty-eight thousand dollars to have the basement finished into a small suite with a bedroom, a bath, and a kitchenette, because I thought that when I was old and could not manage my own stairs, I might want a place to land near my grandson. Later I paid nineteen thousand five hundred for a new roof, and five thousand eight hundred for a furnace that quit on the coldest night of the third winter.

I wrote it all down. Every payment, every repair, every receipt, in three green clothbound ledgers, one page for each month, in pencil first and then in pen once I was sure. I hummed while I did it, mostly "Blessed Assurance."

And for a long time, I will tell you honestly, it was a good life. On Sundays I drove over at two o'clock with a pan of lemon bars, and Cassidy would say, "Oh, Marlene, so retro," and take one anyway. Trevor carved. Jonah learned to ride a bicycle in the circle at the end of the street while I stood at the curb with my hands over my mouth. He lost his first tooth in my kitchen, in a lemon bar. A woman can carry a good deal if she believes it is holding up a roof with people she loves under it.

The first crack came at Thanksgiving, last November. I had hosted that dinner at Tamarack Court for five years running, with my mother's gravy boat and my own turkey. Cassidy called me the week before and said she was doing a "curated tablescape" this year, for a magazine, and it would be simpler if I just brought the rolls.

"The rolls," I said.

"Just the rolls, Marlene," Cassidy said. "Honestly, it's less stress for you. You've done so much already."

I brought the rolls. They were very good rolls. I was seated at the end of the table nearest the kitchen, beside a bowl of decorative gourds, and nobody asked me to pass the gravy because there was none.

In January, I drove over for Sunday dinner and found that the garage keypad would not take my code. For five years, the code had been the date of Gerald's and my wedding anniversary, which Trevor had picked himself. I stood in the cold and tried it three times. Then Cassidy opened the front door in a cream sweater and told me she had changed it for security. She would text me the new one. She never did.

What she texted instead was a link. It was for a calendar app called Hearthline, and it contained, in a soft green color, a schedule for what she called "Grandma time." My time slots were Thursdays, from four to four thirty. That was thirty minutes, and you had to request an extension at least forty-eight hours in advance, with a reason.

I looked at that little green box for a long time. I did not argue. I wrote it in my ledger, and beside it, in pencil, the word "noted."

Then, one evening in February, I saw a photograph Cassidy had posted of the front steps of Tamarack Court, with a wreath of eucalyptus and a handwritten chalkboard. Under it, in her curly little script, she had written: "Home is where WE built it."

I put down my phone. I went into my study, where I keep the ledgers, and I opened the third green book to the last page. I ran my finger down the final column, past the roof and the furnace and the suite, to the very bottom, and I added it up in pen. When I got to the total, I stopped humming in the middle of a line.

It was one hundred sixty-eight thousand five hundred dollars. I had never added it all at once, and I sat there a long time wondering who, exactly, the word we on that chalkboard was meant to include.
