# Ninety Days

When Bruce Whitfield asked his daughter whether he had ever given her a dime, forty people in a backyard stopped breathing at once, and the only sound was the bounce house, wheezing along as if nothing had happened.

"Daddy," Vanessa said, "I was going to explain."

"Did I give you the down payment for this house?"

"You gave us the wedding."

"I gave you a wedding, and I gave you a very nice honeymoon," Bruce said. "I never gave you a house. I stood on this patio and took a bow for fifty-two thousand dollars that came out of another woman's pocket, and you let me." His face had gone gray under the golf tan. He set the champagne flute on a table with great care, as if it were someone else's. Then he turned to me. "Missus Brandt, I owe you an apology. I don't know how I'll make it, but I owe it."

"Thank you, Bruce," I said. "You can start by not blaming Piper's party."

He nodded once. He took his wife by the elbow, and she looked back at the macarons, and at her daughter, and then at me, and for the first time that year she gave me a real look. "Get your purse, we're going," Bruce said, and the two of them went around the side of the house without another word.

The party broke apart like a plate. People collected their children and their paper bags, and the photographer lowered her camera and left. A couple of mothers touched my arm on the way out. One of them murmured, "Good for you," and another said, "I'm so sorry, I had no idea." Vanessa went into the house and did not come back out.

Which left me with a cake.

I want to tell you that I stood there and felt triumphant. I didn't. I stood on a patio in my good navy dress and thought about a child who had been promised a birthday. There were still fifteen guests left on the lawn, mostly small ones who had not noticed anything but the macarons, and Piper was coming out of the bounce house with her braids sticking up and her face flushed red.

"Nana, are we doing cake now?"

"We're doing cake right now," I said.

Derek came and stood beside me without a word. He lit the candles, seven of them, in the lemon glaze. We gathered the children around the dessert table and sang, and my son's voice cracked on the second line and held on through the rest. Piper counted the candles out loud, twice, in case they had changed. She blew them out in two tries and then I put the knife in her hand, put mine over it, and we cut the first slice together.

"Nana," she said, very low, while the other children were reaching for plates. "Why is Mommy crying in the window?"

I looked up. A shape stood behind the upstairs curtain, and then it was gone. "Sometimes grown-ups find out something they didn't know, sweetheart, and it surprises them. That's all. It has nothing to do with you."

She thought about it, with a lump of frosting on her thumb. "And you didn't do something bad?"

"No, sweet girl," I said. "Nobody did anything bad to you. You tell me if anyone says different."

"Okay," she said, and she licked her thumb, and ate her cake.

Derek came to my duplex at nine o'clock that night, with his jacket on over his apron, and he sat on the very edge of my couch like a man waiting for a dentist. He told me all of it in a flat, tired voice. In twenty sixteen, Vanessa's boutique had failed, and she had filed for bankruptcy, and she had asked him never to tell anyone. For years afterward her credit was ruined, and a loan with her name on it would have been turned down at the door. So when he was laid off and the house came along, he told her the bank had taken care of everything. "I was trying to protect her," he said.

"You were trying to protect yourself from a hard conversation," I said, "and you were charging it to me."

He nodded. He did not argue. It was the first honest thing he had said in six years, and I let it count.

We met at Daniel Okafor's office on Tuesday, November third. On the first of the month, my phone had gone ding one last time, the seventy-second payment, and I had let it go, because Daniel had told me that the paper must be finished before the paying could stop. Derek sat on one side of the conference table in a borrowed tie. Vanessa came in a gray suit, with no makeup and no phone, and she took the chair farthest from mine. She did not look at anyone.

Daniel laid it out in his plain, steady way. The ninety days would end on January fourteenth. Derek could refinance in his own name, but at today's rates the payment would come to about two thousand nine hundred dollars a month, and on a salary of seventy-one thousand dollars the bank would never agree. So the house would go on the market. From the sale, the first fifty-two thousand dollars would go to me, and the remaining equity would be split in half. "At the current values," Daniel said, "that is roughly one hundred two thousand five hundred dollars to Helen, and fifty thousand five hundred to Derek."

"And the monthly payments?" Vanessa asked. It was the first thing she had said. Her voice was scraped thin. "The hundred and eighteen thousand. You'll want that too."

"No," I said. "I'm forgiving it. All seventy-two payments, as a gift. Daniel has the letter written."

Derek made a sound in his throat and put both hands over his face. Daniel slid the page across, and I signed it in front of the two of them.

"I'd also like Saturdays back with Piper, from ten to two," I said. "That's a request, not a term. I would not trade a child for a contract."

Vanessa lifted her chin. "You've enjoyed this. Haven't you? Standing up in front of my customers, being the saint."

I answered her as evenly as I knew how. "No, Vanessa. I took no pleasure in it. But I won't pretend I was only sometimes."

She said nothing. She signed nothing, because there was nothing for her to sign, but she listened to every word, and that was more than she had done in six years.

We all stood. Derek shook Daniel's hand with both of his own. I took my coat from the rack, and I was reaching for the door when Vanessa spoke behind me. She was standing at the end of the table with her fingers pressed on the wood.

"Why would you forgive it?" she said. "You could have taken everything."

I turned around and looked at her. "Because a child needs both of her houses to stay standing."
