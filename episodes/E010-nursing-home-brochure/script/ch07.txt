# Read Back

The conference room on the first floor of Silverleaf Terrace had a long oak-colored table, a window with the blinds half drawn, and a bowl of foil-wrapped mints in the exact center, like a centerpiece at a very small wedding. At the head of the table sat a mug that said Welcome Home. It was Thursday, October twenty-second, ten o'clock in the morning, and I had packed my one bag that morning and left it in the truck.

Around the table sat five people, counting me. Gideon Pratt had taken the head, in a crisp vest, with a folder and a smile. On his right sat my daughter in her good wool coat, with a pen in her hand. Beside her sat her husband, hunched, looking at the grain of the table as though it had a sad story to tell. On Gideon's left sat a tall woman with her glasses on a chain and her back very straight, who had been introduced to me as the director of nursing, and who looked at the clock twice before I sat down.

"Miss Peggy," Gideon said, "we are all just so pleased. Let's make this easy."

I took the chair across from Dana and put the green notebook on the table. I wrote the time in the margin, which made my daughter sigh.

Gideon opened his folder. "First, the assessment results. Our team has reviewed Miss Peggy's screening. Nineteen out of thirty, which puts her in the moderate range, and our clinical recommendation is secured memory care. This is a gift, because it means we've caught it early." He slid a page toward me. "Next, the residency agreement for Birch Court. Entrance fee, fourteen thousand dollars. Monthly, seven thousand nine hundred. And the power of attorney, which Dana has so kindly prepared, so that she can handle the details."

Dana uncapped the pen and held it out to me, handle first, the way you hand scissors to a child. "Mom, I know what's best here."

"Thank you, dear," I said. "Before I sign anything, may I read something back? It's a habit from work. It will take about four minutes."

Gideon glanced at the director of nursing, then at the clock. "Of course. Let's not make this about paperwork."

I opened the notebook.

"On Sunday, October eleventh, I arrived at nine fifty-five and signed in. At ten twelve, Mister Pratt began my screening in his office, and I wrote the time. He read me five words, and they were lantern, spoon, river, saddle, ash. I said them back to him. I drew a clock with the hands at ten past eleven. I named twenty-two animals in one minute. I made one mistake, and it was the date, which I was off by a day because I was looking at the fountain. At ten thirty-one the test was over. And at the start of the scoring, I wrote this line. Mister Pratt turned the form over before he scored it."

Nobody moved. Dana's pen was still in the air.

"The form in this folder says my recall was zero of five, and that I was unable to draw a clock, and that my total was nineteen. It carries a stamp of approval at ten twenty, which is eleven minutes before my test ended." I turned to the next page. "On Monday and Tuesday I timed the call lights in Birch Court, and they were answered in eleven, nineteen, and twenty-seven minutes. On Wednesday morning at two forty, Mister Abernathy fell in the hallway. The aide on duty was alone with twenty residents, and I called nine-one-one at two forty-four. The incident was written up as a slip in his room."

"This is absurd," Gideon said, with the smile still on. "Miss Peggy is clearly, understandably, confused."

"Let me read that back," I said, in my very nicest voice. "I am confused, and what happened is on the page, in my handwriting, with the time."

I turned to the woman with the glasses on a chain. "Ma'am, I have one question. I wonder if you'd tell me where you were on the morning of October eleventh."

She was a long moment answering. I think she had already guessed. "I was in Denver," she said stiffly. "At the state care conference. I drove back Monday night."

"So you did not see me on Sunday."

"I never saw you before today, Missus Okerlund."

"But your stamp says you approved my results at ten twenty on Sunday morning."

The director of nursing looked at the form for a while. She looked at Gideon. He had stopped smiling, and without it his face was only a face.

"My stamp," she said slowly, "is kept at the nurses' station."

My daughter turned her head and looked at Gideon Pratt. It was not a hard look. It was the look of a woman who has noticed that the floor is lower than she thought.

At ten twenty, by the clock on the wall, someone knocked, and the receptionist put her head in. "Mister Pratt? There are two people from the state in the lobby. They say they're here for an inspection. They have a laptop."

Behind her in the hallway I could see two people in dark coats and lanyards, a man and a woman, and over their shoulders a pair of round glasses and a canvas tote bag. Fern gave me the smallest nod. At the end of the hall, in the doorway, stood Imelda in her pink cat scrubs on her day off, with her rosary wound around one shaking hand.

"Let's not make this about paperwork," Gideon said, standing.

The woman surveyor stepped past him and set her laptop on the table beside the mints. "Actually, sir," she said, "it's going to be about paperwork."

I closed the notebook. I looked at my daughter across the table, and I kept my voice low, as I have always done when it matters. "Card ending four-four-one-seven," I said.

Dana went white from her collar up. "Mom, I can explain," she said.

Her husband had not spoken all morning. Now he put both of his big hands flat on the table, and he did not look at the table when he did it. He looked at her. "Dana," he said. "She's told you the number."

I stood up. I put the notebook in my cardigan pocket and left the pen on the table. Nobody stopped me.

In the hall, Imelda stepped aside to let me by, and as I passed the dining room, I saw Cyril Abernathy sitting at our table in his plaid blanket with his blue cast on the cloth. He raised his good hand and gave me a salute with a surveyor's pencil.

I walked out under the Welcome Home banner, past the dead heron, and across the lot to Hank's tan truck. I had my keys in my hand when I heard the automatic doors sigh behind me, and quick footsteps on the pavement.

"Mom," my daughter said.

I did not turn around. Not yet.
