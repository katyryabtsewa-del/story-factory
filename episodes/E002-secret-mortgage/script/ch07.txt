# Ninety Days

When Bruce Whitfield asked his daughter whether he had given her one dime for that house, forty people in a backyard went still, and the only sound was the bounce house, wheezing along as if nothing had happened.

"Daddy," Vanessa said, "I was going to explain."

"Where did my fifteen thousand dollars go?" Bruce asked.

"Into Haven and Hue, Daddy. The first inventory." Vanessa's voice had shrunk. "It's the same thing."

"It is not the same thing." He stared at her. "I wrote you a check for a wedding. Afterward you told me it went into this house, and I loved that story. I told it at the club for six years. I never asked to see a closing paper. I wanted to be the man who did it." His face had gone gray under the golf tan. "Just now I stood on this patio and took a bow for a house another woman paid for." He set the champagne flute on a table with great care, as if it were someone else's. Then he turned to me. "Missus Brandt, I owe you an apology, and I don't yet know how to make it."

"Thank you, Bruce," I said. "You can start by not blaming Piper."

He nodded once and took his wife by the elbow. She looked back at her daughter, and then at me, and she gave me a real look, the first of the year. "Get your purse, we're going," Bruce said, and the two of them went around the side of the house.

The party broke apart like a plate. People collected their children, and the photographer lowered her camera and left. A mother touched my arm on the way out and murmured, "Good for you." Vanessa went into the house and did not come back out.

Which left me with a cake.

I did not feel triumphant. I thought about a child who had been promised a birthday. Fifteen guests were still on the lawn, mostly small ones who had noticed nothing but the macarons, and Piper was climbing out of the bounce house with her braids sticking up.

"Nana, are we doing cake now?"

"We're doing cake right now," I said.

Derek came and stood beside me without a word. He lit the seven candles in the lemon glaze, and we gathered the children and sang, and my son's voice cracked on the second line and held on through the rest. Piper counted the candles twice, in case they had changed. She blew them out in two tries, and I put the knife in her hand, put mine over it, and we cut the first slice together.

"Nana," she said, very low, while the other children were reaching for plates. "Why is Mommy crying in the window?"

I looked up. A shape stood behind the upstairs curtain, and then it was gone. "Grown-ups find out things they didn't know, sweetheart, and it surprises them. It has nothing to do with you."

She thought about it. "And you didn't do something bad?"

"No, sweet girl," I said. "Nobody did."

"Okay," she said, and she licked her thumb, and ate her cake.

Derek came to my duplex at nine o'clock that night, still in his apron, and stood in my doorway turning his car keys over until I told him to sit.

"Her boutique closed in twenty sixteen," he said. "She filed for bankruptcy and made me swear never to tell anyone. A loan with her name on it would have been turned down at the door. So when I got laid off and the house came along, I told her the bank had taken care of everything. I was trying to protect her."

"You were trying to protect yourself from a hard conversation," I said, "and you were charging it to me."

He nodded and did not argue, and I let that count. "I told her in the car, Mom. The consulting, the deed, all of it. She said nothing until we reached the driveway. Then she asked, 'Who else knows?' Not why. Who else." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm on the basement sofa. She locked the bedroom door."

We met at Daniel Okafor's office on Tuesday, November third. On the first of the month my phone had gone ding for the seventy-second time. It would keep dinging until the closing, because I was on that loan until it was paid off, and Daniel had been plain that my credit was mine to protect. Derek sat across from me in a borrowed tie. Vanessa came in a gray suit, with no makeup and no phone, and took the chair farthest from mine.

Daniel laid it out plainly. The house would go on the market on November sixteenth, and by January fourteenth, when the ninety days ended, Derek must have a signed contract or a refinance. A refinance would cost about twenty-two hundred dollars a month, and the bank wants a housing payment under a third of his pay. "The costs of selling, the agent and the closing, come out of the total first, and you share them equally," Daniel said. "Then the first fifty-two thousand dollars goes to Helen, and the rest is split in half. Before those costs, at today's value, that is about one hundred ten thousand to Helen and fifty-seven thousand to Derek."

"And the monthly payments?" Vanessa asked. It was the first thing she had said. Her voice was scraped thin. "The monthly payments. You'll want that too."

"More than a hundred and eighteen thousand dollars so far," Daniel said, "and Helen will keep paying until the closing."

"I'm forgiving it," I said. "Everything I have paid and everything I will pay, as a gift. Daniel has the letter written."

"There is a form for a gift this size," Daniel said. "You will owe nothing."

Derek made a sound in his throat and put both hands over his face. I signed the page in front of the two of them.

"I'd also like Saturdays back with Piper, from ten to two," I said. "That's a request, not a term."

Vanessa lifted her chin. "You enjoyed this. Standing up in front of my customers, being the saint."

"No, Vanessa. I took no pleasure in it," I said evenly. "And I know Derek lied to you. About his pay and about the deed. You were told it was all handled, and you believed your husband, which is what a wife is meant to do. It does not excuse what you said in your kitchen. But you were lied to, and I won't say you weren't."

"I don't need your pity." Her chin stayed high, but her voice thinned. "I told my clients that number. I told my mother." She looked at Derek, and he looked at the table, and something in her face slipped for a breath, like a picture hung a little crooked.

We all stood. Derek shook Daniel's hand with both of his own. I was reaching for the door when Vanessa spoke behind me, her fingers pressed on the table.

"Why would you forgive it?" she said. "You could have taken everything."

I turned around and looked at her. "Because a child needs both of her houses to stay standing."

She did not answer, and I did not wait.
