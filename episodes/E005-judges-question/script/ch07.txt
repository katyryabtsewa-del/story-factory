# The Question

Judge Marsh had said she had one question, and the heater under the windows chose that moment to stop ticking.

"Missus Halvorsen, you telephoned the sheriff six times," she said. "Tell me exactly what you saw."

Brent's lawyer was on his feet before she had finished, saying that the respondent had not yet been called and that this was a departure from ordinary procedure. The judge did not raise her voice. She said, "Counsel, I may question anyone in this room, and I have chosen to. Sit down." He sat down.

My young lawyer did not move. He folded his hands on his folder, and I stood up, because I was taught to stand when spoken to by someone who deserved it. The judge had the clerk swear me in where I stood, and I said, "I do," and it was five minutes past eleven.

"Your Honor, I will give it to you as I used to give it to the transportation office," I said. "Six evenings, Monday, Wednesday and Friday, two weeks running. My calls went in at six forty-two, six fifty-one and six forty the first week, and at seven oh five, six forty-eight and six fifty-five the second."

"Go on," the judge said.

"On the first three evenings there were two men, and on the last three there were three. They carried a tripod with a small telescope, a mallet, and orange stakes with pink ribbon. They drove one white pickup truck with a ladder rack. I wrote down the plate every time, on the back of an egg carton lid. It began with the letters K, T, R, and it ended in seven four one."

I reached into the pocket of my barn jacket and took out a rubber band holding six flat pieces of cardboard, and I held them up. The bailiff carried them to the bench.

"You did not ask me this, Your Honor," I said, "but I will tell you anyway. There are forty-one stakes. I counted them twice, going up and coming down. And on the driver's door of that truck somebody had peeled off a sign, but a sign leaves its shadow, and in the beam of my flashlight I could read three letters. They were B, A, S."

Judge Marsh looked at the egg carton lids as though they were the most reasonable documents she had been handed all year. Then she said, "Is the records clerk of the sheriff's office present?"

My young lawyer rose and said that she was in the hall, and the judge said to bring her in. The woman in the green sweater came down the aisle with her folder in both hands, took the oath in a small clear voice, and sat. "Six entries, Your Honor," she said, "each logged as a report of suspicious persons, rural property, caller known to dispatch. Every time matches what Missus Halvorsen just said, to the minute. On the second call the dispatcher noted that the caller gave the plate before she was asked for it. There is also a registration return on the first call. The owner is a business, Basalt Line Surveying, with an address in Camas Junction. It was marked low priority, since no vehicle was present when the deputy arrived."

"B, A, S," the judge said, to no one in particular.

"Yes, ma'am," I said.

"Thank you, Missus Halvorsen. Please sit. Thank you, Madam Clerk." The judge turned her head. "Mister Halvorsen, you are still under oath. Would you return to the stand."

It was eleven twelve by my watch when my son walked past my table without looking down and sat in the witness chair, and for the first time all morning his hands did not know where to go.

"Mister Halvorsen," Judge Marsh said, "did you, or anyone acting for you, give those men permission to enter your mother's land?"

It was the plainest sentence I heard all day, and it was the whole case. If he said no, then strangers had walked my rows. If he said yes, then he had given away land that was not his. Either way my calls had been the truth, and I watched him realize that there was no door in the room that did not lead to me.

"Your Honor," Brent said, "I think it is more complicated than that. There are, ah, commercial relationships. It was really a timing matter."

"That is not an answer to my question," the judge said.

Brent's lawyer half rose. "Your Honor, I request a moment with my client."

"Denied," Judge Marsh said. "The question is simple. Mister Halvorsen, I will ask it once more." She said it gently, which was worse. "Did you or anyone acting for you give those men permission to enter your mother's land?"

He looked at the last row, and Tucker Rand became interested in his own knees. Then my son looked at me. I did not look away, and I was not trying to hurt him. I only wanted him to see that I was still there.

"Yes," Brent said.

"Yes, you gave permission," the judge said.

"Yes, Your Honor," Brent said. "Under an agreement with Copperleaf Homes. Basalt Line is their contractor."

"What were you paid?" the judge asked.

"An advance of sixty thousand dollars," Brent said, "against a finder's fee. And the company has the right to buy the farm for one million four hundred thousand dollars, once I delivered authority."

"Did you tell your mother about this agreement?" the judge asked.

"No," Brent said.

"Did she give you authority to speak for her land?"

"No," he said, very quietly, and he put his hand over his mouth.

The judge turned to my young lawyer, who handed up a single page from his folder, and she read it with her glasses on the end of her nose. "This is the postmaster's statement. Mister Halvorsen, seven weeks ago, did you file a change of address in your mother's name, sending her mail to a box in Camas Junction?"

"Yes," Brent said. "I only meant to." The judge raised a finger, and he stopped.

"So the tax notices went to you," she said, and then, in the same flat dry voice she had used all morning, "Then your mother has been telling the truth about her own back acres since late February."

Pearl, I learned later, had unwrapped a peppermint and forgotten to put it in her mouth.

"The stove was a single afternoon, the tax installment was missed because the petitioner diverted the mail, and a woman who walks her own orchard at dusk, as she has done for forty years, is not wandering," the judge said. "The court finds no evidence, much less clear and convincing evidence, that Vera Halvorsen is unable to manage her affairs. The petition is denied. The clerk will forward a copy of this transcript to the county's civil authorities, as appropriate. Missus Halvorsen, you are free to go home."

The gavel came down. It made a small flat sound, like a pear dropped on a floorboard. In the back row, Tucker Rand was already on his feet, buttoning his jacket and going quietly toward the door, like a man leaving a restaurant when the check is coming.

Brent stepped down from the witness stand and stood in the aisle with his arms hanging, looking at me. I looked at the door. I had begun, without meaning to, to count the steps to it, eleven, the way I always did. My young lawyer put a hand out toward my elbow, and I told him thank you, but no. I put both palms on the table, and I stood up on my own.
