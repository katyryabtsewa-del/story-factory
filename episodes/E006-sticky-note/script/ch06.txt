# Rehearsal

By eight o'clock on Thursday night, an hour after Curtis's taillights had left Hollis Street, my kitchen table held four people, a pot of coffee, and the rough outline of a plan.

Fern had come across the side yard in her barn coat. Detective Hatch had parked his plain gray car in her driveway instead of mine, which Fern said was the most sensible thing a man had done in her presence in thirty years. Marisol came last, through the back gate, with the hood of her parka up, and she sat on the edge of her chair as though it might be taken away. I gave her coffee and a slice of Fern's pound cake, and she held the cake without eating it.

Detective Hatch laid out the morning like a man setting a table. Marisol would be at the front desk when we arrived. At a quarter to ten, she would let him in through the back and into the file room, which sat behind the conference room with only a thin wall and a louvered door between them. He would stand there with a thermos and a notepad, and he would not come out until the right moment.

"And the right moment is?" Fern asked.

"When somebody says something I can use," he said. "Missus Ledbetter will help them along."

He turned to me. "Your telephone has a recording program on it. Your daughter set it up last year, for your voicemails."

"I have never once used it."

"You'll use it tomorrow. Start it before you walk in and put the phone in your cardigan pocket. In general terms, ma'am, in Tennessee you may record a conversation you are part of, and nobody else in the room has to be told. So you must be in it, every word. Tomorrow you are not an observer. You are the guest of honor."

"What do I say?"

"What a confused woman would say." His eyes crinkled. "Ask them to remind you what the first paper was, and when you signed it, and what you wore, and what the weather was. People love to fill a silence for a person who seems lost. And don't finish a sentence for them."

"I have spent forty years not finishing sentences for brides," I said. "I can do that."

Then I went to the kitchen drawer, the one where every house keeps its rubber bands and dead batteries and the key to nothing, and took out a packet of papers folded in thirds. "I should show you what I have," I said. "I haven't looked at it since November."

It was the discharge packet from the Riverbend Eye Surgery Center in Cleveland, Tennessee. I smoothed it on the table under the lamp. At the top it said Josephine A. Ledbetter, and below it, in printed type, a list of times. Check-in, eleven o'clock. Procedure, one-thirty. Discharged, three-ten.

Marisol put down her cake. "Missus Ledbetter. Mister Brandt's paper says you signed at two-fifteen."

"Tuesday, the eighteenth of November," I said. "I was in a recovery chair, with a plastic shield on my face, drinking apple juice out of a paper cup."

Nobody said a word. Then Fern gave a short bark of a laugh, and she took out her telephone and scrolled and held it up. It was a photograph she had taken in the parking lot at Riverbend, and the time in the corner said three-twelve. In it stood a seventy-one-year-old woman in a clear plastic eye shield, taped at the edges, and a pair of enormous wraparound sunglasses that the nurse had handed her. She looked like someone who had lost a duel. Under the picture was the text Fern had sent me that afternoon, one word long. Pirate.

I laughed until I had to take off my glasses. It was the first honest laughter I had had in two days, and it brought Marisol's with it, and then even the detective's long shoulders began to shake. Fern said she had been saving that picture for my funeral.

When I could speak, I said, "He didn't even look. That's what strikes me. Curtis knew I was having my eye done sometime in the fall. Nicole told him. But he never asked which Tuesday. He picked the day Mister Teague's calendar was empty, and he never once thought to wonder whether mine was."

"Men like that don't ask what day you were busy," Fern said. "They only check whether there's room."

Detective Hatch slid the packet and the photograph into a clear envelope and sealed it. "Keep them in your purse until the right moment, Missus Ledbetter. If you wave them early, he tells a different story, and we lose the one he's already told. Let him finish his version first. Then you give him yours."

"I'd like Nicole to see them," I said. "Not hear it from me. See it, in black and white."

"I'd like that too," said the detective, and for a moment he looked tired. "That is as kind as I can make it, ma'am."

They left at ten. Marisol went last, and at the door she squeezed my hand and said nothing.

At ten minutes after midnight, my telephone lit up in the dark on my nightstand. It was her. Mister Teague knows someone talked, the message said. He asked me this afternoon who made copies. I told him the machine does it by itself. He looked at me a long time. Is it still on?

I put on my glasses, and I typed with one finger. It's still on. Bring your receipt.

I slept for four hours, which was more than I expected. At six I dressed with great care. I wore my navy skirt and the gray cardigan with the deep pockets. In the left pocket I put Earl's button, a brass lineman's button from his work coat, the one I had carried for three years. In the right pocket I put the telephone. I opened the recording program and pressed the red circle at ten minutes past nine, and then I sat at the kitchen table with my reading glasses on my nose and waited.

Curtis came up the porch steps at a quarter past nine, in a good wool coat, with a paper cup of coffee in each hand. He had on his best smile, the one that comes with a handshake. He gave me the coffee, and he looked me over as though I were a car he was about to sell.

"Morning, Josie, hon. You look rested. Ready to get ahead of it?"

"I think I'm ready," I said. "I only hope I remember why."
