# On the Record

I did not storm anywhere after the bank, which Winnie felt was a waste of a good pair of boots, and instead I spent the next eight days building a room.

In the courtroom I had watched what happens to people in rooms where every word is taken down. A man who will lie in a parking lot will think twice about lying to a woman with a machine. I had a frightened stepdaughter and a lender who did not know my name, and I meant to put them in the one room where I knew every rule.

On Wednesday night I wrote the plan on a legal pad in four lines. Get the house back. Get Dale Voss to say it out loud. Give Tessa the letter. Decide what comes after.

I started with the house. On Thursday I walked down Canal Street to Kessler Lock and Key, where the bell over the door still rang a flat note and the cat was asleep on the safe, and when the last customer left I told Gus what I wanted. I wanted Ray's lock back in my front door, and I wanted it done in the dark, so that a certain person would not know until she tried her key.

Gus went red to the ears. Then he took a brass cylinder wrapped in a shop rag from a drawer, with a key tag on it that said one word in Ray's hand. Birchwood.

"He keyed a spare to your key years ago, when the old one started to stick," Gus said. "He meant to put it in when it gave out, and then it never did. It'll fit. I can have it in before sunrise."

"Next Thursday," I said. "At five thirty in the morning, in the dark."

Gus nodded.

By Monday he had also cut my steno machine a new leg strap out of harness leather, with two brass rivets.

The second thing was Cecilia. I went back to Water Street on Friday with the photocopies, and she studied them for twenty minutes with a magnifying glass. Then she typed one page on bank letterhead and signed it, stating that the capital R on the Larkmoor paper did not match the bank's own card for Ray, and that the card was in black ink and the loan paper in blue.

"I am a branch manager with a magnifying glass, not an expert," she said. "But I also walked over to the county recorder on my lunch hour. No mortgage has been recorded against eighteen Birchwood Lane since yours was paid off eleven years ago. Larkmoor funded and never filed anything, and a lien that is never filed is only a piece of paper."

She would not come on Thursday. "People talk more freely when the bank is not at the table," she said. "I will be at my desk with my phone."

On Tuesday afternoon I was on a stool at the shop with my legal pad when the bell rang its flat note and Tessa walked in, in her Foxglove jacket, looking exhausted. She stopped when she saw me.

"I only sit," I said. "It is a public shop."

"It's my shop." She turned to Gus, who had put down a key blank as if it had bitten him. "I need the ledgers, and the combination to the safe."

"Ray never gave me the combination," Gus said, which was true, and the cat on the safe opened one eye and shut it again.

Tessa picked up a blank from the counter out of habit and ran her thumb along the teeth, and I saw her father's hands in hers. Then she saw me watching and put it down hard.

"You're taking notes."

"It is a habit."

"I'm his daughter. I'm not doing anything wrong."

"No one has said otherwise in my hearing," I said.

She looked up at the round white clock over the key wall, which Ray had hung crooked and never straightened. It said ten minutes to four. "I have thirty-eight days," she said, and then she stopped, as if she had heard herself. "On Thursday at noon the buyer's man is coming to walk through the house. I need you to stay away from it, Loretta. Please. Just until then."

It was the closest she had come to asking me for anything in nineteen years. "I will keep it in mind," I said. She left without the ledgers, and the clock went on ticking over the wall. Thirty-eight days, and a man at noon on Thursday.

That evening I made two calls from Winnie's kitchen. The first was to Dale Voss, on the number on his heavy cream card. He answered on the second ring in a soft, warm voice that had never once been in a hurry, and said he would be delighted to discuss the sale. He said a timing issue twice before I finished my first sentence, and I wrote it down both times. I said that Thursday at ten at the house would suit me, two hours before his walk-through. He said it suited him perfectly.

The second was a text to Tessa. I wrote, Thursday at ten, at the house, to discuss the sale. Dale Voss will be there. An hour later she wrote back, How do you know Dale? And an hour after that, in a different tone, Fine. I did not answer either one, because I wanted her to wonder what I knew.

Winnie wanted a job, so I gave her the extension cord. She asked whether she should bake something, and I said that no one in the history of the law has been persuaded by a coffee cake. She came with the cord and four separate opinions.

The nights were the hard part. I lay in her spare room wondering whether I might open my mouth in my own kitchen, in front of the girl who had called me Dad's wife for nineteen years, and find that no words came. All my working life I had written down what other people said, and I had never once said anything of my own into the record. At two in the morning on the last night, I stood in Winnie's bathroom and said to the mirror, quite quietly, "Let the record show." It sounded small. I said it again, and it sounded like me.

At five thirty on Thursday morning, Gus knelt on my front porch with a flashlight in his teeth, and Winnie stood at the bottom of the steps in her flowered boots, holding the orange cord like a bouquet. It took Gus nine minutes. The cheap silver deadbolt came out of my door in four turns, and Gus dropped it in a paper bag with great contempt. Then he slid Ray's old brass cylinder home, and it went in as if it had never left.

I took my own key, the one with the small L stamped on its head, and turned it. The bolt slid back with a long, round, comfortable click, the sound of a good door clearing its throat.

I stood in my own kitchen for the first time in twelve days. Every drawer in Ray's desk in the hallway had been pulled out and pushed back crooked. She had been looking. I set the machine on the table, plugged in Winnie's cord, and arranged my paper in the tray, and Gus and Winnie slipped out the back and took their cars away. At nine forty I sat down, folded my hands, and waited.

At nine fifty-eight, headlights swept slowly across the kitchen ceiling.
