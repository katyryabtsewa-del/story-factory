# Gaudet Blue Water

If you have stayed with me this far, you watched me tell my husband to bring a pen, and you may be wondering whether the sky fell. It did not. It came down the way the water in a pool goes down in October, a little each morning, until one day you look and the line is somewhere you did not expect.

The gentlemen from Houston called Tuesday afternoon to say the deal was paused. On Thursday, the second of October, they wrote to say it was over, which meant that the Friday closing, and the one point four million dollars, and the title of Founder and Chairman all went out with the tide. Tripp Haverty sent me a short note saying he had learned something about reading everything, and he was sorry for his part. I wrote back and wished him well, and I meant it.

Dale filed an emergency application with the board that same week. I heard about it from Wanda Pickett, who called me at home to say that she had told him, kindly, exactly what the laminated card says. He could finish the jobs he had for sixty days, and he could not sign a new contract or pull a new permit, and he asked her if there was a different card. "There isn't one, sugar," Wanda told me. "I even looked."

Ray's crews finished all fourteen open jobs on time. I stayed through that Friday to hand over the files and clean out the supply room, and I took not one folder that was not mine. Then, one by one, the people who made that company run went somewhere else. Of the eleven on the payroll, nine had left by Thanksgiving, with Ray the first. One took a job in Pensacola with her cousin's husband. The other seven came to work for Ray and me.

I did not see what happened on November eighteenth, but I was told. Wanda helped proctor that sitting in Montgomery. Dale walked into the testing room in his best golf shirt, sat down at the computer, and read every question aloud, the way he reads sales reports, until a proctor asked him to stop. He failed by four points. Wanda said that afterward, in the parking lot, he stood next to his white truck with his face on the door, and called out, to no one, "Mimi?" Just once. Then he got in the truck.

My daughter did fly in, that December, and she brought a hat.

Skye left Dale in January. By then she had learned what the company really was, which was not a rich founder's empire but a cash-flow problem with a nice smile, and she had ordered a new vault door for the quiet room at Still Water Studio that Dale was supposed to pay for. There was no one left to pay for it. She told him, with great tenderness, that the energy around him was heavy, and she moved to the other side of town. I do not hold it against her. She never lied to him. She simply took him at his word, which is something I understand.

In December, Dale sold the trucks and the customer list to a competitor in Mobile for three hundred and ten thousand dollars. It was not one point four million. It paid off the company's line of credit and left him a small apartment near the interstate with a view of a dumpster. Priya Nair and I settled the divorce over cinnamon rolls. The house, paid off in nineteen ninety-six, stayed with me, and so did my credit union account and my retirement account. What was left, we split by plain arithmetic, and I did not ask for one thing spectacular. I did not want his shirt. I wanted him to find out whose name was on the wall.

He came to my porch in January, on a cold bright afternoon, in a jacket that looked a size too large. I was shelling pecans. He took the other rocker without being asked and sat a while with his hands between his knees. He looked older, the way a house does when somebody has finally taken down the awning.

"Mimi," he said, "I'd like to ask you something, and I hope you'll hear it fairly."

"I'm listening, Dale."

"If you'd come back as the qualifier, just until I get my feet under me, I'd give you forty percent. For old times' sake. Forty years is a long time."

I put down the nutcracker. I thought about it, I truly did, because I owed him that.

"No, Dale," I said. "I'm sorry. But no."

He nodded slowly, and he did not argue, which was the most honest thing he had done in a decade. He stood up, put his hand on the porch rail, and said, "Bless your heart, Mimi," and I do not think he knew where he had heard it before. Then he walked to his car.

The new company is called Gaudet Blue Water Service, which was my name before it was his. Ray insisted on a pelican for the truck door, not a dolphin, because he said that dolphin had been smiling at people long enough. Our very first customer was Odessa Whitfield. She wrote the check standing in her kitchen in a bathrobe and said, "That boy would be ashamed, if he had it in him." The first week we had sixty-one weekly service stops. By this April, one hundred and four customers. Two trucks, doing the work five used to. Ray is my partner at twenty percent, and I am at a desk again, with a box fan, in a small office by the marina, except that the sign on the door says my name.

On the wall of my back porch hangs the certificate Ray carried out of that conference room, and beside it a newer one with the same name and a different company. I look at the two of them in the mornings while the coffee cools.

It is April now, as I tell you this, and I am on that porch with a glass of iced tea sweating in my hand. A white truck with a pelican on the door has just turned into my drive, and Ray is climbing out with a bag of boiled peanuts and a customer list.

Dale used to say that I had never once read the fine print, and the truth is that he was right for forty years. Somebody finally read it, and it turned out to be me.

So I would like to ask you something, since you have been kind enough to sit with me. If you had been in my chair, across that conference table, with the pen in your hand and the man you gave forty years to telling you to sign like always, would you have signed, or would you have told him? And I'd love to know where you are listening from, and what the weather is like there, because it is a beautiful evening here on Mobile Bay, and I am glad of the company.
