# The Needle

The Friday that came apart in that conference room ended, for me, at my own kitchen table, with a cup of tea going cold and Fern Albright eating all of the pound cake.

I am telling you this from April, with the window open and the dogwoods out along Hollis Street, so I will give you the plain facts first and the harder part after. Curtis Brandt was charged within the week with forgery and with attempted theft by deception. He is out on bond, and his lawyer and the prosecutor are talking about a plea. My own telephone, the one in my pocket, had heard every word, and Detective Hatch has the recording. Gordon Teague lost his notary commission before the end of January. He went to Detective Hatch's office the following Monday with a shoebox of papers and a long, ashamed story. The loan was canceled before a single dollar moved, and my house is as paid for as it was the day Earl and I went out for pie. The man at Marlow Bridge Capital is Curtis's problem. I did not feel the smallest wish to make him mine.

I got my car keys back from Detective Hatch in a clear plastic bag, with a note stapled to it that said, in tidy block letters, Ice has melted. I threw away the pill organizer, took the gadget off my thermostat with a butter knife, and had my mail sent back to Hollis Street. My glasses live on their cord now. When I put them in the refrigerator these days, it is by honest accident.

Nicole did not go home that Friday. She stood at the window for a long while after they had taken Curtis out, and then she turned around and asked me, in a voice I will not forget, whether she could come to my house. I said that she could. She slept in the spare room that night, and for ten weeks after, under the quilt Earl's mother made, and for four days she could not look me in the eye. She kept her gaze on the toast. She washed every dish in the house, some of them twice. When I said good morning, she answered the sink.

I let her. The waiting cost me more than anything else that month, because all it asks of you is that you stay out of her way.

On the fifth morning, I was sitting at the sunroom table, hemming, and she came and stood in the doorway in her robe.

"Mama," she said. "I put my name on it. I never read it. I was so busy being the good one. And you knew, and you sat there Thursday and let me cry, and you didn't say anything."

"I did," I said. "I'd do it again, because I could not lose that morning. But I am sorry it hurt you. I am sorry about the glasses in the refrigerator, too."

She nodded. She was crying, but quietly now. "I don't know how to make it right."

"You can't," I said. "Not all at once. I have not forgiven all of it, hon, and I won't pretend. But you can come to the table. That much I can offer. We'll see how the rest goes."

She sat down across from me and picked up a corner of the hem I was sewing, as if to be useful. We did not say anything more. It was enough for a Wednesday.

She filed her papers in February, and at the end of March she moved into a small apartment above the hardware store. Curtis wrote her a long letter, which she burned in my kitchen sink. I did not ask what it said.

Marisol is working for the county clerk now. Detective Hatch put in a word. It pays better, and nobody there stamps anything they have not seen. She and her husband put down a deposit on a little yellow house on Pruitt Lane with a porch that sags at one corner, and I have been promised a rocking chair on it. Fern is making a quilt, a wedding-ring pattern in blue and gray, and she says it is for nobody, but she measured my bed with a yardstick when she thought I was out of the room.

And on the first Tuesday of April, I opened a mending table in the fellowship hall of Sparrow Gap First Methodist. It was Fern's idea and the pastor's blessing, and I brought two sewing machines, a box of buttons, and a tin of lemon cookies. Eleven people came. A boy needed his school jacket patched, and a woman of eighty brought a coat with a torn lining and said she was sure I'd charge her a fortune. I charged her a cookie. I mean to be there every Tuesday until I cannot hold a needle.

The sticky note lives in my sewing box now, in the tray with the thimbles and Earl's brass button, flat and yellow and a little curled at the corners. I take it out sometimes and look at the handwriting of a frightened girl who picked up a pen anyway.

One Saturday in April, the doorbell rang at ten in the morning. Nicole stood on my porch in the thin spring light, holding her good winter coat in her arms. It was the charcoal wool one she had worn to her father's funeral, and the sleeve was torn open at the seam.

"I caught it on a shopping cart," she said. "I know you've closed the shop. I don't know if you still take jobs."

I looked at the coat, and then at her. Her face was bare and tired, and for a moment it was six years old.

"I take a few," I said.

I wrote her a receipt, one coat, sleeve seam, no charge, and signed it. Nicole looked at the little mark under my name. "What is that?" she asked. "I never noticed it."

"A knot," I said. "So the thread won't pull out."

I sat her at the table under the lamp and found a number-eight needle and a length of charcoal thread. I knotted the end of it, and I put the needle in her hand and closed her fingers around it.

"Hold the thread like this," I said.

I will give you what I learned this winter in one sentence, because I do not care for sermons. Whenever someone hands you a pen and you are not sure, you are allowed to say you need your glasses.

Now I would like to ask you something. If you had been me, would you have told Nicole earlier, or held your tongue for two days as I did? And have you ever been handed a pen when what you needed was time? Tell me in the comments, and tell me where you are listening from, because I like to know whose kitchen I am sitting in.
