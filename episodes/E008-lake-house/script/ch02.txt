# The Quiet

On Wednesday afternoon my son and his whole family were forty minutes away in a heated pool, and I was alone at the lake house with a twenty-two-pound turkey and three beds I had made for nobody. I had been told I would love the quiet. I was finding out what a quiet house sounds like when only one person is in it.

The drive up took me the better part of an hour, through the pines and past the hand-lettered sign for the blueberry stand, which shut for the season in September and still hasn't been taken down. The house sat at the end of the gravel the way it always has, cedar gone silver on the sides, green trim Frank repainted every four years whether it needed it or not. I put the turkey in the refrigerator on the bottom shelf, between a jug of cider and a jar of horseradish Frank put up in the last summer he felt like himself. I'm not a woman who throws out a jar like that.

Then I made the beds. I have made them every Wednesday for thirty-eight years, and I was not going to let a change of plans leave a bed unmade. I put the good sheets on the big front bedroom, the one with the window over the cove, which had been mine and Frank's and was now just mine. I put the flannel set on the small room where Craig slept as a boy, the one with the window seat that still has a dent in the cushion from his elbow. Last, I made up the room at the top of the stairs, where Tyler and Brooke sleep when they come. Craig stuck glow-in-the-dark stars on that ceiling the summer after his divorce, so the children would have something to look at besides the ceiling. Most of them had given up, but eleven still glowed when I turned off the light, which was eleven more than I expected.

I tucked the corners the way the school district taught us, forty-five degrees and no wrinkles, a thing nobody in my family has ever respected. Then I stood in the doorway and felt a little foolish. Nothing in that room was going to do anything until dark.

By four o'clock the sun had dropped behind the ridge and the cove had turned the color of an old spoon. I lit the wood stove. The first match caught the newspaper I had folded under the kindling, and in a minute the stove began to tick and warm, and the room filled with the smell of birch and that particular dust that only burns in an old house. I sat down in Frank's chair, which is the only chair in the front room that a grown man can't sink into and disappear, and I let myself be warm for a while.

My phone buzzed on the arm of the chair. It was the family group chat, which Brooke named "Ellery Family Feast" two Thanksgivings ago and which nobody has been allowed to rename since.

The first picture was the pool at Pine Ledge Lodge. It had a glass wall and tall pines beyond it, and steam rose off the water in a way that made the whole room look like a bath in a magazine. The second was Dana in a white robe, holding a tall glass of something orange and smiling the wide, glossy smile of a woman who has been photographed professionally. The third was a plate of shrimp arranged in a circle around a bowl of red sauce, with Brooke's thumb at the edge of the frame. Tyler sent a single word, "Sick," and nothing else, because Tyler has never sent a second word when a first one will do.

Then Craig wrote, "Mom! Miss you. Pool is awesome, you'd love it."

I looked at that for a while. I have been a nurse for a long time, and I know a sentence that is meant to close a door. "I'm sure I would," I typed. I deleted it. I typed, "Glad you're warm," and sent that instead, and I put the phone face down on the arm of the chair.

Around five, I made a cup of tea and carried it out to the screened porch. The light had gone pale over the water. Across the cove, the window in Amos Thibodeau's kitchen was already yellow. Amos is seventy-four years old, and he wears a plaid hat with earflaps in every season, including July, which I have never been able to explain to anybody. He was out on his own dock in a red barn coat, with a bucket at his feet. He saw me, lifted one hand off the bucket, and held it up. That is how men in Maine say hello across the water: one hand, no wave. I lifted mine back. Then he went inside, and that was the most conversation I had all afternoon.

That night I ate soup standing at the counter, because I had forgotten how to sit down for supper alone. I watched the weather on a television that only gets two stations. The forecast promised snow for Friday. While the kettle worked up to a boil, I checked my own pulse out of habit, two fingers on the inside of my wrist, counting against the second hand of the clock over the sink. Sixty-four. Fine. The house made its noises. The stove ticked down. Somewhere out on the ice, a fox barked twice, and I caught myself listening for the sound of a car door, which told me exactly how much I'd been looking forward to the quiet.

I slept in the big front bed. It was wider than it had any right to be. Frank's side had been empty for six years, and I had gotten used to it, and I refused to get used to it again in one night.

I woke at six to gray light coming up over the cove. I made coffee in the percolator, which has never once in its life made good coffee and which I will never throw away. I sat at the kitchen table with my phone and watched it fill up with nothing. Nobody had texted me since Tuesday, when Brooke sent a picture of a shelter puppy two towns over, with the words "Grandma I want this so bad." I wrote back that I would think about it, which is what I say when I have already decided no.

At a quarter to eight the phone lit up on the table. It was Craig.

Happy Thanksgiving! We'll swing by Friday to talk about the house.
