# Dinner for One and a Half

On Thursday morning my son texted that he would swing by on Friday to talk about the house, and somewhere between the coffee and the cutting board I decided I was going to cook the whole Thanksgiving dinner anyway. Nobody had asked me to. That was rather the point.

I started at seven. The turkey had been in a brine since Wednesday night, a bucket of salt water, brown sugar, a handful of peppercorns and two bay leaves, sitting on the back porch where the temperature stays just above freezing in November. Frank did it that way every year, and so did I after he died, mostly because the bucket was already there. I rinsed the bird, patted it dry with a dish towel I will never see again, and got it into the oven before the stove in the kitchen had finished warming up.

Then came the rest of it. I made stuffing with onions and celery cooked down in butter and a lot of sage, because my mother-in-law believed sage was a moral requirement. I mashed potatoes with the skins left on, the way Frank liked them. I slid a can of cranberry sauce onto a saucer with the ridges still in it, because that is the only way it has ever been served in my family. The green bean casserole came together from a can of soup, a can of fried onions, and a stubborn sense of tradition.

The pies were from the Harrow market, in their plastic domes, on the sideboard. Pies are Frank's department, and Frank is gone, so we buy them. I'd like to say I was above that, but I can't.

When the turkey came out of the oven at a quarter past twelve, the whole house smelled like a place where people were expected. Then I got out the good plates, the blue ones with the chipped rim that I've been meaning to replace for twenty years, and I set the table in the kitchen for four.

Then for three. Then for two. Then I took the forks back out of the cupboard drawer and set it for one, and that took about as long as the stuffing did, because it is harder than it sounds. I was not cooking for anybody else. I was cooking because I'd cooked Thanksgiving dinner for thirty-eight years, and a heated pool was not going to undo my record.

At one thirty I fixed a plate for Amos. Turkey, dark meat, because he likes it and has never once admitted that, plus stuffing, a scoop of potatoes, two spoons of cranberry, green beans, and a roll. I wrapped the plate in foil, put on my navy fleece, and carried it up the shore path between our two places. The path runs a quarter mile through birches and an old stone wall that Frank rebuilt the year Brooke was born.

Amos's house is a small white cape with a green door and a swing set in the side yard. Two bicycles lay tipped over in the grass with their wheels still turning in the wind. His granddaughter Jenna and her husband had moved in with him in September, after the rent went up in town, and they had taken the two little ones to Jenna's father's place down the coast for the afternoon. The house was quiet in the way of a place that will be loud again by suppertime.

Amos answered the door in his stocking feet with the plaid hat in one hand. He took the plate from me without a word, looked at it, and then looked at me.

"Frank would've made you a pie," he said.

"Frank could not roll out a crust to save his life, Amos."

"No," Amos said. "Mine's worse." He stepped back to let me in. "Sit down, Dot. You look like you walked up here on purpose."

I had. I sat at his kitchen table, which has a vinyl cloth printed with little red lobsters, and watched him take the foil off the plate with the care of a man opening a letter. He ate a forkful of turkey, slowly, and nodded to himself.

"Frank and I had that cove between us thirty-some years," he said. "He used to say it was one piece of water with two deeds."

"He said a lot of things about water."

"He did." Amos ate another bite of stuffing. "When you two bought the north lot, he came over here with a paper. Wrote it out by hand on the back of a boat invoice. He said if you ever sold, I got first look. First refusal, he called it. I said, Frank, she'll never sell, she's too stubborn. He laughed and said, that's what I'm counting on."

I put my fork down. "I never saw a paper."

"He told you. You were busy with the stove." Amos didn't smile, but something moved at the corner of his mouth. "He put it in the deed too, I think. He liked a clause."

I did not know what to say to that, so I didn't say anything. I have spent thirty-three years in rooms with parents who were about to hear bad news, and the first skill you learn is to let the quiet work for you.

After a while Amos said, "Your boy came by here Tuesday."

"Craig?"

"Stood out by the road with his phone for a good while, taking pictures of your house. Looked at the dock, looked at the porch." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on the lobster cloth. "I walked down and asked him what he was looking for. He said, 'Just the view, Amos.' Then he said it was going to be great for the family."

Something cold went down the back of my neck and stayed there. "Did he say what would be great?"

"No," Amos said. "I didn't ask him twice. I never ask anybody anything twice."

He pushed the plate an inch toward me. On it was a second roll, and a spoonful of stuffing he had clearly saved from his own portion. "You eat too," he said.

So I did. I sat in Amos Thibodeau's kitchen on Thanksgiving afternoon, and I ate a turkey dinner off his blue plates with a fork that didn't match the spoon, and it was the first thing I'd eaten all day that tasted like it had been made for somebody.

I walked home in the last of the light and turned on the lamp over the sink.

Craig's old tablet was on the counter, propped against the fruit bowl where he'd left it last summer. I had assumed it was dead. Then I saw the charging cord running from it to the outlet behind the bread box. As I reached for the light switch, the screen woke up, a small bell chimed, and a white box slid down from the top of the glass.
