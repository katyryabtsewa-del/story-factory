# Sixth Floor

It was a quarter past one in the morning, the feed pump was dead, and the people on the sixth floor of the Ostrander were banging on the pipes to tell me they were still alive.

I stood in the boiler room and made my first decision of the night. A boiler without a pump needs a new seal, and a seal takes about an hour, and in that hour the building would go on cooling. I could not do both jobs at once, and I could not do either one alone, so I split the three of us into two. Dmitri would rebuild the pump if he could find the parts. Isaiah and I would go up and find out how bad it was.

"There might be a seal kit," Dmitri said slowly. "I ordered one in the spring. It came in a box, and then they cancelled the contract, and I was told not to open anything we didn't need." He went to a steel shelf behind the water treatment drums and came back with a toolbox that had dust on its lid thick enough to write in. Inside, in plastic and still taped shut, was exactly the seal I needed. He looked at it the way a man looks at a lottery ticket.

"Open it," I said. "Say what you're doing."

"Opening the seal kit," Dmitri said, and I nearly laughed.

A young man in a sweatshirt had followed the knocking down the stairs. He was about thirty-four, from five-C, with a voice that sounded as though it belonged on the radio, and he stood in the doorway asking what he could do. I handed him a flashlight and put him behind Dmitri's shoulder. "Hold it like it matters," I said. "The light goes where the hands are going, not where your eyes are." He aimed it at the pump, then at Dmitri's hands, and his whole face changed, the way a person's does when he is finally trusted with something.

I will tell you where that voice came from. In my first year at Saint Brendan's, in the second-shift boiler room, the chief told me to go and find the coffee. I found it. I also found a leak in a condensate line that the day crew had walked past for a week, and I put it on his desk with a note, and he did not send me for coffee again. It was not a speech. It was the next right thing.

On my way out I stopped at the riser board and closed one valve, the one for the empty storefront on the street side. For two winters it had been heating a room nobody rented. Dmitri stared at it, and I said, "Every pound of steam we're not giving away is a pound for the north wing." That was the only part of the plan that cost nothing.

Then Isaiah and I took the stairs.

On three, a door stood open, and in the doorway, in a long wool coat buttoned to the chin with a cane planted in front of her, stood Esther Bloom. She was eighty-four, small and upright, with white hair cut like a helmet and the eyes of a woman who had caught thirty years of teenagers lying. "Missus Rudd," she said. "Isaiah told the desk phone you were coming, and the desk phone told the whole building. I didn't think it was true."

"Neither did I," I said. "How are you holding up?"

"I'm warmer than most. I don't want anything. I want information." She held up a thermometer, the old glass laboratory kind. "It was fifty-one in my kitchen at midnight and forty-six at one. In the north stairwell it was thirty-eight a half hour ago. Is the standpipe on six drained?"

I looked at her for a moment, because it was exactly the question I had come upstairs to ask. "Not yet," I said.

"Then it should be first," said Esther. "Water does not care about anyone's opinion at thirty-two degrees. I'd rather it burst on a Monday than on someone's head."

"Isaiah," I said, "write that down. She's right. That pipe is the first thing."

By two o'clock we had the building moving. Isaiah lit the gas fireplace in the lobby with a long match, and it caught with a soft blue whoosh that drew everyone's head up like a lamp in a window. We unlocked the Gallery and dragged the two couches to face the fire, and Isaiah ran an extension cord from the kitchenette to a handful of space heaters that neighbors had carried down from their closets, on separate circuits, so that nothing would trip. I stood in the middle of the room and counted the people who most needed it, the oldest and the sickest, and the number was forty.

The man in six-F was on oxygen, and he came down in the elevator in a borrowed wheelchair, with his portable tank on his lap and his wife beside him with a blanket. Esther, who did not need to come, came anyway, and sat beside him with her cane across her knees.

Then I went to six with Isaiah and three men in pajama bottoms and parkas, to drain the standpipe. Up there the cold sat in the stairwell like water in a bucket. We opened the hose valve at the bottom of the pipe, and the water came out in a rope, and the neighbors carried it away in buckets and dumped it down the bathtubs of anyone who would let them in. A retired man from six-A and a young woman from six-D, who had lived across the hall from each other for six years without once exchanging names, stood side by side with a bucket between them. They introduced themselves over the sound of the water, and neither of them thought it was strange.

At twenty to four I held Esther's thermometer to the north stairwell wall and read it by Isaiah's flashlight. Thirty-one degrees. The pump seal was in by then, and Dmitri called up on Isaiah's radio from the basement, hoarse and proud, that Boiler One was running.

I went down the back stairs, because I needed to see the glass with my own eyes. At the bottom, in the dim light by the bicycle cage, stood Esther Bloom, who had come down in the service elevator on a cane at four in the morning.

She held out a manila folder. "You'll want this," she said. "The minutes of the meeting are inside, with my notes. The report itself is in that young man's desk drawer. He's been told to keep it there."
