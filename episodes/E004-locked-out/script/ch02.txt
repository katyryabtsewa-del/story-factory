# Dad's Wife

I had just been locked out of my own house on the day I buried my husband, and that night I slept in Winnie's spare room, under a quilt that was older than my marriage.

I should tell you who was sleeping under it. My name is Loretta Kessler, I was born in Port Alder, Pennsylvania, and for thirty-three years I was the official court reporter in the County Court of Common Pleas. That means I sat three feet below the judge and wrote down every word anyone said in that room, on a little machine called a stenograph, at two hundred words a minute, without an opinion. It is a good training for a marriage. It teaches you to hear what people say, and what they work very hard not to say.

I met Ray Kessler twenty years ago, when the county hired him to re-key every door in the courthouse. Most people walked past him in the hallways as if he were a coat rack. I stayed late one afternoon and watched him take a lock apart on a bench, laying the pins out in a row on a white handkerchief, and he did not look up for ten minutes. Then he said, without turning around, "You're the only one who ever stays to watch."

"I'm trained to," I said. "It's all going in the record."

He laughed so hard he lost a pin under the radiator. We were married the following year.

I had been widowed once before, at forty-one, when my first husband, a mailman with the kindest feet in Pennsylvania, died of cancer, and I did not expect to be asked again. Ray had been widowed too. His first wife, Joan, died when their daughter Tessa was nineteen. By the time I came along, Tessa was twenty-two, in a leather jacket, with her mother's chin, and she looked at me across the table at the restaurant where we announced it as if I were a bill she had not ordered.

"Dad's wife," she said, when someone asked her later what she thought. She said it that way for nineteen years. At Thanksgiving, at graduations, at Ray's seventieth birthday, where she arranged the family photograph and somehow I ended up holding the camera. She never once called me Mom, which I would not have wanted, and she never once called me Lori, which I would have allowed. It was always Loretta, said with every letter pressed flat, like a flower in a book.

I tried for a while. I sent birthday cards with real checks in them. I drove out to Foxglove Pet Lodge, the pet boarding place she runs on Route Twenty, and I brought coffee and admired the dogs. Eventually I understood that I could not win a war against a dead woman, and I stopped enlisting. Ray knew. He said once, on the porch, in his slow way, "She's not mad at you, Lo. She's mad at the calendar." I said I would take the calendar's side if it came to that, and he hummed a hymn out of tune to change the subject. He was very good at that.

That first night at Winnie's, my phone buzzed on the nightstand, and I read the texts the way I used to read exhibits. The first said, Please don't come to the house tonight. The second said, I'll have your things ready tomorrow. The third arrived at eleven fifteen and said, I'm sorry it has to be like this.

I noticed that she had not said she was sorry for what she had done. Only that it had to be like this. I wrote the three messages, with times, on the back of a church bulletin, and I turned off the light. I did not sleep, but I lay still.

On Sunday, at noon, Winnie drove me back to Birchwood Lane. It had stopped snowing. The sky over the lake was the color of a dishpan. Tessa's car was not in the driveway.

There were eleven black garbage bags lined up along the porch rail, with snow settling on their shoulders like a congregation. I counted them, because I count things. Through the thin plastic I could see the shape of my winter coats, my good shoes, a lamp, the framed picture of Ray holding a trout that he swore was bigger than it looked. At the end of the row, leaning against the rail without a bag, sat my steno case, the gray hard shell with the dented corner, with a rim of snow already on top. She had not even covered it.

Winnie said the word she had not said since nineteen seventy-one. Then she said it again, for the bags.

I walked up the steps and lifted the case off the rail and brushed it with my sleeve. The machine inside was my working life, thirty-three years of other people's worst days, and it was not hers to put out in the weather. I did not say that out loud. I only held it against my chest and looked at the door, at the bright cheap lock that had replaced Ray's, and I felt my hands begin to shake.

"Loretta," Winnie said, "we are going to need a bigger car."

We still had two trips of garbage bags to carry when the shop's old tan van turned onto the lane, since Tessa had apparently taken Ray's truck. Out of the van unfolded Gus Pelletier, who is twenty-seven years old and six foot four, with ears that go scarlet in any temperature below fifty. He had been Ray's apprentice for six years. He stood at the bottom of the steps holding his cap in both hands like a man at a wake.

"Missus Kessler," he said. "Winnie called the shop last night. I'm so sorry. I'd have come then, but she said you needed to sleep. Let me take those."

He took four bags at once. I let him, because he needed to be useful more than I needed to be proud. We loaded the van and Winnie's wagon. My own car had sat all night at the curb with snow on its roof, and since the keys were in my handbag and Tessa had not thought of them, Gus drove it over to Winnie's driveway as well. When he came back up the steps for the last bag, he stopped beside the front door and stared at the new deadbolt, the way a doctor stares at an X-ray.

He touched it with one finger. He crouched and looked at the strike plate. Then he stood up very straight, and the red went out of his ears.

"Missus Kessler," he said, "whoever picked this lock doesn't know a deadbolt from a doorknob. It's a nineteen-dollar builder-grade lock, and the screws are half an inch too short. Somebody put it in fast and unhappy. A pry bar would take it off in a minute. Ray would never have allowed it in the building."

"It's a very poor lock," I agreed. "Tessa seems to be very proud of it."

He nearly smiled. Then he looked at me, and I saw a young man trying to decide whether he was allowed to ask something.

"Missus Kessler," he said. "Did you find it? The thing on Ray's coat?"
