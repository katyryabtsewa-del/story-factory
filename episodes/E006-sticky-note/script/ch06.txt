# Rehearsal

By eight o'clock on Thursday night, an hour after Curtis's taillights had left Hollis Street, my kitchen table held four people, a pot of coffee, and the rough outline of a plan.

Fern had come across the side yard in her barn coat. Detective Hatch had parked his plain gray car in her driveway instead of mine. Marisol came last, through the back gate, with her hood up, and she sat on the edge of her chair as though it might be taken away. I gave her coffee and a slice of Fern's pound cake, and she held the cake without eating it.

First I told them what I had heard that evening, every word of it, the way I would recite a measurement. The man from Marlow Bridge Capital. Two hundred twelve thousand dollars. The first of February. After Friday it's all in order.

Detective Hatch wrote it in a small notebook. "That's motive," he said, "and a date. Now we need the day itself."

He laid out the morning like a man setting a table. Marisol would be at the front desk when we arrived. At a quarter to ten she would let him in through the back and into the file room, which sat behind the conference room with only a thin wall and a slatted door between them. He would stand there with his thermos and notepad and not come out until the right moment.

"And the right moment is?" Fern asked.

"When somebody says something I can use." He turned to me. "Your telephone has a recording program on it. Your daughter set it up last year, for your voicemails."

"I have never once used it."

"You'll use it tomorrow. Start it before you walk in and keep the phone in your cardigan pocket. In general terms, ma'am, in Tennessee you may record a conversation you are part of, and nobody else in the room has to be told. So you must be in it, every word."

"What do I say?"

"What a confused woman would say. Ask them to remind you what the first paper was, and when you signed it, and what you wore, and what the weather was. People love to fill a silence for someone who seems lost. And don't finish a sentence for them."

"I have spent forty years not finishing sentences for brides," I said. "I can do that."

Then I went to the kitchen drawer, the one where every house keeps its rubber bands and dead batteries and the key to nothing, and took out a packet of papers folded in thirds. "Detective, you asked to see this tonight," I said. "Marisol, you ought to see it too."

It was the discharge packet from the Riverbend Eye Surgery Center in Cleveland, Tennessee. At the top it said Josephine A. Ledbetter, and below it, in printed type, a list of times. Check-in, eleven o'clock. Procedure, one-thirty. Discharged, three-ten.

Marisol put down her cake. "Missus Ledbetter. Mister Brandt's paper says you signed at two-fifteen."

"Tuesday, the eighteenth of November," I said. "I was in a recovery chair, with a plastic shield on my face, drinking apple juice out of a paper cup."

Nobody said a word. Then Fern gave a short bark of a laugh and took a glossy print from her pocketbook. She had it made at the drugstore, she said, because she never trusted a telephone to keep anything. It showed the parking lot at Riverbend, shining wet with rain, and in the middle of it a seventy-one-year-old woman in a clear plastic eye shield, taped at the edges, and enormous wraparound sunglasses the nurse had handed her. She looked like someone who had lost a duel. Fern held her telephone beside it, with the time in the corner, three-twelve, and the one-word text she had sent me that afternoon. Pirate.

I laughed until I had to take off my glasses. It was the first honest laughter I had had in two days, and it brought Marisol's with it, and then even the detective's long shoulders began to shake. Fern said she had been saving that picture for my funeral.

When I could speak, I said, "What strikes me is that Curtis never looked. My surgery was set for the twenty-fifth. The center called with a cancellation, and I took the eighteenth, and I told only Nicole and Fern, because I did not want a fuss. Nicole forgot to tell Curtis, and he was in Nashville at a seminar that week. He picked the eighteenth because Mister Teague's calendar was empty. It never crossed his mind to wonder about mine."

"Men like that don't ask what day you were busy," Fern said. "They only check whether there's room."

Detective Hatch slid the packet and the print into a clear envelope and sealed it. "Show them to no one till tomorrow, Missus Ledbetter. If you wave them early, he tells a different story, and we lose the one he's already told. Let him finish his version first. Then you give him yours."

"I'd like Nicole to see them," I said. "Not hear it from me. See it, in black and white."

"I'd like that too," said the detective, and for a moment he looked tired. "That is as kind as I can make it, ma'am."

They left at ten. Marisol went last, and at the door I put her receipt back in her hand. She closed her fingers over it and said nothing.

At ten minutes after midnight my telephone lit up on the nightstand. It was Marisol. "Mister Teague knows someone talked," she wrote. "He asked me this afternoon who made copies. I said the machine does it by itself. He looked at me a long time. Is it still on?"

I lay there a while. A man who knows someone has talked has time to mend his story, and I could not tell which seam he would choose. Then I put on my glasses and typed with one finger. "It's still on," I wrote back. "Bring your receipt."

I slept for four hours, which was more than I expected. At six I dressed with great care. I wore my navy skirt and the gray cardigan with the deep pockets. In the left pocket I put Earl's button, a brass lineman's button from his work coat, the one I had carried for three years. In the right pocket I put the telephone. I opened the recording program and pressed the red circle at ten minutes past nine, and then I sat at the kitchen table with my reading glasses on my nose and waited.

Curtis came up the porch steps at a quarter past nine, in a good wool coat, with a paper cup of coffee in each hand. He had on his best smile, the one that comes with a handshake. He gave me the coffee, and he looked me over as though I were a car he was about to sell.

"Morning, Josie, hon. You look rested. Ready to get ahead of it?"

"I think I'm ready," I said. "I only hope I remember why."
