# Gaudet Blue Water

If you have stayed with me this far, you watched me tell my husband to bring a pen, and you may be wondering whether the sky fell. It did not. It came down the way the water in a pool goes down in October, a little each morning, until one day you look and the line is somewhere you did not expect.

The gentlemen from Houston called Tuesday afternoon to say the deal was paused. On Thursday, the second of October, they wrote to say the lender had pulled its commitment and the deal was over, which meant that the Friday closing, and the one point four million dollars, and the title of Founder and Chairman were gone. Tripp Haverty sent me a short note saying he had learned something about asking twice, and he was sorry for his part, and I wrote back to wish him well.

Boyd filed an emergency application with the board that same week. I heard about it the way you hear everything on the Gulf Coast, from a customer whose cousin works in that building. Wanda Pickett told him, kindly, exactly what the laminated card says. He could finish the jobs he had for sixty days, and he could not sign a new contract or pull a new permit, and he asked her if there was a different card. "There isn't one, sugar," she said. "I even looked." Wanda never told me a word of it herself, and I would not have asked.

Otis's crews finished all fourteen open jobs on time. Then, one by one, the people who made that company run went somewhere else. Of the ten who worked for him besides me, nine had left by Thanksgiving, with Otis the first. One went to Pensacola, and the other seven came to work for Otis and me.

I did not see what happened on November eighteenth, but a proctor at that sitting is married to one of our customers, and the story reached my porch within the week. Boyd walked into the testing room in a pressed golf shirt, sat down at the computer, and read every question aloud, the way he reads sales reports, until a proctor asked him to stop. He failed by four points. Afterward, in the parking lot, he stood next to his white truck with his face on the door, and called out, to no one, "Mimi?" Just once. Then he got in the truck.

My daughter did fly in, that December, and she brought a hat.

Skye left Boyd in January. By then she had learned what the company really was, which was not a rich founder's empire but a cash-flow problem with a nice smile, and she had ordered a new vault door for the quiet room at Still Water Studio that Boyd was supposed to pay for. She told him, that the energy around him was heavy, and she moved to the other side of town. I do not hold it against her. She never lied to him. She simply took him at his word, which is something I understand.

In December, Boyd sold the trucks and the customer list to a competitor in Mobile for three hundred and ten thousand dollars, a fair price for a list of names whose owners had already chosen. It paid off the company's line of credit and left him a small apartment near the interstate with a view of a dumpster. Corinne Doucet and I settled the divorce over cinnamon rolls, and her one letter kept my coverage in place without a missed prescription. The house stayed with me, and so did my credit union account and my retirement account. What was left, we split by plain arithmetic, and I did not ask for one thing spectacular. I did not want his shirt, only for him to learn whose name was on the wall.

He came to my porch in January, on a cold bright afternoon, in a jacket that looked a size too large. I was shelling pecans. He took the other rocker without being asked and sat a while with his hands between his knees. He looked older, the way a house does when somebody has finally taken down the awning.

"Mimi," he said, "I'd like to ask you something, and I hope you'll hear it fairly."

"I'm listening, Boyd."

"Would you take me on at Gaudet? Not as a partner. As the salesman, the face of the company. People like me, Mimi, and I'd work for commission. For old times' sake. Forty-one years is a long time."

I put down the nutcracker. I thought about it, I truly did, because I owed him that.

"No, Boyd," I said. "I'm sorry. The face of Gaudet Blue Water is going to be whoever answers the phone. But no."

He nodded slowly, and he did not argue, which was the most honest thing he had done in a decade. He stood up and said, "Bless your heart, Mimi," and I do not think he knew where he had heard it before.

The new company is called Gaudet Blue Water Service, which was my name before it was his. Otis insisted on a pelican for the truck door, not a dolphin, because he said that dolphin had been smiling at people long enough. Our very first customer was Nettie Rhodes. She wrote the check standing in her kitchen in a bathrobe and said, "That boy would be ashamed, if he had it in him." The first week we had sixty-one weekly service stops. By this April, one hundred and four customers. Two trucks, doing the work three used to. Otis is my partner at twenty percent, and I am at a desk again, with a box fan, in a small office by the marina, except that the sign on the door says my name.

On the wall of my back porch hangs the certificate Otis carried out of that conference room, and beside it a newer one with the same name and a different company.

It is April now, as I tell you this, and I am on that porch with a glass of iced tea sweating in my hand. A white truck with a pelican on the door has just turned into my drive, and Otis is climbing out with a bag of boiled peanuts and a customer list.

Boyd used to say I never read the fine print. I read every line of it for forty-one years. The one line I never read for what it was worth was my own.

So I would like to ask you something, since you have been kind enough to sit with me. If you had been in my chair, across that conference table, with the pen in your hand and the man you gave forty-one years to telling you to sign like always, would you have told him a week early, or waited for Tuesday? And I'd love to know where you are listening from, and what the weather is like there, because it is a beautiful evening here on Mobile Bay, and I am glad of the company.
