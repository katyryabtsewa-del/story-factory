# Sam's Last Good Year

The morning after my son's text, I carried half a kringle across Spaight Street to my oldest friend, and she cut herself a very large piece and told me what she thought of the Ashfords. That was in March. What follows is how the spring went.

Edith Larkin is seventy-one, a retired church secretary with reading glasses the size of saucers and no patience for nonsense. She brings lemon bars to every crisis, and by ten o'clock that Friday she had brought a pan of them to my porch, which tells you how she rated this one. We sat in my yellow kitchen with the stove ticking as it cooled, and she asked me the question I had been avoiding.

"Nora, has Gregory always been like this about the restaurant?"

I wanted to say no. Instead I looked at my kitchen window, and I remembered the booth.

Pruitt's Table had a back booth beside the pie case, with green vinyl seats and a window onto the alley. Gregory did his homework there from the time he was six years old until he left for college. He did long division with the smell of fryer oil drifting over his shoulder, and he ate his supper off a plate Sam passed through the pass-through, and the regulars used to wave at him as if he were a small mayor. I thought he loved it. I told myself so for years.

But I remember the autumn he was eleven, because he came home from school and stood in my kitchen with his backpack still on. "Mom, why do we have to smell like that?" he said. "The kids call me Fish Fry Greg."

I was standing at this same stove. I said, "Honey, that smell paid for your braces." It was true, and it was exactly the wrong thing to say, and he nodded and never raised the subject again. That was what I did not understand until much later. A child who stops complaining has not stopped hurting. He has only decided that nobody will do anything about it.

"He's thirty-nine, Nora," Edith said, when I had finished telling her. "At some point a man owns what he does."

"I know it," I said. "I just did not think it would be at my expense."

Now, you may be wondering about Sam. Sam Pruitt worked that grill every day we were open, and he sold the building nine years ago, the spring he got sick. He told me it was so we could have our last good year. We did have one. We drove to Door County in October and ate whitefish on a picnic table, and he hummed Sinatra the whole way home, and the following summer he was gone. Before he went, he made me promise two things, that I would keep cooking and that I would not let our son feel he had to choose between his parents and his pride. I have been trying to keep both promises ever since, and the second one was getting harder by the week.

April arrived with a cream-colored envelope. The invitation was heavy as a roofing shingle, with the words Courtney and Gregory in a font so thin I had to hold it to the window, and a hashtag at the bottom, pound sign and all, which I read aloud to Edith as though reciting a license plate. The colors, according to a card tucked inside, were sea salt and sage.

Two weeks after that, Courtney sent a seating chart by email, asking us to check our dietary needs. The head table held the wedding party. Table one held Richard Ashford, Courtney's father, and her mother and her aunts. I looked for my name for a while before I found it, and it was on table fourteen, which the little map showed pressed against a rectangle labeled service doors. My table held two of Gregory's bank colleagues, a cousin of Richard's I had never met, and me.

I called Gregory that evening. "Honey, I seem to be next to the kitchen."

"Oh, Mom, it's only a seating thing," he said. "The head table is just the wedding party. Everybody's somewhere."

"The mother of the groom is somewhere by the swinging doors."

"It's a tent, Mom. It's all one big room." He cleared his throat. "Are you getting enough rest? Courtney and I were just saying that the house must be a lot for you."

That was how he began to call me, in those weeks. He asked about my knees and my stairs and whether I still drove at night. Not once did he ask about me. I stood in my yellow kitchen holding the receiver and understood that my son had started to think of me as a project, a nice old woman to be managed gently into the back of the room.

What he did not know, because he had never asked, was what I had waiting in the drawer under the telephone. It was a card, a plain white one with a pressed violet on the front, and inside was a check made out to Gregory and Courtney for forty thousand dollars. I had decided to be generous with both of them, whatever they thought of my cooking. Some of the money was what remained of Sam's life insurance and some of it was mine. It was meant for the down payment on a house, with a note in my handwriting that said, Sam would have wanted you to have a kitchen. I had written that note in January, the night they got engaged, crying over a pie.

I looked at that card for a long time in April. Then I closed the drawer and left it there, and I did not mention it to anyone but Edith.

On the first Sunday in May, Gregory and Courtney came to my house for supper. I made the pot roast with horseradish cream, the real one, from my late mother-in-law's index card, and I set the table with the good plates. Courtney moved the food around her plate and said it was very homestyle. Gregory had two helpings and said nothing about it.

Over the pie, they got to talking about the wedding, as they did, and Gregory finally let his shoulders come down from around his ears. He took a drink of water and smiled at me, the first real smile I had seen from him since January.

"We got really lucky with the caterer, Mom," he said. "Richard knew somebody. They're booked two years out, but he pulled a string." He leaned back with his fork in the air. "It's Corso Events."
