# Thirty Minutes on Thursday

The green box on my phone had given me thirty minutes on Thursdays, and by March, even those were being canceled for reasons that included a lighting consultation and a delivery of pillows.

I was a grandmother, not a general. I kept bringing lemon bars to a house that had begun to treat them as a kind of delivery.

Then, on a Saturday in March, Odessa called me at nine in the morning, which is early for her, and said, "Marlene, are you sitting down?"

"I'm standing at the sink," I said.

"Sit down anyway."

Jonah had turned eight the day before. His present was a wooden train that Gerald had started to build before he died, an engine and four cars I had found in a box marked "Jonah, someday" and spent the winter finishing with my own sandpaper. I had left the underside alone, where Gerald's pencil marks were. It sat on my dining table, wrapped in a bedsheet because I had run out of paper. I had asked Trevor when the party would be, and he had said, "We're still figuring that out, Mom."

"There's a photograph," Odessa said. "On that girl's page. Balloons, a bounce house, twelve children, and a cake shaped like a locomotive. Posted last night. The caption says, 'Our boy turned eight at a small, sweet family party at home.'"

"Odessa, you follow her?"

"I follow her the way you follow a weather front," Odessa said. "Marlene, it was yesterday. Everybody was there but you."

I put down the dish towel. I did not cry, because I had a train to deliver. I put on my coat in the middle of a Saturday morning, without a pan of anything, which I had not done in six years, and drove to Tamarack Court with the train on the back seat in its bedsheet.

It was twelve minutes. I rehearsed what I would say four ways, and none of them was loud, because I have never been able to manage loud.

The cars were gone from the driveway. The bounce house was folded up on the lawn like a very large, very tired animal. I went to the side door, the one I had always used, and I put in my key.

It did not turn. I tried it again, and I tried it upside down, as though a lock might be fooled by good manners. It was a new lock, handsome brushed brass, put in without a word to the woman who owned the door.

Then the curtain moved in the kitchen window, and there was Cassidy, in a white apron, holding a cup of something. She raised her free hand and opened the window two inches.

"Marlene," she said, and she smiled brightly. "Hi. It's not really a good time."

"I came about Jonah's birthday," I said.

"It was a small thing," Cassidy said. "We kept it small, just us. And honestly, we've been working on boundaries as a family. I know it's hard to hear, but it's healthy. Boundaries, Marlene. It's healthy."

"My key doesn't work," I said.

"We rekeyed," Cassidy said. "For safety. I'll have Trevor get you a new one. Sometime."

She closed the window gently, the way you close a window on a bird you do not wish to frighten. I stood on the step with a useless key in my hand and understood that I had been uninvited from a party in a house that was mine. I drove home with the train still in its bedsheet, because a gift handed over a rekeyed door is not a gift.

Trevor came to my kitchen table that evening. I had called him, and I had not raised my voice, and I think that frightened him more than shouting would have. He sat in the chair he had sat in when he was nine, and he looked at the oilcloth.

"Mom," he said, "it's fine."

"Trevor, you didn't invite me to my grandson's birthday."

"It was a small thing, it was really more of a, Cass wanted to keep it simple. It's fine, Mom. Really, it's fine."

"I'd like you to look at me when you say it a third time," I said.

"It's fine, Mom," Trevor said, and rubbed the back of his neck, and did not look at me.

I had put a plate of lemon bars between us, and neither of us touched them. I chose the plainest question I had, the kind a bookkeeper asks when she has been hinting at a number for six years.

"Trevor, does Cassidy know whose name is on the deed?"

My son looked at the floor. He looked at it for so long that the refrigerator cycled on and off, and a clock I have had since my wedding struck the half hour. He did not say yes, and he did not say no, which in my experience is what a man says when the honest answer costs too much.

"I'll talk to her," he finally said. "I will, Mom. Soon."

I gave him until the end of June to tell her himself. He did not. I know because of what happened next.

Two days later, Odessa came to my door at seven in the evening, in the purple jacket, with a manila folder under one arm and a bag of peaches under the other, because she believes that no important conversation should happen without fruit. She sat at my table and laid out three sheets of paper. She had pulled the public record on the house and written out, in her loan officer's block letters, the numbers I knew by heart.

"Marlene," Odessa said, "I have known you for thirty years, and I am going to say this once, and then I am going to eat a peach. You are not a guest in that house. You are not support staff. You are the bank."

"I'm his mother," I said.

"You are both," said Odessa. "That's the problem. They have been so comfortable with the mother that they forgot about the bank."

I did not answer her. I looked at the three pages. The idea had already been sitting in me for weeks, quietly, like a seed in a drawer, and I had just never said it to myself in a plain sentence.

Odessa ate her peach. I made tea. I had given Trevor until the end of June, so in April I did nothing at all. I brought lemon bars on Sundays and took my thirty minutes on Thursdays, and I gave a very good impression of a woman who was being patient.

Then, on a Monday in May, while I was weeding my front garden on my knees with a trowel, my phone buzzed on the step. It was Cassidy, and it was a text message, which is how she preferred to deliver the things she did not want to say to my face.

It said: "Bernadette is moving into the basement suite in August. Trevor agreed. Please clear your things out by July."
