# Sixty Thousand and the Buick

By the second week of September I was running a swimming pool company out of a supply room, and my husband was running short of patience with how well I was taking it.

He asked me to lunch that Thursday, which should have warned me. We went to the Dockside Diner on the bay, where Boyd ordered a grilled shrimp salad with the dressing on the side, which was new, and I ordered the fried catfish, which was not. Then he took a pen out of his golf shirt, drew a napkin across the table, and wrote a number on it, the way he used to write estimates on the back of a hand.

"I want to be fair, Mimi," he said.

I turned the napkin around with one finger. It said sixty thousand dollars and the Buick. Underneath, in his big loopy handwriting: the house is to be sold, and the money goes to Boyd. Underneath that, as if the thought had come late: nothing else to be divided.

"That's the whole offer," I said.

"It's generous. You're sixty-five. You don't need a lot." He smiled at me kindly. "My attorney will send it over properly, so it's all nice and official."

I folded the napkin and put it in my purse. I keep every scrap of paper that has a number on it, and I could tell this one would be useful.

"Thank you for lunch, Boyd," I said, and I ate every bite of that catfish, which was very good.

The attorney's letter arrived on Monday, on thick cream paper, and it said exactly what the napkin had said, with more commas. The next envelope was smaller, and it was from the company's health plan. It informed me, in the cheerful tone those letters use, that my coverage would end with the month, at the request of the plan holder, for the transition. I am old enough for Medicare, but my supplement and my prescriptions had always ridden along on the company plan. I sat in the supply room with that letter and the box fan for a good minute. It is a particular feeling, being cut off by a man who learned his own blood type from you.

That afternoon I called a lawyer. I had her name from the woman who does my hair, who had been divorced twice and had opinions.

Corinne Doucet practiced family law in two small rooms above a bakery on Bay Street in Dauphin Landing, and the whole place smelled like cinnamon rolls, which I considered a good omen. She was forty-one years old, with a calm face and a straight back. She read the napkin, the lawyer's letter, and the insurance notice, in that order, without a word, and then she took off her glasses.

"Missus Lanier, I'm going to tell you what I tell everyone. Don't sign anything, don't move out of the house, and don't do anything dramatic." She paused. "Is there anything dramatic you were planning?"

"I was planning to be extremely cooperative."

"Good. That's the most underrated strategy in my business." She pulled a legal pad toward her. "In your husband's company, what do you hold, on paper, that can walk out the door with you?"

I thought about it. "I don't own the company. It's Boyd's name on the trucks. I've got my own savings account at the credit union, forty-four thousand dollars, and a little retirement account. And the house is in both of our names."

"That's yours already. That's not what I mean. What do you do at that company that nobody else can do?"

I said, as modestly as I could, that I did most of it, and that was the whole trouble.

"Did you ever sign a non-compete? A paper that says if you leave, you can't work in the same business?"

"Never. Boyd never asked." I shrugged. "He never thought I'd go anywhere."

Then she asked me, in the same quiet tone, who held the company's contractor license.

"Me," I said, and I heard how it sounded out loud in a lawyer's office, like the name of an asset. That morning, for no reason I could name, I had taken a photograph of the certificate on the wall while Tripp was at lunch. I found it on my phone and handed it across the desk.

State Licensing Board for Pool and Spa Contractors, it said across the top, and then, in the center of the page in good plain print, the words qualifying agent, and under that my name. Marilyn G. Lanier. I had known that name for twelve years. I had just never asked what it was worth.

"Missus Lanier," Corinne said, gently, "I'm a divorce lawyer. I am not a licensing lawyer. But I can read, and I can tell you that a license held by a person is not usually something a company owns. It belongs to the person who passed the test."

"Boyd walked past that wall every day for twelve years."

"Then he has had plenty of time to learn what it says," said Corinne. "I would find out what the state does when a qualifier leaves. Quietly. Before anyone knows you're asking. Don't fret about that insurance letter. I'll write to the plan today, and you will not miss a day of your prescriptions."

I went down the stairs with a cinnamon roll in a paper bag that she had pressed on me, and I sat in Gladys in the parking lot and ate it, feeling like a woman who had found a twenty-dollar bill sewn into the lining of an old coat.

The next day I ran into Skye Calloway in the produce section of the Bay Street Market, which I regard as a low blow on the part of the universe. She was thirty-nine years old, dressed in white linen, with a basket holding a single mango. She put her hand on her heart and approached me as if I were a skittish horse.

"Marilyn," she said. "I just want you to know that I hold space for your anger."

"That's very thoughtful," I said. "I'm out of anger at the moment, but I'll take the space. I've got a supply room."

She blinked, searching her training for a response and not finding one. I bought my cantaloupe and went home.

That night my daughter called from Denver and asked if she should fly in. "Mama, you ought to fight him. You ought to take him for everything."

"Baby," I said, "I intend to be so helpful to your father that he won't know what hit him."

"What does that mean?"

"It means don't buy a plane ticket yet. And when you do, bring a hat."

After we hung up, I sat at the kitchen table where I had studied for that exam twelve years before, and I did not decide to be angry, and I did not decide to be kind. My phone buzzed against the wood. It was Corinne, who types in complete sentences even when she texts. It said, Do not resign yet. Ask the board who may resign, and effective when.
