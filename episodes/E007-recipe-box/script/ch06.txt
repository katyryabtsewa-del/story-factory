# The Man from Ames

I got the red toolbox back on Wednesday, though it cost me a locked shed, a short night, and three hours sitting on a piece of steel.

Greg and I had walked straight from the truck to the machine shed on Tuesday morning, with the brass key in my pocket. The door was shut with a new silver padlock, and a yellow note under the hasp said, in Marnie's tidy hand, Secured for the buyer. Helpers at five thirty Wednesday. Greg phoned her, and she called back at four and said kindly that her helpers had the key, that they would carry the box out to the lawn at five thirty so the man could load it at nine, and that we would all talk after.

Greg offered to put his shoulder to the door. I told him it had been hung by a shop teacher and it would win, so I did what I used to do when a road was closed on the route. I went around by waiting.

At twenty-five past five on Wednesday I was standing at the shed door in my canvas jacket with a cup of coffee. At five thirty exactly, two young men in gray shirts pulled in, unlocked the padlock, and found a woman in their way. "Morning, ma'am," said the taller one, and they carried the toolbox out to the grass by the driveway, laid a blue tarp over it, and stuck a note on the lid that said Ames, nine o'clock. Nobody argues with a mail carrier at a door. I sat down on it. I could have opened it then, but until I had said no to the buyer's face it did not feel like mine.

At twenty to nine a pickup with a utility trailer turned into my gravel. The man who got out was about fifty, in a seed cap. He took in a seventy-year-old woman sitting on a toolbox in the damp with a sticky note stuck to her knee, and he took off his cap.

"Morning, ma'am," he said. "I believe I'm early."

"You're right on time," I said. "But I'm afraid the box isn't for sale."

"The lady said everything out here was an estate cleanup," he said. "She said the owner had passed and the family wanted it gone."

"He did pass," I said. "I'm the family."

He turned his cap around in his hands. "Ma'am, I'd just as soon not carry off a thing that makes a woman sit on it in the rain."

I counted out his forty dollars in twenties, and he looked past me into the open shed. "That's a nice old band saw," he said, carefully.

Walt owned two, and he had cursed the older one since nineteen ninety-one. I had told Marnie I was not selling Walt's tools, and I meant it, but this sale was mine, and a man who drives from Ames at dawn deserves something for it. I let it go for sixty dollars, which was more than Marnie's tag. At the end he said whoever built that pegboard knew what he was doing. I said the man who built it had whistled the whole time, and he said he could tell.

When the trailer was gone I took the tarp off the box and unlocked the padlock. The brass key turned as if it had been waiting.

The lid came up on a smell of oil and old wood. Under a tray of pencils and a tape measure lay three things wrapped in a clean dish towel. A green spiral notebook. A bank passbook with a blue cover. And a folded index card with Bern on the front, which said, in pencil, Don't give it away on a whim. Walk the route first.

The passbook was from Fenmore Savings and Loan, and across the top Walt had written Route Fund. The deposits ran down the column, fifteen dollars, forty, one hundred and twenty, going back sixteen years, and the last line gave a balance of eighteen thousand four hundred dollars. I know what a roof and a furnace cost, and the sum would not come out until I read the notebook. The lumberyard and the hardware store had charged him at cost for sixteen years, and he had put his summer pay in besides. In the margin, in pencil, he had named me beneficiary, which is exactly the kind of paperwork a shop teacher does.

The notebook held the rest, in his hand. Date, name, mile marker, what he had done. The very first line read Hilda Tolliver, mile eleven, roof, two thousand nine, and under it, in the margin, he had drawn a small dot. After that there were eighteen more. A furnace for Dewey Marsh. A well pump for the Lund twins. A wheelchair ramp for Ila Fenn's father. A rope for the church bell, for the Pruss family, who ring it. A shelter at mile marker nineteen, for the three Alden children who wait there for the bus.

Then I noticed the dates. Every one of them was a Thursday.

For sixteen years my husband had said, "I'll be at the shop, Bern," and I had never once asked at whose. I looked for mad, the way you hunt for your glasses on top of your head, and found something large and sore and proud instead.

I was still sitting there when Marnie's minivan came up the drive.

She got out with her clipboard and looked at the empty trailer tracks, and at the open toolbox, and at the notebook in my lap. "Where is the man from Ames?"

"He went home with a band saw."

"I gave my word to a stranger." She did not raise her voice, because she never does, and it was worse for that. "I can't get this house ready if you undo every single thing I do. Bernie, you cannot live in a museum."

I closed the notebook. "A museum is where you keep things nobody visits, Marnie. A route is the opposite. Everything on it is still in use. Every box has somebody at the other end waiting for something."

"It's a tin of index cards." Her voice shook, just a little, and I saw her catch it. "Two hundred scraps of paper that you never cooked from."

"I never cooked from it," I agreed. "I read it. You didn't throw out a shelf. You threw out the route."

For one moment I thought she would argue. Then something went across her face that I could not read.

"The open house is Saturday at ten," she said, quietly. "It's small. You don't even have to come."

"It's my house."

"I know it is." She turned to go. "I'm only trying to help you."

I could have phoned Bryce that minute and called off the whole business. I decided against it. A cancelled open house would make Marnie an enemy. A full lawn might make her a question.

I took down the map of Route Three from behind the vacuum cleaner, big as a bedsheet, with two hundred and forty-odd pins, and I unrolled it over the passbook and the ledger, and I did not hurry. I had eighteen thousand four hundred dollars, nineteen porches, and three days to find out whether anybody would come.
