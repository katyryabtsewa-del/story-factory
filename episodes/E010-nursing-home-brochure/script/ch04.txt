# Eleven, Nineteen, Twenty-Seven

On my second day behind the keypad door, I started listening to Silverleaf Terrace the way I used to listen to a telephone line. A person who has done that work for thirty-six years never really stops. You hear the background first, the television, the dog, the car door, and the background tells you more than the caller does.

Birch Court had a background of its own. It had the squeak of a walker wheel that needed oil, and it had the call lights. Over each door was a small white bulb, and when a resident pressed the button by the bed, the bulb lit up and a soft chime sounded at the nurses' station. Then you waited.

I started timing it Monday morning. I sat in the vinyl chair by the open door of my room, with the notebook on my knee, and I wrote down the time a light came on and the time somebody turned it off. The first light, a gentleman down the hall, took eleven minutes. The second, a lady who needed help to the bathroom, took nineteen, and I do not need to tell you what can happen in nineteen minutes to a woman who needs the bathroom. By Tuesday dinner I had a column of numbers, and the longest was twenty-seven.

I counted the staff, too. On the day shift there were three aides for the nineteen residents in Birch Court,. They were not unkind. They were outnumbered.

At ten past eleven Monday morning I carried a lunch tray down the hall for a lady who couldn't reach her table, and an aide with about four seconds of patience left in her day took it out of my hands. "Honey, I know you mean well," she said, "but you can't. Insurance. Go sit."

I went and sat. That is how I met Cyril Abernathy, properly, at the dining table where he had slid me a pencil the night before.

Cyril was ninety-one. He had been the county surveyor for thirty-eight years, and he sat in his plaid blanket with his pencil bag and a worn yellow field book on his knees. He spoke the way he had worked, in measurements. "That's sixty-two feet from the nurses' station to the east stairwell," he told me, "and the stairwell door has been alarmed since two years ago March."

"How do you know?"

"I pace it." He opened the field book, and every page was in small square handwriting, columns of dates and counts. Forty-one paces from the dining room to the keypad door. A note about a call light on the previous Tuesday that had taken thirty-one minutes. He had logged the building for nine years because, he said, "a thing you don't measure gets away from you." He was in Birch Court for wandering. He had walked out to the county road one morning to check a survey marker he had set in nineteen seventy-four, and somebody had written wandering on a form.

"Mister Abernathy," I said, "do you know what day it is?"

"It's Monday, October twelfth, and the soup is split pea, and you should not drink the decaf." He tapped his hearing aid. "I hear fine when it's turned on. The trouble is, they like it better off."

Dana called that afternoon on the room phone. "How is the spa?" she said. I told her the soup had a good texture. She said she would stop by later in the week with some paperwork "so there's no stress," and I said I would look forward to it.

That night I met Imelda Ortiz.

I could not sleep, and at a quarter past eleven I went out to the hall in my slippers, where a short, strong woman in pink scrubs printed with cartoon cats was pushing a linen cart with one hand and holding up a very confused man with the other. She had a rosary in the pocket of her scrubs, and she had the quick, tired hands of someone who had done three jobs for eleven years. She told me, in a low voice, that she was the only aide on Birch Court after ten o'clock.

"Nineteen residents and now you," she said. "One of me. Don't tell anybody I told you. I like this job."

"I won't tell anybody anything," I said, "that I don't have to."

On Tuesday night I slept, and then I woke, and looked at the clock on the nightstand. It was two thirty-nine. A moment later, down the hall, came a heavy, soft crash, and then a thin, high noise that was not quite a word.

I was in the hallway before I had decided to be. Cyril lay on the carpet outside his room, on his side, with his blanket half off and one arm bent under him in a way an arm should not bend. His field book lay open a few feet away. He was awake and gray in the face, and he said distinctly, "Forty-one paces. I was going to the door."

At the other end of the hall, I could hear Imelda's voice and the crying of another resident, and I knew she was holding someone up in a bathroom and could not let go.

I knelt on the carpet. I used my low voice, the one that has gotten a lot of people through a lot of nights. "Cyril, it's Peg. Don't move your head. Tell me your name and where you are."

"Cyril Abernathy. Silverleaf. Birch Court. Floor, the second."

"Good. Can you feel your feet?"

"Both of them."

I could see his wrist already swelling. I put my cardigan under his head, and with a nod from Imelda, who had come to the end of the hall and taken it in at one look, I went to the telephone on the wall behind the nurses' station, the old beige one with the long cord, and dialed nine-one-one.

"Nine-one-one, what is the address of your emergency?"

I knew the voice. I had trained that woman twenty years ago, on a night shift very much like this one.

"Silverleaf Terrace, Birch Court, second floor," I said. "Let me read that back. A ninety-one-year-old man, fall, awake, breathing, probable broken wrist, possible hip. Time is two forty-four."

There was a pause. Then, very quietly, the voice said, "Peg? Is that you, Dispatch Four?"

"It is. Send them, honey."

"They're rolling," she said. I could hear her smiling. "It's good to hear you."

The ambulance arrived in eleven minutes, which was faster than any call light on that floor. Two young paramedics came in with a gurney, and I stood out of their way. Gideon lived in the townhomes across the road, he had told me on the tour, and could see the front portico from his kitchen. I was holding Cyril's field book when the elevator opened with a ding, and out stepped Gideon Pratt in a fleece vest pulled over striped pajamas, his hair standing up, his lanyard around his neck.

"Who," he said, "authorized that call?"
