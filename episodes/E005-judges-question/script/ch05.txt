# The Mailbox in Medford

Danny had stepped down, Tucker Rand had leaned forward in the last row, and I sat very still and thought about a Tuesday one week before, when I drove into town to find out why I had never seen a property tax notice.

I had been reading the petition at my kitchen table that morning, for perhaps the fifth time, and the third item stopped me. It said I had missed an installment, and that the county had sent notices, and that the notices had gone unanswered. I have paid my taxes on the first of the month, in person, with a check from my green ledger, every year since Lloyd and I took over the farm. I had no memory of any notice. Then I began to think about what else I had not seen lately, and I realized that I had not received my electric bill in six weeks, or the seed catalogs I order every winter, or the card my cousin sends me every February with a pressed flower inside.

I put on my barn jacket and drove my old truck to the Tamarack Flats post office, which is a low brick building with a flag and a bench, and a postmaster who used to ride my bus. She sat in the third seat on the left for six years, with a lunch box shaped like a horse. She is a woman of forty-five now, with reading glasses on her head and an excellent memory for what people mail, and when I came through the door she looked up from the counter and her face changed.

"Missus Halvorsen," she said. "I was going to come see you. I have been trying to work out how to do it all week."

"Then I saved you a trip," I said. "I think my mail has gone missing."

She took off her glasses, looked at the two people in line behind me, and said, "Can you come around the back?"

We stood in the sorting room, among the gray bins and the smell of paper and rubber bands, and she opened a drawer and took out a small card. It was a change of address form, the kind anyone can fill out in two minutes, and it said that Vera Halvorsen of the old farmhouse at the end of Orchard Hollow Road had moved her mail, effective immediately, to a postal box in Medford. It had come in an envelope, she said, not at the counter, which was why nobody had asked for identification. It was dated six weeks earlier. That was a full week before the first white truck had come up the county road.

"I should have called you," she said. "It did not feel right. But the signature matched the card on file, close enough, and I am not supposed to second-guess a customer's own form."

I looked at the signature. It read Vera Halvorsen in blue ink, in a hand that was almost mine. But the sevens in the date were crossed in the middle with a short bar, the way they teach it in some schools, and I have never in my life crossed a seven. I know only one person who does, because I sat at the kitchen table with him at eight years old and tried to cure him of it and failed.

"Thank you," I said. "Could you put my mail back the way it was?"

"I already did," she said, and she was nearly crying. "Last Friday. I was not going to sit there and let it go on."

I should tell you that I did not cry either. I drove my truck to the end of the post office lot, put it in park, and sat there with both hands on the wheel for what my husband's watch told me was forty minutes. People walked in with packages and walked out with stamps. I thought about the sevens. I knew my son was ashamed, because I had seen it at Christmas, in the way he would not look at the pear pie. But a man can be ashamed and still go on doing it. I think that is how most of it is done.

Then I drove home, the long way, by the river road, and it was one of those clear, cold March afternoons when the whole valley looks as though somebody has washed it. The mountain stood white in the distance. At the farm I parked by the red barn, and I did what I always do. I walked the rows. The buds on the Bosc were still tight and brown, a week or so from breaking, and in the north corner the orange stakes stood in the grass with their pink ribbons lifting a little in the wind. I counted them again, forty-one. Then I stood a while with my hand on a tree that Lloyd's father had planted, and I told it that I would not be going anywhere.

The courtroom clock said ten thirty-six, and my watch said ten forty, and Judge Marsh looked at both of them in turn, as if she had a private opinion about the clock, and called a recess of fifteen minutes.

Brent stood up with his lawyer, and they went out through the side door together, speaking low. Tucker Rand got up from the last row and followed them with his hands in his pockets. I waited until the gallery had moved, and then I took the long way out, past the water fountain at the end of the hall, because I needed a drink and because my knees had gone stiff.

The old courthouse is all tile and marble, and it carries sound the way a drum does. I was still around the corner when I heard my son's voice, low and tight, saying, "Once she's under, the timing works. The petition goes through, I sign, and you have your authority by the end of the month."

"That is what we were told," said a soft voice I knew from the back row. "I will be honest, Brent. We are a little concerned about the optics of the sheriff's reports. Our people already got what they needed from the ridge. This is a timing matter now. Nothing more."

"The judge isn't going to look at the reports. They're just a deputy saying nobody was there."

"Of course," said Tucker Rand. "A timing matter."

I stood with my hand flat on the cool marble, and I did not move, and I was not angry. I want you to understand that. What I felt was the thing I used to feel at the top of a mountain grade in an ice storm, with forty children behind me, when I knew that the next thing I did would be the only thing that mattered. I counted. Six evenings. Forty-one stakes. Two men, then three. One plate, ending seven four one. And I decided, very calmly, that if anybody ever asked me, I would give them every number I had.

I turned the corner and walked down the hall without my drink. Pearl was sitting on a bench with her feed-sack purse in her lap, unwrapping a peppermint, and she looked up and studied my face.

"Vera Halvorsen," she said, in her loudest whisper, "you look like you are about to drive a bus."
