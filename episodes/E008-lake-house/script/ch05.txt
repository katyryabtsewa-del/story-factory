# Nan Pelletier Answers on the First Ring

By nine o'clock on Thanksgiving night, I had read everything my son left on that tablet, and I was calling a realtor on a holiday.

Nan picked up before the first ring had finished.

"Nan Pelletier, realtor and notary, what's wrong?"

"Nan, it's Dot Ellery."

"I know who it is, Dot. I have a caller list." There was a clatter behind her, a dog barking and a television, and then a door shut and the world got quieter. "Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Is the house on fire?"

"Not yet."

"Well, that's a relief, because I'm at my sister's and she's just cut the pie, and I have had exactly one slice of it." She breathed out. "Okay. Tell me."

So I told her everything, from the pool to the quilt to the room in January. Nan didn't interrupt. When I got to the room, she said one word I had never heard from her before, and I will not repeat it in polite company.

"Dot," she said. "Did he ask you?"

"No."

"Did he ask you one single thing?"

"No, Nan."

"Give me twenty minutes. I'm a Catholic, not a monk. Holiday weekends are for closings." Then she hung up, and I put the kettle on for the second time that day.

She came up the drive at twenty-five past nine in a white pickup with a plow blade on the front. Nan is fifty-two years old, five foot two in her boots, and she wore a plaid wool coat over sweatpants printed with small cartoon turkeys. She had a zippered canvas bag under one arm and her reading glasses pushed up into her red hair. She came straight into the kitchen like she'd been doing it all her life, which she more or less had.

"Coffee," she said, as if it were a legal document.

I poured her a cup from the percolator. She sat at Frank's table and opened the canvas bag, and out came a stack of folders and a rubber notary stamp in a black case, which she set down very gently. Then she picked up the tablet and looked at the photographs for a long time.

"Primary suite," she said at last. "Well, that's a crime against your quilt."

"It's a crime against the English language."

"It's both." She swiped to the next photo and her mouth went flat. "They put a comforter on the bed."

"A white one."

"Of course it's white." She pushed the tablet away as if it were warm. Then she opened the top folder to the copy of the deed. Nan ran a finger down the first page.

"One name," she said. "Dorothy Ellery. Sole owner. Frank did that on purpose, and it's the smartest thing a man ever did for his wife in this county." She tapped the second page. "And here's his clause."

She read it out loud, in the careful voice of someone who has read ten thousand documents and still likes a good sentence. "If the grantee elects to sell, the north adjoining owner, Amos Thibodeau, shall have first refusal at the price offered by a bona fide buyer."

"Amos told me about that today," I said. "Over turkey."

"Did he now." Nan almost smiled. "That's Frank talking. He liked a clause."

"What does it mean, in plain English?"

"It means Craig can't sell this house, Dot. He's not on the deed, he's not on the tax bill, and he has no power of attorney. He's a man with a tablet and a feeling." She sipped her coffee. "What matters is what you do next, and whether Amos gets his first look, and at what price."

"What's it worth?"

"The town assessor has it at six hundred and forty thousand."

"Then I'd like six hundred and fifty thousand," I said. "In cash."

Nan put her cup down. "You want to ask for more than the assessor?"

"I want to ask for the roof, Nan. Frank put that roof on himself. It's a good roof."

She laughed, a real laugh, the kind that makes a woman put a hand on her heart. "Oh, Dot. Okay. Six-fifty. I'll write it up tonight."

"Then I need to call Amos."

It was ten o'clock. I dialed the number Amos has had since the telephone company was a single office in Harrow. He answered on the sixth ring.

"Ellery."

"Amos, it's Dot. I need you to come over."

There was a long pause, the sort a man takes when he's deciding whether a thing is worth putting his boots on for. "Now?"

"Now."

"Be right there."

It took him eleven minutes. I watched his flashlight come down the shore path through the falling snow. He came in stamping, wearing long johns under his red barn coat and the plaid hat with the earflaps tied under his chin. He looked like a man who had been dressed since Labor Day.

Nan was in a Harrow Hockey Boosters sweatshirt over flannel pajamas. I was in Frank's old bathrobe, which I hadn't worn since the funeral. Everybody in that kitchen was wearing something they'd slept in, and not one of us looked like we'd slept.

Amos sat down across from me and read the clause twice. He did it with his finger, the way Frank used to read a tide table.

"Six-fifty," he said.

"Yes."

"Cash."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, then took a cell phone out of his coat pocket and laid it on the table between the coffee cups. He tapped one button and set it on speaker.

It rang four times. "Dad?" said a man's voice, thick with sleep. "It's ten o'clock."

"Ray," Amos said. "Dot Ellery is selling the lake house."

A long silence. "Is that right."

"It is."

"What's the number?"

Amos told him. Ray breathed out through his nose, and I heard a television go quiet in the background. "I've got that in the savings account for boat engines," Ray said. "I can have a cashier's check at the bank by eight. Tell her yes, Dad."

Amos looked at me across the table. "He says yes."

"I heard," I said.

Nan was already filling in the purchase agreement. "Closing's at nine tomorrow," she said. "Right here, at this table. You'll have the check by then, Ray?"

"Yes, ma'am," said the phone.

"Good. Dot, you're going to sign your name about eleven times."

"I've signed my name for worse reasons."

"That's the spirit."

Amos tugged his earflaps down and walked back up the shore path. Nan drove off in her plow truck, promising to be back at a quarter to six with a thermos. I stood in the kitchen with the stove ticking low and the house finally quiet.

At midnight my phone rang on the table, next to the legal pad. The screen said Brooke.

I picked it up on the first ring. "Brooke."

"Grandma?" Her voice was small and fast, like she had been holding her breath since supper. "Are you okay? I found the listing."
