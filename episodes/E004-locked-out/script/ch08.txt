# One Chance

I stood in my own hallway for twenty-two minutes by the clock over the coat rack, outside my own kitchen door, while my stepdaughter read her father's letter, or read it ten times, and I did not once knock.

The radiator ticked. A snowplow went by on Birchwood Lane and shook the glass in the front door. I had waited outside a great many jury rooms, but I had never before been the person waiting on a verdict of my own.

At last the door opened. Tessa came out with the letter pressed flat against her coat, and her face looked scrubbed raw, like a child's after a long bath. For a moment neither of us said a word.

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out in pieces. "The whole time. He never said one word to me about it after that day. He paid for it, Loretta, and he sat at my table on Sundays and told me to eat."

"I know," I said. "I read the notebook."

"He wrote that he wanted me to be the one to tell you."

"He wrote that too."

She put her free hand over her mouth. I did not hug her. We were not that kind of family, and I did not think she would have forgiven me for it. I did the one useful thing I could think of, which was to put the kettle on.

We sat at the same table where she had been taken apart in front of a stranger an hour earlier. I had turned off the machine, and it was only a table again, with the peppermint wrapper still on it. Tessa wrapped both hands around her cup. I have found over the years that tea does half the work of mediation.

"I am going to tell you what happens next," I said. "I have written it down, because that is what I do. You may say yes, or you may say no, and either answer will go in the record."

I took out the legal pad. I had written it the night before in Winnie's spare room, in my best hand. I read the terms aloud, one by one, as plainly as a verdict.

First, the bank on Water Street would proceed with its fraud review against Lakeshore Bridge Funding. Lakeshore's claim on this house was void, because the house was mine and I had signed nothing, and how they lent without checking who signed was now their problem and the bank's. Second, Tessa would write a full, true statement of what she had done, with dates, signed and witnessed, a statement that protected me and the bank, and she would not leave out the part that shamed her. Third, in return, I would not ask anyone to press charges against her, for as long as she kept every line on the page.

"Fourth," I said. "The shop goes to you, as your father wished. It is in his will, which I have copied for you. Gus has the first right to buy in or manage, and I would like him to manage, because he loves it, and you do not. The shop's earnings and savings go to pay what Ray promised on the debt. Fifth, you will sell the spa building, and close the second wing, and keep Happy Tails only as it was before you got ambitious. You will see a debt advisor at the credit union. I have made the appointment. It is Monday at nine."

"You made the appointment," Tessa said.

"I made the appointment. I am a very efficient woman. Sixth, you will go to the church basement and tell seven ladies, in a clear voice, that I did not work on a dying man. I would like to be present for that, but I will not be, because I was not invited."

Tessa let out a short, damp, astonished sound, close to a laugh with a crack through the middle.

"And seventh," I said. I looked down at the pad. I had written this one last, and crossed it out, and written it again. "We will have supper at this table every second Sunday for one year. I will set the table. You will bring the pie. We will not discuss the past unless it comes up on its own."

She stared at me. "Why? I lied to you. I tried to take your house. I put your things on the porch in the snow, and I let a church full of people think you were some kind of vulture." Her chin shook. "You could have called the sheriff and watched me lose everything. I wouldn't have blamed you."

"I know you would have," I said. "That is rather the point."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Is this for Dad?"

I owed her the truth, because the whole morning was built on it.

"Partly," I said. "He asked me to give you one chance he gave you too late. I am not going to pretend that does not matter to me. But mostly, Tessa, I am tired. I am tired of being the only person in this family who remembers how to be decent, and I would like some company."

She said nothing. Then, in a very small voice, "I called you Dad's wife because if I called you anything else, it meant she was really gone."

"I know that too," I said. "I worked it out about year four. I just never found the right moment to say so. You were always on your way out the door."

She laughed, a wet and shaky laugh, and I let her have it.

I want to be honest. It was not forgiveness, not yet. It was something smaller and sturdier, like a plank laid across a creek, and I did not know whether it would hold both of us. I slid the legal pad across the table with a pen. She read all seven terms with her lips moving, like a girl who was taught to check a key twice before it leaves the shop. Then she signed, and I signed under her, and I wrote the time and the date and the word witnessed.

Tessa put the pen down. She reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out something small and silver, the key to the cheap lock that Gus had carried off in a paper bag. She laid it on the table and looked at it with disgust.

"I won't be needing this," she said.

"No," I agreed. "It was a very poor lock."

She did not smile this time. She was looking at my hand, which had gone, without my deciding it, to the pocket of my cardigan, where the small brass key lay with its string and its paper tag.

"Loretta," she said. "Can I see it? The one he left you."

I took it out and set it on the table between the teacups. She did not pick it up. She only leaned closer, and read the five words in her father's capitals, and her mouth moved with them.

I had not told her about the other key. It was in my handbag, wrapped in a clean handkerchief, on a brass ring with a paper tag that carried a single letter, and the letter was hers.
