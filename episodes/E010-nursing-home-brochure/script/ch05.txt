# What the Form Says

At three in the morning on Wednesday I stood in a hallway holding a ninety-one-year-old man's field book, while the admissions director of Silverleaf Terrace asked me who had authorized a telephone call. I told him the truth. I said, "I did, Mister Pratt, and it was nine-one-one, and it was answered in two rings."

He said nothing more until the paramedics had gone. Then he asked me to come to his office at nine.

At nine, he had a fresh vest, a cup of coffee, and the smile, which had been put back on like a man putting on a hat. "Miss Peggy, I know you meant well," he said. "But we have a policy. We call the administrator first. Residents do not make calls from the nurses' station. It's about safety, and about the family. Mister Abernathy has a niece who will be very upset to get a hospital bill."

"A man was on the floor," I said. "I called the number that sends help."

"And that is lovely. But it's going into the file as a minor slip, in his room. No need to make it bigger than it is."

"Let me read that back," I said. "He fell in the hallway at two forty. I called at two forty-four. The ambulance arrived at two fifty-five. And the file will say he slipped in his room."

"Let's not make this about paperwork," said Gideon, for the second time that week.

I thanked him, and I wrote nine ten in the margin. Then I asked, in the pleasantest voice I own, whether a respite guest might have a copy of her own file, since I was paying for the week and was curious how I'd done. He said of course, and that his records coordinator was out until tomorrow.

Cyril came back from the hospital that afternoon in a taxi van, with a blue cast from his knuckles to his elbow. It was a cracked wrist and nothing worse, which the emergency doctor had called a lucky break, though Cyril disagreed with the word. He sat down at our table and began writing in his field book with his right hand, in the same small square letters. I asked what he was putting down. "Time of return," he said. "Four ten. And that nobody asked how I felt, but you."

That evening I went to the supply room off the Birch Court hallway for an extra towel, and found Imelda there, sitting on an upturned bucket with her shoe off, rubbing her knee. She had come in early to cover.

"You're the one who called," she said.

"I am."

"I should've called myself." She looked at her hands. "I'm not allowed. I have to page him, and he has to say yes. I was paging him when you picked up the phone."

I sat on a stack of boxed adult briefs, which is the least dignified seat I have ever taken, and waited. You do not fill a silence on the radio, and eventually the caller fills it.

"He needs seven more beds by the end of December," Imelda said. "Birch Court has nineteen, now twenty with you, thirty beds, and the man who owns the building loaned money against it, and the lender wants twenty-six full. Gideon gets a bonus on every move-in. And he does the test himself. He says he's certified." She pressed her lips together. "A retired teacher came in a few weeks ago, walking and doing the crossword in pen. By the second week she didn't know what day it was. Her form said nineteen. They all say nineteen, Miss Peg. Or eighteen. Or twenty. Never twenty-six, never twenty-nine. Nineteen is the one that gets you in the door."

"Does anyone sign these?"

"Somebody always signs." She put on her shoe. "I have eleven years here and a knee that's done. I'm going to pretend I didn't say anything."

"I won't hold you to it," I said, "and I won't forget it."

On Thursday morning the records coordinator was back, a pale young woman who looked at me as if I'd asked for a kidney. She printed my file and handed it over in a manila folder. I read it in the vinyl chair with my glasses down where they belong.

Cognitive screening, October eleventh. Total score, nineteen out of thirty. Word recall, zero of five. Clock drawing, unable to complete. Orientation, impaired. Summary, moderate concern, recommend secured memory care. At the bottom was a rubber stamp, Reviewed and Approved, with the name of the director of nursing, and a date and time written in the blue ink of a pen, October eleventh, ten twenty in the morning.

I took out the green notebook. My own margin said that Gideon and I started at ten twelve, and I had looked at the wall clock at ten thirty-one, and I had said five words back to him that I can still say now. Lantern, spoon, river, saddle, ash. I had also drawn the clock again that afternoon in my notebook, from memory, with the hour hand shorter than the minute hand.

Somebody had stamped my approval at ten twenty, eleven minutes before my test was over.

Then I turned to a page from Monday, where I had copied from the whiteboard at the nurses' station, because I always write down who is absent, a line in green marker. Director of Nursing, away, care conference, Denver, October ninth through twelfth.

I felt very calm. It arrives when you stop being surprised and begin to take down the details.

Dana came at half past three, in her good wool coat, carrying a blue folder against her chest like a hymnal. She looked around my small room and said it was cozy. Then she set the folder on my bed and told me it held two things, a residency agreement for Birch Court and a financial power of attorney. "Just for convenience, Mom. So that I can deal with the billing and you don't have to worry about a thing."

"I'll read it," I said.

"Gideon thinks we should sit down together next Thursday morning at ten. A family care conference, to finalize placement. Doesn't that sound easy?"

I said it sounded very organized, and she squeezed my hand and left. I put the folder in the drawer, and I stood at the window, which I had cracked open an inch because the radiator in that room ran like a bakery. Below me in the parking lot, Dana walked to her car with her telephone against her ear. Her voice came up very clearly in the cold air.

"No, it's fine. She's fine. She'll sign Thursday. Seven-five, Gideon says, and my husband says the bank wants it by the thirtieth, so I need it by Friday. I know. I'm doing everything I can."

When I looked down at the notebook in my hand, I had already written the time in the margin, and underneath it I wrote, Thursday, three fifty-two in the afternoon. Dana. Seven-five. The thirtieth.
