# The Stove

By the last week of October, I had a real estate listing for my own house hidden in my recipe box, filed under S for spring, between sour cherry and sweet potato. I also had a checkup with my doctor that I had booked back in the summer, long before any of this started.

Tommy offered to drive me, the way he always does, but he had a transmission on the lift that morning. I told him I had been driving myself places since before he was born, and he made me promise to call him when I got home.

My doctor's office is in a low brick building on the edge of town, with a fish tank in the waiting room and magazines from last spring. She has been my doctor for twenty years. When she started, she looked so young I worried she had wandered into the wrong room, and every Christmas since, I have brought her an apple pie with the cheddar crust.

She took my blood pressure and listened to my heart and told me both were better than hers, which she says every year. I was sitting on that long strip of white paper they roll over the exam table, and every time I shifted, it crackled like a candy wrapper. Then she sat down on her little rolling stool and folded her hands, and I knew something was coming, because in twenty years she had never once folded her hands at me.

"Diane, I need to ask you a couple of questions," my doctor said. "I want you to know I'm asking because I have to."

"Ask away," I said.

"Have you had any trouble with the stove?" she asked. "Leaving a burner on, maybe, and forgetting about it?"

I laughed, because I thought she was joking. Then I saw that she wasn't.

"And have you gotten lost at all?" she asked. "Driving to church, for instance?"

"Saint Anne's is four blocks from my front door," I said. "It's one left turn. I could get there with my eyes closed, though I'd rather not try." I looked at her. "Who told you that?"

She hesitated. "A family member called the office with some concerns."

"Which family member?" I asked.

She studied me for a long moment, and then she turned her computer screen around so I could see it. "It's your chart, Diane," she said. "You have every right to read it."

It was a phone message, typed up by someone at the front desk and dated the morning after my tour of Larkspur Commons. It said that the patient's daughter-in-law, Brittany Whitaker, had called with concerns. It said the patient had been leaving the stove on. It said the patient had gotten lost driving to church. And at the bottom, it said, "Family is worried about patient living alone."

I want to tell you how that felt, because I think some of you will know. For thirty-one years I ran a diner kitchen with six burners going at once, eggs on two of them, gravy on a third, soup on a fourth, and a line of hungry men out the door. In all that time I never once left a burner on, because a diner that burns down does not open again on Monday. And there I sat on that crackling paper in my stocking feet, which did not even reach the floor, like a little girl waiting for a shot. For the first time in my whole life, I felt small.

My doctor put her hand on my arm. "I don't believe a word of it, for what it's worth," she said. "But when someone calls, I have to ask."

I sat there for a minute and let myself feel it. Then I sat up as straight as that table would let me.

"I want a full cognitive evaluation," I said. "The longest one you have, on paper, with your signature at the bottom."

"Diane, you don't need one," my doctor said.

"I know I don't need one," I said. "I want it anyway. And I'd like a printed copy of that phone message, please."

She smiled then, the first real smile since she had folded her hands. She printed the message, signed the bottom, and put me on her schedule for the first week of November. I folded that page into my purse next to my checkbook.

I did not drive straight home. I drove down Main Street and parked across from the old Blue Kettle building, the way I sometimes do when I need to think. It is still red brick, with the same big front window where we used to tape up a card with the pie of the day. There is a chalkboard easel on the sidewalk now, and through the glass I could see a young man in a knit cap working a silver espresso machine right where Walter's grill used to be.

I could see Walter there anyway. He wore a white apron and a paper hat, and he kept a pencil behind his ear and a spiral notebook in his apron pocket. Every night after we closed, he sat at the end of the counter and wrote down the day: how many eggs, how many pies, what the bread man charged, and anything anybody had said that he might need later.

One year our egg supplier billed us twice for the same delivery and swore up and down that he hadn't. Walter did not argue with him. He went and got his notebook, turned to the right page, and read the man the date, the count, and the time his truck had pulled in. We got a free case of eggs every Christmas for ten years after that.

"Memory gets tired, Diane," Walter used to say. "Paper doesn't."

Sitting there across from his old grill, I understood that Brittany had already put her version of me on paper, in a doctor's office, where it would sit and wait. Well, two can keep a notebook. On the way home I stopped at the drugstore and bought a spiral notebook with a green cover, the kind Walter always bought. In the parking lot, with my reading glasses on, I wrote down everything since the fourteenth of September: the spreadsheet, the dots, the ring, the man with the laser tape, the sixty thousand dollars, the listing, and the phone call. It filled four pages. My handwriting was very neat. I was very angry.

When I pulled into my driveway, the little door on my mailbox was hanging open, which it never does. Inside, on top of the electric bill, was a long white envelope, and stuck to it was a note on the back of a grocery receipt from my neighbor across the street.

"I signed for this so you wouldn't have to drive to the post office," she had written. "Hope that's all right."

The envelope had come certified. The return address was a law firm in Columbus, with three last names printed across the corner, and not one of those names was Greene.

I had spent the morning being accused of leaving the stove on. Standing at the end of my driveway with that envelope in my hand, I could finally smell the smoke.
