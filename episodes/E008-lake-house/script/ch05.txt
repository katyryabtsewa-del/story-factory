# Nan Ouellette Answers on the First Ring

Nan picked up before the first ring had finished.

"Nan Ouellette, realtor and notary, what's wrong?"

"Nan, it's Dot Ellery."

"I know who it is, Dot. I have a caller list." There was a clatter behind her, a dog barking, and then a door shut. "Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Is the house on fire?"

"Not yet."

"Well, that's a relief, because I'm at my sister's and she has just cut the pie. Tell me."

So I told her everything, from the pool to the quilt to the room in January. Nan didn't interrupt. When I got to the room, she said one word I had never heard from her before, and I will not repeat it in polite company.

"Dot," she said. "Did he ask you?"

"No."

"Did he ask you one single thing?"

"No, Nan. I want to sell the house. Tonight, if the law allows it. And I want to talk about the farmhouse on Mill Street."

There was a pause long enough for me to hear her put down a fork. "Give me twenty minutes," she said. "I'm a Catholic, not a monk. Holiday weekends are for closings."

She came up the drive at twenty-five past nine in a white pickup with a plow blade on the front. Nan is fifty-two years old, five foot two in her boots, and she wore a plaid wool coat over sweatpants printed with small cartoon turkeys. She had a canvas bag under one arm and her reading glasses pushed up into her red hair, and she came straight into the kitchen like she'd been doing it all her life.

"Coffee," she said, as if it were a legal document.

She sat at Frank's table and laid out a stack of folders and a rubber notary stamp. Then she picked up the tablet and looked at the photographs for a long time. "Primary suite," she said at last. "That's a crime against your quilt."

"It's a crime against the English language."

"It's both." She pushed the tablet away as if it were warm, opened the top folder, and ran a finger down the first page of the deed. "One name. Dorothy Ellery, sole owner. Frank did that on purpose, and it's the smartest thing a man ever did for his wife in this county. And here is his clause." She read it out. "If the grantee elects to sell, the north adjoining owner, Amos Thibodeau, shall have first refusal at the price the grantee names."

"At the price I name."

"That's what it says." She sipped her coffee. "Craig can't sell this house, Dot. He's not on the deed, he's not on the tax bill, and he has no power of attorney. He's a man with a tablet and a feeling. The only question is whether you want to sell, and I have to ask it properly. If a lawyer ever reads what he wrote to Pinecrest, he'll say a woman who needs someone to look in on her cannot sell her house at ten o'clock at night."

I took the folded letter out of my pocket and laid it on the table. "My doctor gave me a cognitive screening in September. I score perfectly. I keep the letter in the tackle box."

Nan read it and laughed out loud. "A pleasure to examine. I'm framing this. I have my own copy, you know, because you made me drive you." She tapped the folder. "You called me. I didn't call you. A credit union manager will witness in the morning, and I'm writing down the time you called and the time I arrived. Nobody will ever say you were pushed."

"What is it worth?"

"The assessor has it at six hundred and forty thousand."

"Then I'd like six hundred and fifty thousand," I said. "In cash."

"I want to ask for the roof, Nan. Frank put that roof on himself. It's a good roof."

She laughed. "Oh, Dot. Okay. Six hundred and fifty thousand. I'll write it up tonight. And Mill Street?"

"Two hundred and ten thousand, in cash, from Frank's life insurance, which I have never touched."

"The heirs are asking two thirty and have had no offers since August. I'll call them in the morning." "If they say yes, the purchase closes in two weeks, but I can get you into the house tomorrow on a signed occupancy agreement."

"You can do that?"

"I can do almost anything with a notary stamp." She grinned. "Now call Amos."

It was ten o'clock. I dialed Amos. He answered on the sixth ring.

"Ellery."

"Amos, it's Dot. I need you to come over. Now."

There was a pause. "Be right there."

It took him twelve minutes. I watched his flashlight come down the shore path through the falling snow. He came in stamping, in long johns under his barn coat, with the hat tied under his chin. He sat down across from me and read the clause twice, with his finger.

"Six hundred and fifty thousand," he said. "Cash."

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, then took a cell phone out of his coat pocket and set it on the table. He tapped one button and put it on speaker. It rang four times. "Dad?" said a man's voice, thick with sleep. "It's ten o'clock."

"Leo," Amos said. "Dot Ellery is selling the lake house."

A long silence. "Is that right."

"It is. She'll take six hundred and fifty thousand."

"We can do that," Leo Gagnon said. "The loan's been approved since October, Dad. Jenna and I have been saving for that cove for six years, and we were never going to ask Frank, and we were never going to ask Dot. Tell her yes."

Amos did not look at me. He looked at the table. "I sold the boat shop two years ago," he said, in a low voice. "I put every dollar of it away for this and I never told her. I didn't think a man was allowed to want somebody's water."

My eyes did something unprofessional, and I let them.

"I'll have the bank manager open early," Nan said briskly, and I understood she was giving us a minute. "Leo, a hundred thousand in a certified check, to be cleared by eight. The credit union manager owes me a favor. The loan and the rest go through the title company Tuesday. I ran the title search while Dot was talking, and it's clean. I'll walk the deed to the registry Monday morning. But it is sold the moment you sign, Dot. The rest is paper."

"Yes, ma'am," said the phone.

Amos tugged down his earflaps and walked back up the shore path. Nan drove off, promising to be back at ten minutes to six with a thermos, and I stood in the finally quiet kitchen.

At midnight my phone rang on the table, next to the legal pad. The screen said Brooke.

I picked it up on the first ring. "Brooke."

"Grandma?" Her voice was small and fast, like she had been holding her breath since supper. "Are you okay? I found the listing."
