# The Kitchen Table Closing

Nan Ouellette came in at ten minutes to six on Friday morning with a thermos under her arm and snow in her hair, and I showed her my phone before she had her coat off. "Craig is awake," I said.

She read the text and whistled. "Early, you said?"

"He has never been early in his life."

"Then he's nervous." She laid out the papers in an order I could follow: the purchase agreement, the deed, the disclosure form, and a one-page acknowledgment of the first refusal clause for Amos, which he would sign to say he had been offered the house and had taken it. Then she pulled a sheet from the bottom of the stack, headed Reserved Property.

It listed the red canoe. It listed three notebooks, marked dock and tide. It listed one quilt, hand sewn, and six sacks of boots. At the bottom, in Nan's slanting hand, it said, Wood stove, cast iron, remains with the house and is included in the sale.

"You're sure about the stove?" Nan asked.

"It's a family's winter. It stays."

"Then everything that is not nailed down is yours, Dot. The stove, I'm told, is nailed down."

The phone buzzed again at seven forty. Craig had written, Leaving early! Be there by ten. Put the kettle on :)

My son had sent a face made of punctuation to a woman he planned to move to Portland. Then I did the hardest piece of acting of my career, which I had learned from thirty-three years of telling parents the nosebleed was nothing. I typed, Drive safe. See you then, I'll have coffee. I sent it before my hand could object.

"Ten o'clock," Nan said, looking at the clock over the sink. "Two hours and twenty minutes. Let's not chat."

My phone buzzed a third time, at seven fifty-two. Brooke. He's in the shower. I could try to slow things down? I stared at it. I typed, Do not. Be a good passenger. I love you. She wrote back a single heart.

At eight, Leo Gagnon came up the drive in a blue pickup with a tall woman in a quilted coat, the credit union manager, who carried a bank envelope held flat against her chest. Leo is a broad, quiet man in his forties. He shook my hand at the door and said, "Missus Ellery," and I said, "Dot, please, Leo. I pulled a splinter out of your thumb when you were ten."

"I remember," he said, embarrassed all over again about the thumb.

The manager laid the certified check on the table. It was for one hundred thousand dollars, on heavy paper, and she said it had cleared at seven forty. I read the number out loud, the way I used to read a thermometer. "The balance goes through the title company Tuesday," Nan said, "and the bank has Leo's loan papers. It's sold the moment you sign, Dot. The rest is paper."

Amos came in behind them, stamping snow off his boots with the plaid hat still on. He took a fountain pen from his shirt pocket, the kind he uses for the ledger on his boat, and laid one hand flat on the table before he sat down. Then he signed the acknowledgment in big, slow letters, the way he marks his lobster tags. It took him a long time, and nobody hurried him.

I signed where Nan put the little yellow flags. I initialed the boot list, which I was pleased to do. I signed so many times that I lost count, and Nan pressed her stamp onto each page with a soft, flat sound, like a small door closing somewhere in the house. The manager signed as witness, and the time was written in the margin. It said eight forty-eight.

"Done," said Nan. "Dorothy Ellery, you are no longer the owner of a lake house on Wassumkeag Cove. Amos Thibodeau and Leo Gagnon are."

Leo unclenched his jaw. Amos said, "Well," and put his hat a little straighter.

Nan's phone rang. She listened, said "Wonderful," and hung up. "The heirs said yes to Mill Street. Here." She put a brass key in my palm, on a loop of red yarn. "It's yours to live in as of this morning, and the purchase closes in two weeks."

I held it a moment. It weighed more than the stove.

The moving truck arrived at nine, driven by Leo's son Kyle, who is nineteen and has his grandfather's shoulders. He backed it down the gravel with his arm out the window. Then he looked at the canoe. "Missus Ellery, this thing weighs as much as a Labrador." I told him it had opinions.

Kyle and his father took the bed, the dresser, Frank's chair, the cedar chest and the six boxes of clothes, and strapped the canoe over the cab with the quilt wrapped inside a sheet on the seat beside the driver. Nobody touched the stove. I stood at it for a minute while they carried things past me. It was cold, with a little white ash in the grate, and I rested my hand on the iron the way you rest it on a sleeping child. Nothing in me broke. I let the house be Frank's and mine for the last time.

At nine forty, Jenna pulled in behind the truck in an old minivan with her children, a boy of five named Mason with a cowlick and a girl of three holding a plastic flashlight, and a brown puppy named Biscuit, who went straight for the dock. Jenna stayed on the porch and called after them to keep to the shore end. The children ran down the planks shrieking, and the heron, which I had not seen since dawn, lifted off the far piling and went away low over the ice without any hurry at all.

At the kitchen table I took out two things. The first was an index card, which I propped against Frank's brass compass on the mantel.

Frank Ellery built this house in nineteen eighty-nine, and it was loved here every single day. Please keep loving it on purpose. The stove is yours, and so is the roof.

The second was an envelope with Craig written on the front, which I left on the kitchen table where he could not miss it. It was short.

At ten fifteen, I handed Kyle and his father turkey sandwiches wrapped in foil, because a twenty-two-pound bird does not go to waste on my watch, and the truck went down the drive with Leo's pickup behind it. Across the cove, Amos raised one hand off his bucket, and I raised mine back. My phone buzzed. Brooke. Dad stopped for Dana's latte. I didn't do anything, I swear. I wrote, Good.

At ten thirty-five I was sitting on the bench at the head of my dock, facing the house, with Jenna on the porch and the children throwing sticks at the shallows. A silver crossover came around the bend in the gravel. I could see my son's face through the windshield before he could see mine.
