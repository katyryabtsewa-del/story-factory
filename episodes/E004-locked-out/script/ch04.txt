# Box 417

On Wednesday, as soon as the mail had come to Winnie's porch, with a lender's business card in one coat pocket and a dead man's key in the other, I went to find out what door a number like four hundred seventeen could open.

Gus had answered half the question on Tuesday night. I had brought him the key at the shop, where the cat was asleep on top of the old safe, as it has been every day for eleven years, and he turned it under the work lamp and actually smiled. It was a safe deposit box key, he said, the flat double-bitted kind, and the style was the one Merrow Valley Bank on Water Street used. Ray had serviced the locks on their boxes for thirty years. He would have known that vault the way a farmer knows a barn.

The Water Street branch of Merrow Valley Bank has a marble floor that rings under your shoes and a ceiling nobody has dusted since the Eisenhower administration. Winnie drove me, parked across from the front door with the engine running, and informed me that she would sit with her thermos and a crossword until I came out, or until the police needed to be called, whichever happened first.

The branch manager was a neat, upright woman of about fifty-five with silver-rimmed glasses and a gray suit so well pressed it looked ironed on. Her nameplate said Cecilia Rowe. She had the stillness of a person who has seen every kind of family come through her door and been astonished by none of them. I put the key on her desk, with the tag turned up, and said, "I believe this belongs to a box in your vault. My husband was Raymond Kessler. He died eleven days before he was buried, and I am his widow."

She did not say sorry for your loss in the hurried way people do. She said, "I knew Ray, Missus Kessler. He kept my vault door from sticking for thirty years. I was sorry to hear it," and she meant it, and I was grateful. Then she turned to her computer, typed, and went still for a moment.

"Box four hundred seventeen," she said. "Rented three years ago this month. It's in two names, Raymond Kessler and Loretta Kessler." She looked up. "You are a co-renter, Missus Kessler. Do you remember signing the card?"

I opened my mouth to say no, and then I stopped, because something came back to me from a very long way off. It was a Wednesday, our old habit, when Ray would drive to the courthouse at noon and we would eat sandwiches on the steps, in all weather, as we had when we were courting. Three years ago in January, he had slid a clipboard across my knees and said it was a card for the shop insurance, the agent needed a second signature, and would I mind. I had a ham sandwich in one hand. I signed without reading, and he thanked me with a kiss on the cheek.

"I signed it on the courthouse steps," I said. "He told me it was for the shop insurance."

Cecilia took off her glasses, and her face changed. "Then he took good care of you," she said, "though I would not say he was honest about it." She slid a plastic card across the desk. It was a copy of my own signature, three years younger, and above it, in black ink with the loops of every letter leaning forward the way Ray's always did, was his.

I noticed the black ink. I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn before you can name it, and I filed it away.

The rules were simple, and Cecilia recited them like a woman who enjoyed rules. A box needs two keys, she said, the renter's key, which I held, and the bank's guard key, and neither works alone. Because I was a living co-renter, I needed only my identification and a certified copy of the death certificate, which had come in that morning's mail from the funeral home, in an envelope with a raised seal, and was riding in my purse among a great many other papers nobody wants to read. She stamped her form, I signed her book in my best court hand, and she picked up her own ring of keys.

"I will take you back," she said. "I would ask that you not remove anything today that you cannot carry. If anyone else asks about this box, the bank will not discuss it, with anyone, without your written permission. That includes family."

"Especially family," I said.

"Especially," she said, and she did not smile, but I believe she wanted to.

The vault was behind a round steel door as thick as a pie safe, and the room beyond it was long and narrow and smelled of cold metal and old paper. Hundreds of little steel doors lined both walls, each with two keyholes. Cecilia found four hundred seventeen near the bottom of the left wall, turned her key in one lock, and stepped back, and I put Ray's brass key in the other. It turned as if it had been oiled the day before, and perhaps it had. She slid out a long gray metal box and carried it into a small private room with a table, a lamp, and a box of tissues, and she set it down very gently.

"I will be at my desk," Cecilia said. "Take whatever time you need. There is no clock in here."

She closed the door. I sat with my hands flat on the lid for what I would guess was a full minute. I am sixty-eight years old, and I have heard men confess to terrible things in a courtroom without my pulse changing, but my pulse changed.

I opened the lid.

Because I cannot help myself, I took an inventory, out loud, in a whisper, as I would have at the end of a long trial. A cloth-bound notebook, dark green, the size of a hymnal. A thick folder of photocopies, with a paper clip. A single sheet in a plastic sleeve, in Ray's hand. A long white envelope containing what looked like a will. A brass key ring with a single house key on it, never used and still bright, and a paper tag bearing one letter, T. And at the bottom, side by side, two cream colored envelopes, sealed, in his square capital letters. One said, For Loretta. The other said, For Tessa.

I touched mine, and then I laid both envelopes on the table, face down.

I did not read my letter. I was not ready to hear him say whatever he had to say, not in a bank, not under a lamp. So I took the thing I could bear to hold, which was the notebook. It was soft with handling. It had been carried in a coat and a truck and a shop apron. It opened on its own where he had folded back the cover, to the first page, and there was the date, in Ray's black ink, and under it the shortest first entry I have ever read.

November third. Opened the envelope meant for the shop. It was not for the shop.
